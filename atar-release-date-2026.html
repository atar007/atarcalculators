ATAR release date 2026

After a long year, the wait for your ATAR is its own kind of stress. Here is when to expect your 2026 results, how they arrive, and what happens next.

ATAR results for 2026 are released between Thursday 10 December and Sunday 20 December 2026, depending on your state. Victoria is first on 10 December, South Australia and the NT are expected on 14 December, NSW, the ACT and Tasmania follow on 16 December, Queensland on 18 December and Western Australia on 20 December. Your Year 12 subject results arrive on the same day or shortly before, and the first ATAR-based university offers start arriving on 23 December in most states.

Reading the table

Three columns matter, and they are easy to confuse. Subject results are your marks, study scores or grades from your state’s curriculum authority: NESA in NSW, the VCAA in Victoria, the QCAA in Queensland, the SACE Board in South Australia and the Northern Territory, SCSA in Western Australia, TASC in Tasmania and the BSSS in the ACT. ATAR is your rank, calculated by the tertiary admissions centre. First ATAR-based offers is the first offer round in which universities can use your ATAR to make you an offer.

In some states, the subject results and the ATAR arrive together. In Victoria, the VCE Results and ATAR Service delivers both at once. In others, they arrive separately. In NSW, HSC results arrive at 6am and the ATAR at 9am the same morning. In Queensland, there are two days between QCE results on Wednesday 16 December and the ATAR on Friday 18 December.

The order of release in 2026

If you are following results across the country, for example because you have friends or family in other states, the 2026 order is:

  1. Thursday 10 December: Victoria (VCE results and ATARs together).
  2. Monday 14 December: South Australia and the Northern Territory (SACE and NTCET results, with the ATAR on the Tertiary Entrance Statement), as expected by SA schools and universities.
  3. Wednesday 16 December: NSW (HSC results at 6am, ATARs at 9am), the ACT (ATARs through UAC), Tasmania (TASC results) and Queensland’s QCE subject results.
  4. Friday 18 December: Queensland ATARs from 9am.
  5. Sunday 20 December: Western Australia ATARs through TISC.

So in 2026, the ATAR release window stretches from Thursday 10 December to Sunday 20 December. That is a slightly wider spread than students in some states expect, and it matters if you are applying interstate, because your ATAR has to reach the other admissions centre in time for its offer rounds.

Which dates are confirmed and which are expected

Most dates in the table come directly from the organisation that issues the result. Victoria’s date comes from VTAC and the VCAA’s results service information. The NSW dates come from NESA and UAC. Queensland’s ATAR date comes from QTAC and the QCE results date from QCAA material. Tasmania’s date comes from TASC. Western Australia’s ATAR date is the one TISC has published, as reported by Curtin University. The South Australian and Northern Territory date is the one South Australian schools and universities are listing for 2026; the SACE Board and SATAC confirm it closer to the day.

Every organisation reserves the right to change its dates. Changes are rare, but they do happen, so treat the table as your plan and the admissions centre’s website as the final word. A good habit is to check your centre’s key dates page once at the start of December and again a few days before your release date.

When do ATAR results come out in 2026?

Across the country, ATAR results land in mid-to-late December. For 2026, the dates run from Thursday 10 December in Victoria to Sunday 20 December in Western Australia. The pattern is very consistent from year to year.

Each state’s admissions centre sets and publishes the exact date and time, usually months in advance. All the 2026 dates are in the table above, but dates can still change, so check your admissions centre’s website in the week before results.

Why results always arrive in December

The timing of ATAR release is driven by the exam calendar. In each state, final Year 12 exams run from October into November. NSW’s 2026 HSC written exams began on 13 October and finished no later than 5 November. Queensland’s external assessments finished in mid-November, and Western Australia’s written ATAR exams ran from late October to 19 November. After the last exam, markers need several weeks to mark an enormous volume of exam responses, the curriculum authorities need to check and finalise every result, and the admissions centres need the final results before they can scale them and calculate ATARs.

That sequence takes most of November and the first half of December. It is why no state can release ATARs much earlier than it does, and why the dates fall in a narrow window just before Christmas. It is also why the dates are so similar from one year to the next: the exam calendar and the marking process are themselves very stable.

Why the admissions centre and the school authority release separately

Your subject results and your ATAR come from two different organisations because they measure two different things. The curriculum authority certifies what you achieved in each subject: your HSC marks, VCE study scores, QCE results, SACE grades, WACE results or TCE awards. The admissions centre then uses those results, scales them so different subjects can be compared, and calculates your ATAR for university selection.

The admissions centre cannot start until it has the final subject results. In most states the handover happens in the days before release, and the ATAR follows on the same day or soon after. Queensland’s two-day gap between QCE results and ATARs reflects that handover: QTAC receives the final results from the QCAA, runs the inter-subject scaling, calculates ATARs and checks them before release.

A nationally consistent window

ATAR release dates used to vary more between states. In 2020, education ministers agreed that ATARs would be released within a nationally consistent window of dates. That agreement does not mean every state releases on the same day; it means the releases sit close together in mid-to-late December, so interstate applicants are not left waiting weeks longer than local students. The 2026 dates, from 10 to 20 December, sit inside that pattern.

Why some releases happen on weekends

Most releases fall on weekdays, but not all. Western Australia’s 2026 ATAR release is on Sunday 20 December, and in 2025 SCSA released WACE results on a Sunday as well. Weekend releases are not a sign that anything is wrong; they reflect the time each organisation needs after exams finish and its own timetable for checking results. If your release falls on a weekend, expect admissions centres and universities to run extra support, such as phone lines and advice sessions, around it.

Why this matters for interstate applicants

Time zones become important when a deadline in one state falls close to a release in another. For example, UAC’s deadline to change preferences for its December Round 2 is 11.59pm Sydney time on Friday 18 December. A Queensland student applying to NSW universities receives their ATAR at 9am Brisbane time that same day, which gives them the rest of the day to review their UAC preferences. A Western Australian student applying to NSW receives their ATAR two days after that deadline, so their ATAR-based UAC offers usually come in the January round instead.

The same logic applies everywhere. Before results day, write down every deadline that applies to you in your own local time. It takes five minutes and removes a common source of last-minute panic.

Darwin’s results arrive an hour earlier on the clock

Northern Territory students receive their results through the same South Australian systems, but the Northern Territory does not use daylight saving. If the SACE results release is at 8.30am Adelaide time, it appears at 7.30am in Darwin. That one-hour difference regularly confuses families in the Territory, so it is worth setting your alarm for local time, not Adelaide time.

What happens between your last exam and results day

The weeks between your final exam and results day can feel like nothing is happening. In fact, this is when the most complex part of the process takes place. Understanding it can make the wait feel less mysterious, and it explains why results cannot simply be released as soon as exams finish.

Stage 1: marking

Once exams finish, thousands of trained markers work through students’ responses. Written responses are usually marked by more than one marker or checked against marking guidelines, and senior markers monitor consistency. For subjects with practical or performance components, assessment may have taken place earlier in the year, but those marks still need to be finalised alongside the written exam results. Marking a whole state’s Year 12 exams to a consistent standard takes several weeks.

Stage 2: combining school assessment and exam results

In most states, your final result in each subject combines school-based assessment with external exam results, in proportions that vary by state and subject. In Queensland, for example, the external assessment contributes 25 per cent of the final result in most General subjects and 50 per cent in mathematics and science subjects. Before school marks are combined with exam marks, many systems moderate them, adjusting each school’s marks so they are comparable across schools, often using that school’s exam performance as a reference point.

Stage 3: certification by the curriculum authority

The curriculum authority finalises each student’s subject results, checks eligibility for the certificate, and prepares results documents. This involves extensive checking, because errors at this stage would flow through to every later step. Schools also have deadlines to submit final data: the QCAA, for example, set Thursday 26 November 2026 as the final date for schools to report results for students finishing in 2026.

Stage 4: scaling and ATAR calculation

Once the admissions centre receives the final subject results, it scales them so results from different subjects can be compared fairly, combines each student’s scaled results into an aggregate according to the state’s rules, and converts each aggregate into a percentile rank. QTAC’s description of the last step gives a sense of how fine the process is: if Queensland’s Year 12 population is about 60,000, roughly 30 students are placed in each 0.05 ATAR band, with the top 30 receiving 99.95.

The timetable for this stage can be tight. When QTAC calculated Queensland’s first ATARs in December 2020, it published its plan for the final week: receiving the Year 12 results file from the QCAA on the Monday, carrying out inter-subject scaling on the Tuesday, checking and validating data on the Wednesday, calculating ATARs on the Thursday, loading them into its portal on the Friday and releasing them on the Saturday. Every state’s admissions centre goes through a similar sequence.

Stage 5: checks and release

Before release, admissions centres run checks to make sure ATARs have been calculated correctly and are consistent with students’ results. The admissions centres also coordinate nationally through ACTAC, both on release timing and on the shared principles that make ATARs comparable between states. Only after these checks are results loaded into release systems and scheduled for the published time.

This is why release dates are set well in advance and rarely change. Each stage depends on the one before, and the whole chain has to be complete before a single result can be released.

Making the most of the wait

For many students, the weeks between the last exam and results day are the first real break in years. There is no single right way to use them, but a few things help.

Rest first

Year 12 is demanding, and most students finish exams tired. Taking a week or two to rest, sleep properly and do things you enjoy is not wasted time; it makes the decisions that follow easier.

Then do the useful admin

Once you have recovered, set aside a few hours for the administrative tasks that make results day smoother: checking your logins, updating your contact details, reviewing your preference list, and looking into scholarships and accommodation if you will need them. Doing these in November or early December takes a lot of pressure off the week of results.

Research your options properly

The wait is a good time to look beyond course names. Read course structures, look at the units you would take in first year, and check whether courses have placements, majors or pathways that interest you. Many students change their preference order after this kind of research, and it is much better to do it calmly now than in the two days after results.

Earn, travel or try something new

Many students take up holiday jobs, travel or start learning to drive. If you have a job, plan around your results day and change of preference deadline so you are not trying to make important decisions during a shift.

Avoid trying to calculate your exact ATAR every day

It is natural to keep running numbers through a calculator while you wait. A single estimate, or a range, is useful; recalculating every day usually just increases anxiety, because your inputs are still guesses. Run your estimate once, plan around a range, and then let it go until results day.

Why your school does not release your ATAR

Schools see a lot of their students’ results during the year, so it surprises some families that the school is not the source of the ATAR. The reason is that the ATAR is calculated after statewide scaling, using every student’s final results, and only the admissions centre has that complete picture. Your school will usually receive information about its students after release, and many schools publish cohort highlights, but your own ATAR comes to you directly from the admissions centre or the results service, not through your school.

NSW: HSC results and ATARs on Wednesday 16 December

For NSW students, results day 2026 is Wednesday 16 December, and it happens in two steps on the same morning.

Step 1: HSC results from 6am (NESA)

The NSW Education Standards Authority (NESA) releases HSC results online, by email and by SMS from 6am on Wednesday 16 December 2026. You can see them in your NESA Students Online account. Your results include your HSC mark and performance band for each course, and your Record of Achievement lists every HSC course you completed with its result.

If you have questions about your results, NESA runs an HSC results enquiry centre on 1300 13 83 23. On HSC results day, NESA’s phone service starts at 8am; on other weekdays it runs from 9am to 5pm. NESA also offers fee-based results checks, mark reports and copies of your examination responses after results are released.

Step 2: ATARs from 9am (UAC)

UAC releases NSW and ACT ATARs on its website from 9am on the same day. To log in you will need your Year 12 student number and your UAC PIN, so make sure you know where both are before the morning. UAC runs an ATAR Enquiry Centre after release for questions about the ATAR itself.

The three hours between 6am and 9am are worth thinking about in advance. Some students like to look at their HSC results first and work out roughly where their ATAR might land; others prefer to wait and see both together. Either approach is fine, but decide beforehand so the morning has a plan. Our HSC to ATAR conversion guide and HSC ATAR calculator can help you interpret your marks in that window.

NSW results day: quick answers

Can I see my HSC results before 6am? No. NESA releases HSC results from 6am on Wednesday 16 December 2026, online and by email and SMS. Nothing is available earlier, so there is no advantage in staying up through the night.

Will my ATAR arrive by text message? Your HSC results come from NESA by email and SMS, but your ATAR is released on UAC’s website from 9am. Log in with your Year 12 student number and UAC PIN to see it. If you are unsure of your PIN, sort it out with UAC well before results day.

Can I change my UAC preferences as often as I like? Yes. UAC allows undergraduate applicants to change their preferences as many times as they like, free of charge, up to each round’s deadline. The one restriction is that you cannot change preferences between a round’s deadline and the release of its offers, so for December Round 2 there is a freeze from 11.59pm on Friday 18 December until offers are released at 7.30am on Wednesday 23 December.

I am applying for medicine. Is my timeline different? Often, yes. UAC notes that some medical degrees do not make offers in December Round 2 and wait until January, and medicine, dentistry and veterinary science have their own key dates and assessment processes, including interviews for some courses. Check UAC’s medicine, dentistry and vet key dates page and each university’s requirements. Our ATAR for medicine guide covers typical requirements.

What if my HSC mark looks wrong? Start by checking which courses count towards your ATAR, since only your best 2 units of English and best 8 other units are used. If you still believe there is an error in an HSC mark, NESA offers fee-based results checks after release. Questions about the ATAR calculation go to UAC.

Does a strong HSC band guarantee a high ATAR? Not on its own. Your ATAR depends on scaled marks across ten units, and scaling varies by course. A set of strong bands usually goes with a strong ATAR, but the exact number depends on which courses you took. Our HSC scaling explainer shows why.

ACT: ATARs through UAC on Wednesday 16 December

ACT students receive their ATARs on the same day as NSW students, because UAC calculates and releases ATARs for both. In 2026, ACT ATARs are available on UAC’s website from 9am Wednesday 16 December.

Your BSSS certificate and Tertiary Entrance Statement

The ACT Board of Senior Secondary Studies (BSSS) issues the ACT Senior Secondary Certificate to students who meet its requirements, along with a Tertiary Entrance Statement, which includes the ATAR, for students who chose the tertiary pathway. UAC’s key dates list these statements as being issued by the BSSS in mid-December 2026. In 2025, 5,235 students achieved the ACT Senior Secondary Certificate, and about 61 per cent of them also received a Tertiary Entrance Statement.

Same deadlines as NSW

Because ACT students apply through UAC, they follow the same offer rounds and deadlines as NSW students, including the 11.59pm Friday 18 December deadline to change preferences for December Round 2 and the 7.30am Wednesday 23 December release of the first ATAR-based offers. Canberra is on the same time zone as Sydney in December, so there is no time difference to worry about. For more on the ACT system, see our guides to the ACT ATAR release date, how the ACT ATAR is calculated and UAC in the ACT.

ACT results day: quick answers

Do ACT students use NESA? No. The ACT has its own senior system run by the BSSS, which issues the ACT Senior Secondary Certificate and Tertiary Entrance Statement. ACT students share UAC with NSW students for ATARs and university applications.

Is the ACT ATAR the same as a NSW ATAR? They are calculated from different certificates, but UAC aligns them so they are directly comparable, and ACT students compete in the same UAC offer rounds as NSW students.

When do ACT students change preferences? On the same timetable as NSW: by 11.59pm on Friday 18 December for December Round 2, with offers at 7.30am on Wednesday 23 December 2026.

Victoria: VCE results and ATARs on Thursday 10 December

Victorian students are the first in the country to receive their 2026 results. VCE results and ATARs are released together from 7am Thursday 10 December 2026 through the VCE Results and ATAR Service.

Three ways to see your results

VTAC lists three ways to access your VCE results and ATAR once they are released:

  • The VCE Results and ATAR Service website, which you need to register for in advance at resultsandatar.vic.edu.au.
  • The Results and ATAR app for iPhone and Android, which you log in to with your VCAA student number and results service password. The app is available before results day, with results appearing from 7am on 10 December.
  • Your VTAC account, if you have applied through VTAC.

The most common results-day problem in Victoria is not having registered for the results service. VTAC’s own Year 12 materials remind students to register in November, so if you have not done it yet, do it now rather than on the morning.

Digital ATAR statements and the PRAS

Paid VTAC applicants who are eligible for a 2026 ATAR will receive an email from My eQuals, a digital credentials platform, with instructions for setting up access to their official digital ATAR statement. VTAC says these registration emails will be sent from 15 December 2026, and from 21 December for students eligible for a Notional ATAR. Students who are not VTAC applicants can order a paid printed ATAR statement on VTAC’s website after results are released.

Immediately after results are released, the Post Results and ATAR Service (PRAS) is the main point of contact for questions, on 1800 653 080. Its opening hours are published on the PRAS website.

Victorian results day: quick answers

Do I need to be a VTAC applicant to see my ATAR? No. Eligible VCE students can see their ATAR through the VCE Results and ATAR Service and app. Being a paid VTAC applicant means you can also use your VTAC account and receive a digital ATAR statement through My eQuals; other students can order a printed ATAR statement from VTAC after release.

I missed the 12 December deadline. What now? You can still change your preferences for January round 1. After December offers are released on Wednesday 23 December, the change of preference window opens again until 12pm on Thursday 24 December. When do applications close for later rounds? VTAC lists 13 January 2027 as the closing date for applications for January round 2 and subsequent rounds. If you have not applied through VTAC at all, check this date carefully.

Who do I call with questions on results day? The Post Results and ATAR Service (PRAS) on 1800 653 080 is the main contact immediately after release. For questions about specific courses, contact the institution directly; many run change of preference information sessions in the days after results.

Queensland: QCE results on 16 December, ATARs on 18 December

Queensland is the one state where subject results and ATARs come out two days apart in 2026.

QCE results on Wednesday 16 December (QCAA)

The QCAA releases Year 12 subject results and Senior Education Profiles in students’ learning accounts, which you access through the Student Portal on the myQCE website. For 2026, Queensland school and QCAA material lists Wednesday 16 December 2026 as the release date. In 2025, results were available from 9am. Your Senior Education Profile includes your Senior Statement and, if you are eligible, your Queensland Certificate of Education.

One change for 2026 graduates is worth knowing: from 2026, the QCAA requires Year 12 graduates to have completed an academic integrity course during senior schooling as part of QCE eligibility. Your school will have handled this, but it is one more reason to check your learning account before results day.

When do university offers follow?

Getting your ATAR is not the same as getting an offer. After results, you finalise your university preferences, and offers are made in rounds.

In 2026, the first ATAR-based round arrives on 23 December in NSW, the ACT, Victoria and Queensland, on 24 December in Western Australia, in mid-December for Tasmanian Year 12s applying to the University of Tasmania, and on 14 January 2027 in South Australia and the NT. Several more rounds follow through January and February, so a course you miss in the first round can still come through later. Deciding your preferences early makes this smoother.

How an offer round actually works

Every admissions centre runs offer rounds in broadly the same way. For each course, applicants who list it are placed in an order of merit, usually by selection rank, which is your ATAR plus any adjustment factors that apply to that course. Places are offered down the list until they are filled. In each round, you receive at most one offer: for the highest course on your preference list for which you are eligible in that round.

That last point is important, and it is widely misunderstood. Where a course sits on your list does not change your chances of getting into it. UAC states plainly that many students believe a course further down the list is harder to get into, and that this is not the case: you receive an offer for the first course on your list for which you are eligible. So your list should be ordered by what you want most, not by what you think you are most likely to get.

Early offers and ATAR-based offers

By results day, many students already hold an early offer. Early offer schemes use school recommendations, Year 11 results, predicted ATARs or other criteria to make offers before final results. NSW’s Schools Recommendation Scheme made its first offers in UAC’s November Round 1 on 12 November 2026, and WA’s TISC made early offers on 5 October and 2 December 2026.

Early offers can be conditional or unconditional. A conditional offer might require you to achieve a minimum ATAR or pass a particular subject. An unconditional offer guarantees a place as long as you complete Year 12 and receive an ATAR. Read your offer carefully: results day is when any conditions are tested.

Having an early offer does not stop you being considered for other courses. If your ATAR is stronger than expected, you may become eligible for a higher preference in an ATAR-based round. UAC’s advice is to accept an early offer you want to keep, because it may expire, and then manage your preference list carefully for later rounds.

Accepting, deferring and responding on time

When you receive an offer, you usually have a limited time to respond. QTAC’s 2026–27 timetable, for example, gives students until Wednesday 30 December to respond to offers made on 23 December. If you miss a response deadline, the offer can lapse and the place may go to someone else.

Accepting an offer does not stop you receiving a higher-preference offer in a later round. VTAC explains that if you receive an offer, you are still automatically considered for higher preferences in later rounds. Many universities also allow deferral, so you can accept now and start a year later. Deferral rules vary by course and institution, so check the offer letter and the university’s website.

Which round is the “main” one?

The answer depends on the state. UAC calls December Round 2 its “Main Offer Round”, and it carries the first ATAR-based offers. VTAC’s December round is limited to current Year 12 students, but VTAC says most places are allocated in January round 1, which every course must take part in. QTAC’s 23 December round is the first major round for Queensland Year 12 students. TISC calls its 24 December round the main round. SATAC’s mid-January round is the first to include Year 12 results.

The practical lesson is the same everywhere: the first ATAR-based round is the one to prepare for, but it is not the last chance. Places continue to be offered in later rounds, sometimes in large numbers, as students decline offers, accept other courses or change their plans.

How many offers are made

The numbers are large. In late January 2025, the admissions centres reported that they had made 271,097 offers for semester 1 2025 courses across all their offer rounds up to and including each centre’s main January round. That included 85,125 offers through UAC, 64,745 through VTAC and 25,065 through the University of Tasmania. Most offers for first semester are made in December and January, with remaining places offered through February.

Those figures include offers to people who are not school leavers and offers for VET courses, and some applicants receive more than one offer across rounds. But they show the scale of the system that swings into action after results day.

How many preferences you can list

The number of courses you can list depends on the admissions centre. UAC allows undergraduate applicants up to five preferences. QTAC allows up to six. TISC applicants can list up to six. VTAC’s current fact sheet allows up to eight. Every centre gives the same advice: use all of your preferences, because each one is another chance of an offer.

How to order your preferences

A good preference list has three layers:

  1. Your dream courses at the top. Put the courses you most want first, even if your ATAR is a little below last year’s selection rank. Selection ranks change each year, adjustment factors may lift you, and there is no penalty for aiming high.
  2. Realistic options in the middle. Courses you want and that your ATAR, plus any adjustments, is likely to reach.
  3. A safe option at the bottom. At least one course you would be happy with and are very likely to receive, such as a related degree, a diploma pathway or the same course at a campus with a lower threshold.

Universities’ own advice repeats this pattern. A common tip is to order your preferences by what you most want to study, not by what you think you will get into. The system already gives you the highest course you are eligible for, so ordering by likelihood only risks missing out on something you would have preferred.

Compare selection ranks, not just ATARs

When you check whether a course is realistic, compare your likely selection rank with the course’s published threshold. Many courses publish the lowest selection rank that received an offer, which includes adjustment factors. If you are eligible for adjustment factors, such as subject bonuses, regional bonuses or educational access schemes, your selection rank for a course may be several points above your ATAR. Our guides to selection rank vs ATAR and the selection rank calculator explain how to estimate this.

What to do if your ATAR is higher than expected

A strong result is great news, but it can also prompt hasty decisions. Before you replace your whole list, ask yourself whether you actually want the more competitive courses you now qualify for, or whether you are adding them because the number allows it. If you do want them, add them above your existing preferences, keeping your current first choice somewhere on the list as a backup.

What to do if your ATAR is lower than expected

Do not remove your dream course immediately. Check whether adjustment factors might lift your selection rank, and look at whether the course has a pathway, such as a diploma, a related degree or a different campus. Then make sure the lower half of your list contains courses you would genuinely accept. Our section on what to do if your ATAR is not what you hoped, below, covers this in more detail.

After you receive an offer

Once you receive and accept an offer, think about how it affects your list for later rounds. UAC advises accepting any offer you receive and then removing that course from your preference list so it does not block offers to other courses in future rounds. Rules differ between centres, so read your admissions centre’s advice on what to do with your list after an offer.

Worked example: building a preference list around your ATAR

Here is how one student might build and adjust a preference list in the days after results. The student and course thresholds are invented, but the process is the one admissions centres and careers advisers recommend.

Before results day

Amira is a NSW student who wants to study physiotherapy. Her estimate, based on trial results and an online calculator, is an ATAR of 92, with a realistic range of 89 to 95. She is eligible for a small subject adjustment at two universities. Before results day, her UAC list of five preferences looks like this:

  1. Physiotherapy at University A (last year’s lowest selection rank: 96.5)
  2. Physiotherapy at University B (last year: 94.0)
  3. Exercise physiology at University B (last year: 88.0)
  4. Exercise and sport science at University C (last year: 82.0)
  5. Health science at University C (last year: 75.0)

Her list follows the three-layer pattern. The top two are her dream courses, the middle is realistic, and the bottom two are options she would genuinely accept, one of which is very safe. She has also written down UAC’s deadline: 11.59pm Friday 18 December.

Scenario 1: her ATAR is 95.10

With a 95.10 and a two-point subject adjustment at University B, her selection rank there is around 97. Physiotherapy at University A is still slightly above last year’s threshold for her ATAR, but thresholds move and she has nothing to lose by keeping it first. She leaves her list unchanged. In December Round 2 she receives an offer for physiotherapy at University B, her second preference, because she was not quite above University A’s threshold this year. She accepts it and keeps watching later rounds in case University A’s threshold drops.

Scenario 2: her ATAR is 92.00

With a 92.00, her University B selection rank is about 94, right on last year’s physiotherapy threshold. She keeps both physiotherapy courses at the top. She checks whether University B offers a pathway from exercise physiology into physiotherapy, and finds that strong first-year results can support a transfer application. She keeps exercise physiology third, as the most direct backup. Her list stays the same, and her plan B is clear.

Scenario 3: her ATAR is 87.50

An 87.50 is below her realistic range. Before changing anything, she takes a break. Later in the day she checks the numbers: with the adjustment, her selection rank for exercise physiology at University B is around 89.5, just above last year’s 88.0. Physiotherapy now looks unlikely in this round, but there is no penalty for keeping it on the list. She decides to keep University B physiotherapy first, move exercise physiology up to second, and add a physiotherapy pathway course at another university to replace University A, which is now out of reach. She keeps her two safe options at the bottom. She makes the changes on Thursday, checks them again on Friday afternoon, and confirms they are saved well before the deadline.

What the example shows

  • A good list works across a range of results, so most scenarios need only small changes.
  • Dream courses can stay at the top; there is no penalty for aiming high.
  • Selection ranks, not ATARs, decide offers, so adjustments matter.
  • Pathways turn a disappointing result into a longer route rather than a dead end.
  • Changes are made calmly, checked and saved before the deadline.

Results day scenarios: what to do next

Every student’s situation is different, but most fall into a handful of common scenarios. Here is what to do in each, using the 2026 dates.

Scenario A: your ATAR is higher than you expected

Congratulations. Your main job now is to decide whether you want to aim higher. If there is a course you had ruled out but would genuinely prefer, add it above your current first preference before your deadline. If you are happy with your current plan, there is no need to change anything just because your number allows it. If you hold an early offer you are happy with, think carefully before letting it go, and read your admissions centre’s guidance on early offers and later rounds.

Scenario B: your ATAR is just below the threshold you wanted

Keep the course on your list. Thresholds change from year to year, and adjustment factors may lift your selection rank above your ATAR. Check every adjustment you might be eligible for. Then make sure your next preferences include a close alternative and at least one safe option. If you miss out in the first round, keep watching later rounds, where thresholds sometimes drop slightly as places are filled.

Scenario C: your ATAR is well below what you hoped

Take time before deciding anything. Then look at pathways: diplomas, enabling programs, related degrees with transfer options and different campuses. Talk to the universities’ future student teams, which deal with this situation every year. Rebuild your preference list so that it includes courses you would accept at your actual selection rank, with your preferred pathway near the top. A lower ATAR changes the route, not necessarily the destination.

Scenario D: you already have an early offer

Check whether your early offer is conditional, and whether your results meet the condition. If you are happy with the course, follow your admissions centre’s instructions for accepting it. If your ATAR opens a course you would prefer, find out from your admissions centre how early offers interact with later rounds before you make changes, because the rules differ between states and schemes.

Scenario E: you are applying interstate

Find out which round in the other state will be the first to include your ATAR. For example, Western Australian ATARs arrive on 20 December, after UAC’s December Round 2 deadline, so WA students’ first UAC offers will generally come in January Round 1. Keep a home-state option on your list in case the interstate offer comes later or not at all.

Scenario F: you did not receive an ATAR

First, find out why. Check that you met your state’s eligibility rules and, in Queensland, that you registered with QTAC and achieved a C or better in an English subject. If you think there is an error, contact your admissions centre or, in Tasmania, use TASC’s Results Query form. If you are not eligible, ask universities about entry without an ATAR, such as vocational pathways, enabling programs or alternative entry schemes.

Scenario G: you are unsure whether to go to university at all

Results day can bring this question into focus. It is a legitimate question, and there is no rush. Many universities allow deferral, so you can accept an offer and start a year later. Vocational education, apprenticeships and work are all valid next steps, and university will still be there if you decide to apply later. Your Year 12 results do not expire.

How to read course thresholds after results

Once you have your ATAR, the next question is usually “is it enough?” The answer depends on reading course information correctly.

Lowest selection rank

Most course information shows the lowest selection rank that received an offer in the previous year. A selection rank includes adjustment factors, so the lowest selection rank is not the same as the lowest ATAR. Some students in the course will have been admitted with ATARs below the listed figure, because adjustments lifted their selection rank.

Thresholds move

The lowest selection rank depends on how many people applied, how many places were offered and how strong the applicants were. It can move up or down from year to year. Treat last year’s figure as a guide, and be especially cautious with small courses, where a handful of applicants can move the threshold noticeably.

Guaranteed entry ranks

Some universities publish a guaranteed entry rank for certain courses. If your selection rank is at or above the guaranteed rank and you meet any prerequisites, you are assured of an offer if the course is your highest eligible preference. Where a guarantee exists, it is very useful for planning, because it removes the uncertainty of a moving threshold.

Prerequisites and other requirements

An ATAR is not the only requirement for many courses. Some have subject prerequisites, such as a particular level of mathematics or English. Others require interviews, portfolios, auditions or admissions tests. Medicine, many health courses and creative arts courses often have additional criteria and their own timelines. Check every requirement, not just the rank.

Assumed knowledge

Some courses list assumed knowledge rather than prerequisites. You can be admitted without it, but the course will be taught as if you have it. If you are missing assumed knowledge, many universities offer bridging courses over summer.

When adjustment factors are applied

Adjustment factors can make a real difference after results, so it is worth understanding when they are applied.

Automatic adjustments

Some adjustments are applied automatically, using information the admissions centre already has, such as your subject results or your address. If you are eligible, your selection rank for the relevant courses is calculated without you needing to do anything, although it is worth checking that your details are correct.

Adjustments that need an application

Others require an application with supporting documents, especially equity schemes such as UAC’s Educational Access Schemes and VTAC’s SEAS. These have their own deadlines, which can fall well before results day if you want them applied in the first ATAR-based round. UAC, for example, advises submitting EAS documents by 19 November for December Round 2. If you miss a deadline, an adjustment may still be applied in a later round.

University-specific schemes

Many universities run their own schemes, for example for students from particular schools or regions, elite athletes or students with strong results in particular subjects. These schemes vary widely and change from year to year, so check each university’s website. Our guides to bonus points by university, maximum bonus points and regional bonus points are good starting points.

Results day for popular courses

Some courses have their own requirements and timelines that matter just as much as the ATAR release date. If you are applying for one of the courses below, here is what to keep in mind in the weeks around results day.

Medicine, dentistry and veterinary science

Undergraduate medicine and related courses usually combine the ATAR with an admissions test, typically the UCAT ANZ, and often an interview. That means your ATAR is only one part of the decision, and the timeline can be longer than for other courses. UAC notes that some medical degrees do not make offers in December Round 2 and wait until January, and it publishes separate key dates for medicine, dentistry and veterinary science, including assessment days that eligible candidates must attend after the release of their ATAR. If you are applying for these courses, read those dates carefully and keep the days immediately after results free. Our guides to the ATAR for medicine, UCAT and ATAR for medicine, dentistry and veterinary science explain typical requirements, and our postgraduate vs undergraduate medicine guide covers the longer pathway if direct entry does not work out.

Law

Law is often offered as a double degree with high selection ranks. Most law courses rely on the ATAR alone, although some universities use additional criteria or run alternative entry schemes. After results, compare your selection rank, not just your ATAR, with each university’s law threshold, and remember that many students enter law through a different degree and transfer, or through a postgraduate Juris Doctor later. See our guides to the ATAR for law, combined law degrees and the lowest ATAR for law.

Nursing and health sciences

Nursing and many allied health courses have a wide range of thresholds across universities and campuses. Some also have requirements related to placements, such as health checks and working with children checks, which you complete after enrolling. If your ATAR is below the threshold at your first-choice university, the same course at another campus or university may be within reach. See our guides to the ATAR for nursing, physiotherapy, pharmacy and psychology.

Teaching

Initial teacher education courses have non-academic requirements as well as academic ones. Applicants are often asked to provide a written statement about their motivation and suitability for teaching. South Australian schools, for example, remind education applicants to submit a Teaching Capabilities Statement as part of their SATAC application. Check whether your chosen teaching course needs such a statement and when it is due, because missing it can stop an offer even if your ATAR is high enough. See our guide to the ATAR for teaching.

Engineering and science

Engineering and many science courses have mathematics prerequisites or assumed knowledge. After results, check that you met any prerequisite, because a strong ATAR does not override a missing prerequisite. If you are missing assumed knowledge, look for a summer bridging course. See our guide to the ATAR for engineering.

Commerce and business

Commerce and business courses are offered at most universities with a wide range of thresholds. Many have mathematics assumed knowledge. Double degrees combining commerce with law, engineering or science usually have higher thresholds than the single degree. See our guide to the ATAR for commerce.

Creative and performing arts

Music, performing arts, fine arts and design courses often use auditions, interviews or portfolios, sometimes alongside a minimum ATAR. These assessments frequently happen before results day. The University of Tasmania, for example, lists November as the start of interviews and auditions for music, performing arts and fine arts. If you are applying for these courses, results day may matter less than your audition or portfolio, but check any minimum ATAR requirement.

Questions to ask a university after results

Universities’ future student teams are busy in the days after results, so it helps to have clear questions ready. Here are some of the most useful.

  • What was the lowest selection rank for this course last year, and how many offers were made? This tells you how competitive the course was and how much the threshold might move.
  • Which adjustment factors apply to me for this course? Ask specifically about subject adjustments, regional adjustments and any university-specific schemes.
  • Is there a guaranteed entry rank? If so, and you meet it, you can plan with much more certainty.
  • Are there pathways into this course if I miss out? Ask about diplomas, related degrees, internal transfers and the grades needed to transfer.
  • Can I study the same course at another campus? Thresholds can differ between campuses.
  • Can I defer if I accept an offer? Useful if you are considering a gap year.
  • Are there any non-academic requirements? Statements, interviews, portfolios or checks that need to be completed.
  • What scholarships are available, and when do applications close? Some close soon after offers.

Write down the answers, including the name of the person you spoke to and the date. If you speak to several universities, notes help you compare options calmly before your deadline.

How to prepare for results day

Two things make the day easier. First, check your login and contact details early, so you can see your result the moment it is released. Second, plan for both outcomes in advance.

Know what you will do if your ATAR beats your target, and what your backup options are if it falls short. Deciding while calm, before the number arrives, takes a lot of pressure off the day itself.

Plan for both outcomes in writing

Planning “for both outcomes” is easier said than done, so make it concrete. Take a sheet of paper or a note on your phone and write two short plans.

Plan A: if my ATAR is at or above my estimate. List your preferences in order. Note any courses you might add if your result is strong. Write down the deadline for changing preferences and the time you will make any changes.

Plan B: if my ATAR is below my estimate. List the courses you would still be happy with, including at least one you are very likely to receive. Note the pathways into your dream course, such as a diploma or a related degree. Write down who you will call for advice: a careers adviser, a university’s future students team or the admissions centre.

Writing both plans down before results day has a calming effect. Whatever number arrives, you already know what you will do next, and you are following a plan rather than making decisions in the most emotional moment of the year.

Know your estimate, and how uncertain it is

Most students go into results day with some idea of where their ATAR will land, from school assessments, trial exams or an online calculator. That estimate is useful, but it comes with uncertainty. Exam marks can differ from school marks, scaling changes from year to year, and small changes in your aggregate can move your ATAR by more than you might expect in the middle of the range.

A sensible approach is to think of your estimate as a range rather than a single number. If your estimate is 85, plan for anything from about 80 to 90, and make sure your preference list works across that whole range. Our guides on how accurate ATAR calculators are and ATAR predictor accuracy explain why a range is more honest than a point estimate.

Line up advice before you need it

Results day is busy for everyone who gives advice. School careers advisers, university future student teams and admissions centre help lines all experience their highest demand in the days after release. Find out now:

  • whether your school’s careers adviser will be available after results, and how to contact them,
  • which universities in your state are running information days, help days or change of preference events, and when. Curtin University, for example, is running a Year 12 help day on 20 December, the day WA ATARs are released,
  • the help line for your admissions centre, and its hours around results day.

Look after the basics

It sounds obvious, but the days around results are easier if you look after yourself. Sleep the night before, eat breakfast before you log in, and plan something enjoyable for later in the day regardless of the result. If you are working a holiday job, consider asking for results morning off, so you are not checking your phone in a break room or making preference decisions on a lunch break.

A results-day plan, step by step

Here is a simple plan you can adapt for the day your results arrive. The times are examples; adjust them to your state’s release time.

Before release

  • Wake up with time to spare. Aim to be awake and settled at least half an hour before release, not rushing to log in.
  • Have your details ready. Your student number, PINs, passwords and any app installed and logged in where possible.
  • Choose your setting. Some students want family around; others prefer to look alone first. Decide in advance and tell the people around you.

The first hour after release

  • Look at your results. Read your subject results and your ATAR. If your state releases them separately, look at both before drawing conclusions.
  • Check that it makes sense. Compare your ATAR with your subject results. If something looks badly wrong, such as a missing subject, note it and contact the relevant body later in the day.
  • Pause. Take at least half an hour before changing anything. Share your news, have a coffee, go for a walk.

Later that day

  • Review your preferences against your plan. Take out your Plan A or Plan B. Check your list against the course thresholds and your likely selection rank.
  • Get advice if you are unsure. Call a university’s future students line, attend an information session or talk to your careers adviser.
  • Make changes calmly. If you decide to change your preferences, do it carefully, double-check the course codes and save the changes. Note how many free saves you have, if your centre limits them.

Before the deadline

  • Review your list once more. The night before the deadline, read your list one last time, from top to bottom, and ask whether you would be happy with each course if it were the one you were offered.
  • Confirm it is saved. Log in again and check your final list is the one on the system. Do not leave this until the last few minutes.

Offer day

  • Check your email and account at release time. Offers are released at set times: 7.30am for UAC, 10am for VTAC’s December round, from 11am for QTAC’s 23 December round, and by 9am for SATAC’s January round.
  • Read the offer carefully. Note whether it is conditional, how to accept, and the response deadline.
  • Respond on time. Accept, defer or decline before the deadline, following the university’s instructions.
A simple rule for results day
Look, pause, then act. Read your results, take a break before deciding anything, and then make changes calmly against the plan you wrote before results day.

If your ATAR is not what you hoped

A lower ATAR is not the end of the road. Universities run many pathways for exactly this situation: diplomas that lead into a degree, enabling and foundation programs, and course transfers after a year of strong university marks.

Adjustment factors can also lift your selection rank above your ATAR for a course. So before results day, it is worth knowing your backup courses and pathways, so a disappointing number does not feel like a dead end.

First, check the number properly

Before deciding your ATAR is a disappointment, make sure you are comparing the right things. Course thresholds are usually published as the lowest selection rank that received an offer, not the lowest ATAR. Your selection rank for a course may be higher than your ATAR if you are eligible for adjustment factors. A course that looks out of reach on ATAR alone may still be within range once adjustments are added. Our guide to why your selection rank can be higher than your ATAR explains how this works.

It is also worth remembering that thresholds are set by the applicants in each year. Last year’s selection rank is a guide, not a fixed requirement. Thresholds can rise or fall by a few points, especially for courses with smaller intakes.

Adjustment factors you might have forgotten

Adjustment factors, sometimes still called bonus points, are added to your ATAR for a particular course to give your selection rank. Common types include:

  • Subject adjustments for strong results in subjects related to the course, such as mathematics or languages.
  • Regional or rural adjustments for students who live or study in particular areas.
  • Equity adjustments through schemes such as UAC’s Educational Access Schemes or VTAC’s SEAS, for students who have experienced disadvantage.
  • Elite athlete and performer adjustments at some universities.

Some adjustments are applied automatically, while others require an application. Check whether you have applied for everything you are eligible for. See our guides to ATAR bonus points, regional bonus points and subject bonus points.

Pathways into the course you wanted

If your selection rank is still below the course you wanted, there are well-trodden routes in:

  • Diplomas and associate degrees that lead into the second year of a related bachelor degree, often with guaranteed entry if you achieve a set grade average.
  • Related degrees with internal transfer. Many students start in a related course, perform strongly in their first year and then transfer. Transfer rules vary, so check the university’s requirements.
  • Enabling and foundation programs that prepare students for university and lead to entry to a range of degrees.
  • A different campus of the same university, where the same course may have a lower threshold.
  • Later offer rounds, where places can open up as other students decline offers.

Taking a gap year or repeating subjects

Some students decide to take a break before university, either because they want time out or because they plan to improve their result. A gap year can be valuable, and many universities allow you to defer an offer. Repeating Year 12 subjects is possible in most states, and the rules for how repeated or additional subjects count towards a new ATAR differ by state. In Queensland, for example, QTAC sets minimum numbers of additional subjects to qualify for a new ATAR. If you are considering this, talk to your school and your admissions centre before deciding.

Remember that the first round is not the last

The first ATAR-based round is the biggest, but it is followed by several more. If you do not receive an offer, or do not receive the one you wanted, keep your list up to date and keep checking. Students regularly receive offers in January and February rounds for courses they had given up on.

Checking or querying your results

Occasionally a result looks wrong. Most of the time it is correct and simply unexpected, but every state has a process for checking results. Here is what is available.

Check the basics first

Before requesting a formal check, look for simple explanations. Is every subject you completed listed? Did a subject you expected to count actually count? In some states, the ATAR only counts certain subjects or combinations, so a result you thought would be included may not be. Are you comparing your ATAR with an estimate that used last year’s scaling? Many surprises disappear once you understand which results were used.

For 2025 HSC students, these services ran from 19 December 2025 to 9 May 2026; the 2026 timetable follows a similar pattern. In Victoria, the Post Results and ATAR Service is the main contact for questions immediately after results are released, on 1800 653 080. It can explain your results and point you to the right process if you think there is an error.

South Australia and the NT: SACE Board services

TASC asks students who believe there is a problem with their results, including students who expected an ATAR but did not receive one, to use its Results Query form.

Queensland: QCAA and QTAC

Queensland students should raise questions about subject results with their school and the QCAA, and questions about the ATAR with QTAC. If your ATAR has not appeared, first check that you created your QTAC ATAR account and that the details match your QCAA learning account.

What a check can and cannot do

A results check confirms that your marks were recorded and added correctly. It is not a second opinion on the quality of your work, and in most cases results do not change. If a genuine error is found, the result is corrected and, where it affects your ATAR, the admissions centre recalculates it. Because checks take time, it is wise to finalise your preferences for the first round based on the results you have, rather than waiting for the outcome of a check.

Results day for regional and rural students

Students from regional, rural and remote areas often face extra decisions on results day, especially if accepting an offer means moving away from home.

Regional adjustment factors

Many universities add adjustment factors for students who live or attend school in regional areas. Some of these are applied automatically based on your address or school, while others need an application. Check which apply to you before you finalise your preferences, because they can lift your selection rank for some courses by several points. See our regional bonus points calculator and regional postcodes guide.

Accommodation and timing

If you will need to move, think about accommodation early. University residential colleges and student accommodation often open applications well before offers, and some allocate rooms in order of application. Having an application in before results day gives you more choice once your offer arrives.

Regional campuses and pathways

Many universities have regional campuses offering the same or similar courses, sometimes with different thresholds. Studying close to home for the first year and transferring later is a common and sensible pathway. Rural and regional entry schemes in medicine and health, in particular, are designed to support students from regional backgrounds. Our guides to the best universities for regional students, rural entry schemes and rural medical school entry explore these options.

Support services

Many universities run dedicated support for regional students, including transition programs, financial support and mentoring. Ask about these when you speak to future student teams after results.

If your year was disrupted

Some students experience illness, injury, family difficulties or other disruption during Year 12. Each state has processes to take this into account, both in your Year 12 results and in university admission.

Disruption during exams

Curriculum authorities have processes for students whose exam performance was affected by illness or misadventure. NESA runs illness and misadventure applications for the HSC. In Victoria, the VCAA has a derived examination score process for students affected during VCE exams. In Western Australia, SCSA has sickness and misadventure applications, and students can download the outcome from the student portal. In Queensland, the QCAA’s access arrangements and reasonable adjustments (AARA) apply to assessment, and in South Australia the SACE Board runs special provisions. These processes have deadlines that usually fall during or soon after exams, so if you think one applies to you, ask your school immediately.

Disadvantage and university admission

Separately, admissions centres and universities run equity schemes that can add adjustment factors to your selection rank if you have experienced educational disadvantage. These include UAC’s Educational Access Schemes and VTAC’s Special Entry Access Scheme. The University of Tasmania lists 11.59pm on 4 January 2027 as its closing date for special consideration applications. If you have not applied and think you may be eligible, check whether late applications are still accepted for later offer rounds.

Students with disability

Students with disability may be eligible for adjustments in exams and for equity schemes in admission. After you accept an offer, contact the university’s disability or accessibility service early, so support is in place before semester starts.

Estimate your ATAR before results

You do not have to wait until December to get a sense of where you stand. Our ATAR calculators estimate your ATAR from your predicted or current marks, using the latest official scaling for your state.

An estimate is a guide, not your official result, but it helps you set your preferences and expectations before the real number lands.

Choose the calculator for your state

Because each state calculates the ATAR differently, use an estimate built for your own system. Our state calculators apply each state’s aggregate rules and the latest published scaling data:

  • NSW: the NSW ATAR calculator or the HSC ATAR calculator, which use the best 2 units of English plus the best 8 other units.
  • Victoria: the VIC ATAR calculator or the VCE ATAR calculator, which use English plus your best three scaled study scores and 10 per cent of the fifth and sixth.
  • Queensland: the QLD ATAR calculator or the QCE ATAR calculator, which use your best five eligible results.
  • Western Australia: the WA ATAR calculator or the WACE ATAR calculator, which use your best four scaled scores plus bonuses.
  • South Australia: the SA ATAR calculator or the SACE ATAR calculator, built on the 90-point university aggregate.
  • Tasmania: the TAS ATAR calculator or the TCE ATAR calculator.
  • ACT and NT: the ACT ATAR calculator and the NT ATAR calculator.

Queensland students can also use QTAC’s own ATAR Navigator tool, which offers an ATAR prediction based on your subjects and results to help with your QTAC application.

Which marks to enter

Your estimate is only as good as the marks you put in. Before exams, your best inputs are recent school assessment results and trial exam marks. After exams, if you have a good sense of how each exam went, you can adjust each subject up or down. Be honest with yourself: entering your best-case marks gives you a best-case ATAR, which is not much use for planning. Our guides to estimating your ATAR from trial results and using mock exam results explain how to choose sensible inputs.

Run three scenarios

Rather than one estimate, run three: a cautious version with slightly lower marks than you expect, a realistic version, and an optimistic version. The spread between them shows how much your result could move. If your preference list works across all three, you are well prepared for results day. If it only works for the optimistic scenario, add a safer option to the bottom of your list.

Work backwards from your goal

If you have a specific course in mind, it can help to work backwards. Our ATAR target calculator shows the aggregate and approximate marks you would need for a target ATAR. Combined with the course’s published selection rank and any adjustment factors you expect, this gives you a clear picture of how close you are, and whether you need a pathway option as well.

Why estimates and official ATARs differ

Even a careful estimate can differ from your official ATAR. The most common reasons are:

  • Scaling changes. Calculators use the latest published scaling, but each year’s scaling depends on that year’s students.
  • Exam results. Your final subject result combines school assessment and exams in proportions that differ by state and subject.
  • Moderation. In several states, school assessment marks are moderated against exam performance, which can move your school marks.
  • Which subjects count. Rules about English, the number of subjects and restricted combinations can change which results end up in your aggregate.

That is why an estimate is best treated as a planning tool. It is excellent for setting preferences and expectations, and it is no substitute for the official number. If you want to understand the limits in detail, see our guide to improving your predicted ATAR.

What your results will include

On results morning you typically receive two things. From your admissions centre comes your ATAR, the rank universities use. From your state’s school authority come your subject results, the marks or study scores behind that rank.

They arrive around the same time, often on the same morning, but from two different bodies. Seeing both together lets you check that your rank makes sense against your subject results.

What you receive in each state

The names of the documents differ around the country. Here is what students typically receive with their 2026 results.

NSW

From NESA, you receive your HSC results for each course, including your HSC mark and band, and your Record of Achievement, which lists every HSC course you completed and your results. Students who complete the HSC are awarded the Higher School Certificate. From UAC, you receive your ATAR, which you view on UAC’s website using your student number and PIN.

ACT

The BSSS issues the ACT Senior Secondary Certificate to students who meet its requirements, and a Tertiary Entrance Statement, including the ATAR, to students on the tertiary pathway. Your ATAR is also available through UAC.

Victoria

Through the VCE Results and ATAR Service, you receive your VCE study scores and your ATAR together. Paid VTAC applicants are then invited to access an official digital ATAR statement through My eQuals, and other students can order a printed ATAR statement from VTAC. Students who satisfactorily complete the VCE are awarded the Victorian Certificate of Education.

Queensland

From the QCAA, you receive a Senior Education Profile in your learning account. For students finishing Year 12, the profile includes a Senior Statement listing your results and, if you are eligible, your Queensland Certificate of Education. Some students receive a Queensland Certificate of Individual Achievement instead. From QTAC, you receive your ATAR through the ATAR portal. In Queensland, ATARs at or below 30 are reported as “30.00 or less”.

South Australia and the Northern Territory

From the SACE Board, you receive your Stage 2 results in Students Online and, if you are eligible, your SACE or NTCET, with the certificate sent by post. From SATAC, eligible students receive a Tertiary Entrance Statement showing their university aggregate out of 90 and their ATAR, available digitally through Students Online.

Western Australia

From SCSA, you can see your WACE achievement and examination results in the student portal. Digital copies of your WACE, your Western Australian Statement of Student Achievement and your ATAR course reports become available from mid-January 2027, with award certificates from mid-February. From TISC, you receive your ATAR and scaled scores, and you will receive a University Admissions Advice Letter by email.

Tasmania

From TASC, you receive your results by email on results day, including your ATAR if you are eligible. Printed results documents and any certificates you achieved, such as the Tasmanian Certificate of Education, are posted by the end of January.

How to check your ATAR makes sense

Once you have both your subject results and your ATAR, a quick sense check is worthwhile. Look at your strongest subjects: are they the ones you expected to count? Look at your weakest: did a rule, such as the requirement to include English, mean it counted anyway? Then compare with an estimate. If your official ATAR is within a few points of a careful estimate, everything is probably as it should be. If it is very different, check which subjects counted before assuming there is an error.

What if you did not receive an ATAR?

Some students are surprised to find they have results but no ATAR. Common reasons include not meeting the eligibility rules, for example not completing enough eligible subjects or, in Queensland, not achieving a C or better in an English subject; not registering for an ATAR where registration is required, as in Queensland; or choosing a pathway that does not lead to an ATAR, such as the VCE Vocational Major in Victoria. If you expected an ATAR and did not receive one, contact your admissions centre or, in Tasmania, use TASC’s Results Query form. If you are not eligible, universities still have pathways for you, which admissions centres and universities can explain.

Keep copies of everything

Download or save copies of your results and your ATAR statement as soon as they are available. Results portals do not stay open forever. NESA, for example, notes that students’ Students Online accounts close some months after results, and SCSA and other authorities move documents into different systems over time. You may need your results later for scholarships, gap-year programs, jobs or applications in future years, so store them somewhere safe.

What your ATAR means once you have it

When the number arrives, it helps to know exactly what it represents. The ATAR is a percentile rank from 0.00 to 99.95, in steps of 0.05. It shows your position relative to all students in your age group, not just those who finished Year 12. An ATAR of 80.00 means you finished ahead of about 80 per cent of your age group.

Why it is not a percentage mark

Because the ATAR is a rank, it does not tell you what percentage of the course content you mastered. A student with an ATAR of 75 did not “get 75 per cent”. They placed in roughly the top quarter of their age group. This is why ATARs often look lower than students expect from their marks: a set of strong marks can translate to an ATAR in the 80s or 90s, depending on scaling and the strength of the cohort.

Why the age group matters

The ATAR compares you with everyone in your age group, including people who did not finish Year 12 or did not seek an ATAR. Because those people are part of the comparison group but do not receive ATARs, the typical ATAR among students who do receive one is well above 50. So an ATAR around the middle of the range is a respectable result, and it opens many courses.

Comparing across states

ATARs are calculated by each state from its own certificate, but they are treated as equivalent between states. An ATAR of 85 from Queensland and an ATAR of 85 from Victoria mean the same thing for university selection. This is what makes interstate applications straightforward.

Putting your ATAR in context

To judge your result, compare it with the selection ranks of the courses you want, not with your friends’ ATARs or with general labels. A “good” ATAR is one that gets you into the course you want, possibly with adjustments. Our guides to what counts as a good ATAR and state-specific pages such as a good ATAR in NSW, in Victoria and in Queensland give useful reference points.

Your next steps after results

Once you have your ATAR, the process is not over. You finalise your university preferences, in order, and wait for offers. Many centres let you adjust your preferences after seeing your ATAR, which is worth doing if your number differs from what you expected.

Then offers come in rounds from late December through January and February, with South Australia and the NT starting in mid-January. If your first preference comes through, you accept it. If not, later rounds and pathways give you more chances, so a quiet first round is not the end of the story.

The first week after results

The week after your results is usually the busiest of the whole process. In most states it contains your change of preference deadline, information days at universities, and, by the end of it, the first ATAR-based offers. A simple way to stay on top of it is to keep a short list of three things: your deadline, your current preference list and the date of the next offer round. Check all three each day until the first round has been released.

Enrolment, orientation and the start of semester

Once you accept an offer, the university will send instructions for enrolment. Enrolment involves choosing your units, setting up your student account and, for Commonwealth supported places, completing government forms. Orientation usually runs in February, with semester 1 starting in late February or early March. In South Australia, for example, Adelaide University’s 2027 orientation runs from 8 to 19 February with classes starting on 22 February, and Flinders University’s semester 1 starts on 1 March 2027.

Keep an eye on later rounds

If you accepted an offer for a lower preference, you may still receive an offer for a higher preference in a later round. Keep checking your email and admissions centre account until the final rounds in February, and respond promptly if a new offer arrives. If you do switch, follow the university’s instructions for declining or withdrawing from the first offer so the place can go to someone else.

After you accept an offer: the next steps

Accepting an offer is a milestone, but there are a few more steps between accepting and your first class. Most are straightforward if you do them in order.

1. Accept the offer correctly

Follow the instructions in your offer email exactly. Depending on the admissions centre and university, you may accept through the admissions centre’s portal or the university’s own system. Do it before the response date shown on the offer. Keep a screenshot or confirmation email.

2. Watch for university emails

After acceptance, the university will start sending emails about your student account, enrolment and orientation. Check your email regularly over the summer, including spam folders, and make sure the address you gave is one you will keep using.

3. Enrol in your units

Enrolment is when you choose your units for the first semester and confirm your study load. Universities provide course maps and advice for first-year students; follow the recommended pattern unless you have a good reason to change it. If you are unsure, contact the university’s student centre.

4. Sort out fees and government forms

Most domestic undergraduate students study in a Commonwealth supported place and can defer their student contribution through a government loan. Your university will guide you through the forms during enrolment. Read them carefully and ask the university if anything is unclear.

5. Look into scholarships and accommodation

Many scholarships and student accommodation options have deadlines in December and January, some of which fall soon after offers. If you need either, look into them as early as possible, ideally before results day, so you can apply quickly once your offer arrives.

6. Attend orientation

Orientation usually runs in February. It is the best time to learn your way around campus, meet other students and understand how your course works. Many universities also run online preparation programs during summer.

7. Keep an eye on later rounds if you want to

If you accepted a lower preference but still hope for a higher one, keep your preference list up to date and keep checking for offers in later rounds. If a better offer arrives, follow the instructions for accepting the new offer and withdrawing from the old one.

Applying interstate: how the 2026 dates interact

Students who apply to universities in another state face an extra layer of timing, because their ATAR comes from one admissions centre and their offers from another. Here is how the 2026 dates fit together for the most common combinations.

Applying to NSW or ACT universities from another state

UAC’s first ATAR-based round, December Round 2, has a change of preference deadline of 11.59pm Sydney time on Friday 18 December, with offers on Wednesday 23 December. Students whose ATARs are released before that deadline, such as Victorian, South Australian, Northern Territory and Tasmanian students, and Queensland students from 9am Brisbane time on 18 December, have their ATARs available before the deadline. Whether UAC receives interstate results in time for a particular round is up to UAC, so check its guidance. Western Australian students, whose ATARs arrive on 20 December, will generally receive their first UAC offers in January Round 1 on Friday 8 January 2027.

Applying to Victorian universities from another state

VTAC’s December round is for current Year 12 students, and VTAC says interstate Year 12 students are eligible if it receives their results in time. The University of Melbourne’s guidance for 2026, for example, lists NSW, ACT and Tasmanian ATARs on 16 December and Western Australian ATARs on 20 December, with a change of preference deadline of 12pm on Thursday 24 December for January round 1, released on Tuesday 12 January 2027. Because VTAC’s December round change of preference closes on 12 December, most interstate students should expect their first Victorian offer in January round 1.

Applying to Queensland universities from another state

QTAC’s first major round for Year 12 students is on Wednesday 23 December, with a change of preference deadline of 12pm Monday 21 December. Interstate students whose ATARs are released by then may be considered in that round, subject to QTAC receiving their results. The next round, on Thursday 14 January 2027, has a change of preference deadline of 11.45pm on Thursday 7 January.

Applying to SA, NT or WA universities from another state

SATAC’s first round with Year 12 results is on Thursday 14 January 2027, after a change of preference deadline on Wednesday 6 January, so interstate students’ ATARs will all be available in time. TISC’s main round is on Thursday 24 December, after a change of preference deadline of 11.59pm Sunday 20 December; interstate students should check TISC’s guidance on which round applies to them.

Applying to the University of Tasmania from another state

The University of Tasmania makes first-round offers to Tasmanian Year 12 students in mid-December, and to interstate Year 12 and IB applicants progressively in January.

The interstate rule of thumb
Your ATAR travels between admissions centres automatically, but not instantly. If your ATAR is released close to another state’s deadline, assume your first offer from that state may come in its January round, and keep a home-state option on your list.

For parents and carers: helping on results day

Results day is a big moment for families as well as students. Parents and carers often want to help but are not sure how, especially if the system has changed since they finished school. A few principles go a long way.

Let your young person lead

Ask in advance how they want to receive their results. Some students want family beside them when they log in; others want to look privately and share the news when they are ready. Either is reasonable. Respecting their choice signals that the result is theirs, and makes it easier for them to talk honestly about it afterwards.

Know the dates and deadlines

One of the most practical ways to help is to know the timeline. Note the release time, the change of preference deadline and the first offer round for your state, using the tables on this page. Deadlines often fall within two or three days of results, and in Western Australia in 2026 the main round deadline falls on the same day as the ATAR release. A parent who knows the deadline can gently make sure it is not missed.

Help with the information, not the decision

You can be very helpful by gathering information: finding course thresholds, checking adjustment factors, looking up information days and pathway programs. Try to leave the final decisions to your young person, though. Students are more committed to courses they chose themselves, and it is their study and their career.

Respond to the person, not the number

Whatever the ATAR, the first response that helps most is about the person: recognition of the effort they put in over two years. Comparisons with siblings, friends or your own results rarely help on the day. If the result is disappointing, the conversation about options can wait an hour or two. Our guide to ATAR vs OP vs ENTER is useful if you want to translate your own old Year 12 score before any comparisons come up.

Plan something for later in the day

Many families find it helps to plan a meal or outing for the evening of results day, regardless of the outcome. It marks the end of school, gives everyone something to look forward to, and puts the number in perspective as one part of a bigger day.

For parents: responding to different outcomes

Parents and carers often worry most about what to say in the moment the result arrives. A few suggestions for different situations follow.

If the result is what they hoped for

Celebrate with them, and recognise the effort behind the result as well as the number itself. Then help them stay focused on the next steps, such as confirming preferences before the deadline. Strong results can lead to rushed decisions about more competitive courses, so it helps to ask gently whether a change is something they really want.

If the result is disappointing

Lead with empathy rather than solutions. “I’m sorry it wasn’t what you hoped for, I know how hard you worked” is usually more helpful than an immediate list of alternatives. Later in the day, when they are ready, you can help them look at adjustment factors, pathways and later rounds. Many students who receive a disappointing ATAR reach the course they wanted through a pathway, and hearing that from a calm adult can make a real difference.

If they seem unsure what they want

Results day sometimes reveals that a student is not sure about their plans. That is not a crisis. Deferral, gap years, vocational study and work are all valid options, and Year 12 results do not expire. Helping them gather information without pressure to decide immediately is often the most useful thing you can do.

If they want space

Some students want to be alone with their result for a while. Let them know you are available and that you are proud of them, and give them time. Most will want to talk once they have processed the news.

Talking about results with friends

Results day is social as well as personal. Group chats light up within minutes of release, and it can feel as though everyone is sharing their number. A few thoughts can make this easier.

You do not have to share your ATAR

Your results are yours. You can share them with everyone, with a few close friends or with nobody, and any of these choices is fine. If someone asks and you do not want to say, a simple “I’m happy with how it went” or “I’m still working out my options” is enough.

Be thoughtful about how you share good news

If your result is strong, celebrate it. It is also worth remembering that friends may be dealing with disappointment on the same day. Sharing your news with the people closest to you first, and checking in on friends before posting widely, goes a long way.

Check in on friends

If a friend goes quiet on results day, a short message asking how they are can matter a lot. You do not need to ask about their number. Asking how they are feeling, or suggesting you catch up later in the week, is often more helpful.

Remember that the numbers are not comparable

Friends took different subjects, faced different circumstances and are aiming for different courses. Two ATARs a few points apart may lead to exactly the same course, and a lower ATAR may lead to a course someone wanted more. The number that matters for each person is the one that gets them where they want to go.

If you are not a current Year 12 student

This page focuses on current Year 12 students, but plenty of other applicants follow results day closely.

Students who finished Year 12 in an earlier year

Your ATAR from a previous year does not change, and you can usually use it to apply in later years. Admissions centres can retrieve past results. In Victoria, VTAC offers an ATAR reprint service for students who completed Year 12 in a previous year. If you are completing additional Year 12 subjects in 2026 to qualify for a new ATAR, as QTAC allows, your new ATAR will be released on the normal results day.

Applicants who are not school leavers

Adults and other non-school leavers are assessed on different criteria, such as previous tertiary study, vocational qualifications, work experience or admissions tests. They are often eligible for offers before Christmas, because their results do not depend on Year 12 release. UAC’s December Round 1 and SATAC’s mid-December round, for example, include non-school-leaver applicants but do not use 2026 Year 12 results.

International students studying in Australia

International students completing an Australian Year 12 receive their results on the same days as domestic students, but their applications may follow different timetables and deadlines. SATAC, for example, lists 6 January 2027 as its final offer round for international Year 12 applicants. Check your admissions centre’s international key dates.

If you completed Year 12 over more than one year

Some students complete their senior studies over more than one year, either by accelerating in a subject, studying part-time, or repeating or adding subjects after finishing Year 12. Results day works slightly differently for them, and the rules depend on the state.

NSW

In NSW, UAC calculates the ATAR from the best ten units of scaled marks, regardless of the year in which each course was completed. That means results from accelerated courses or courses completed in an earlier year can count, as long as the rules are met. Students can also repeat courses to try to improve their result, and UAC explains how repeated courses are treated in its published guidance. If you completed courses in more than one year, your ATAR is released on the normal results day for the year you finish.

Queensland

In Queensland, ATAR eligibility requires results to be accumulated within a five-year period. Students who have already received an ATAR can qualify for a new one by completing additional subjects; QTAC sets the minimum as three General subjects, or two General subjects plus one Applied subject or VET qualification. Students in this situation access their new ATAR through their existing QTAC account on the normal release day.

Other states

Every state has rules about combining results from different years, repeating subjects and counting subjects completed early. The details differ, so if your studies span more than one year, check your admissions centre’s guidance well before results day, and make sure the curriculum authority has a complete record of your results.

Why it matters for results day

If your results come from more than one year, the most common surprise is a subject not counting the way you expected. Checking your eligibility and which results will be included before results day avoids that surprise, and gives you time to ask questions while help lines are quieter.

What schools do on results day

Schools play a supporting role on results day rather than delivering results. Many schools open their careers offices for students who want advice, run change of preference sessions or arrange for teachers to be available by phone or email. If your school does this, find out the times in advance.

Schools also receive information about their cohort’s results, and many publish highlights, such as the number of students above a particular ATAR or the cohort’s median ATAR. In Queensland in 2025, for example, some schools announced cohort statistics on the afternoon ATARs were released. Whether schools should publish such statistics is debated in some places, including the ACT. Either way, your own results are yours to share or keep private as you choose.

Deferral and gap years

Some students decide on results day, or soon after, that they would like a year before starting university. Many universities allow you to defer an offer, usually for one year, which means you accept now and start later. Deferral rules differ between universities and courses, and some courses, such as those with limited places or professional accreditation requirements, may not allow it. If you are thinking about deferring, accept your offer first, then check the university’s deferral process and deadline.

A gap year can be valuable for work, travel, saving money or simply resting after Year 12. If you plan to apply for a different course after a gap year, remember that your Year 12 results do not expire, and that study or work during the year may give you additional ways to strengthen your application.

How results day has changed over the years

Results day has looked very different for each generation of Year 12 students. Knowing a little of the history helps explain why the system works the way it does now.

From newspapers to phones

For decades, students learned their results by post, and many states published exam results and university offers in newspapers. In Victoria, VTAC’s history notes the year when course offers were first published in The Age. Over time, admissions moved from paper to phone and then online. VTAC launched its website in 1998, phased out paper applications in 2004 and later sent results by SMS. Today, results are released online, through apps, by email and by SMS, and official ATAR statements are increasingly digital.

Queensland joins the ATAR

Until 2019, Queensland students received an Overall Position (OP) rather than an ATAR. The first Queensland ATARs were released by QTAC on 19 December 2020, at the same time as QCAA results. Since then, every Australian state has released ATARs in the same December window. If you want the full history of the old ranks, see our guide to ATAR vs OP vs ENTER.

A nationally consistent window

Also from 2020, education ministers agreed that ATARs would be released within a nationally consistent window of dates. The admissions centres also coordinate through their national body, the Australasian Conference of Tertiary Admission Centres (ACTAC), which publishes a joint list of release dates ahead of results. In December 2025, for example, ACTAC’s joint announcement listed releases from Thursday 11 December in Victoria to Friday 19 December in Western Australia.

Earlier access, later certificates

A clear trend over the past decade has been to separate the electronic release of results from the delivery of printed documents. The SACE Board, for example, announced in 2015 that it would release results online two days earlier than in the previous year by separating the online release from the printing of certificates. Today, most states release results online on results day and send printed certificates weeks later. SCSA’s 2026 timetable, for instance, makes digital WACE documents available from mid-January and award certificates from mid-February 2027, and TASC posts printed results documents by the end of January.

Common results-day mistakes, and how to avoid them

Admissions centres and careers advisers see the same mistakes every year. Most are easy to avoid with a little preparation.

Mistake 1: not registering in advance

In Victoria you need to register for the VCE Results and ATAR Service, and in Queensland you must create a QTAC ATAR account for your ATAR to be calculated and released to you. Students who leave this until results morning risk delays at the worst possible time. Register now if you have not already.

Mistake 2: using a school email address

School email accounts are often closed soon after Year 12 finishes. If your admissions centre account or results notifications use a school address, you may miss important messages, including offers. Switch to a personal email address now.

Mistake 3: missing the change of preference deadline

In 2026, change of preference deadlines fall as little as two days after results in Victoria and NSW, and on the same day as results in Western Australia. Students who wait until “after the excitement settles” can miss the first ATAR-based round. Write your deadline down in local time.

Mistake 4: ordering preferences by likelihood

Putting a “safe” course first because you are sure of getting in means you will be offered that course even if you were eligible for something you wanted more. Order your list by what you want, with safer options lower down.

Mistake 5: comparing your ATAR with the wrong threshold

Course thresholds are usually selection ranks, which include adjustment factors. Comparing your raw ATAR with a selection rank can make a course look further away than it is. Check whether adjustments apply to you.

Mistake 6: removing your dream course too quickly

If your ATAR is slightly below last year’s threshold, keep the course on your list. Thresholds change from year to year, and there is no penalty for listing it. Just make sure the rest of your list includes courses you would accept.

Mistake 7: not responding to an offer in time

Offers come with response deadlines. QTAC’s offers on 23 December, for example, must be responded to by 30 December. Missing the deadline can mean losing the place. Check your email and account regularly through the offer period, even over the holidays.

Mistake 8: forgetting to save changes

Changing your preferences is only effective once you save or submit the changes. After making changes, log out, log back in and check the list is correct. With QTAC, remember that only the first three saves are free.

Mistake 9: making decisions in the first five minutes

The first minutes after seeing your result are emotional, whether it is good news or bad. Decisions made then are more likely to be reversed later. Look, pause, and then act.

Mistake 10: forgetting about later rounds

If the first round does not go your way, there are more. Keep your list updated and keep checking until February. Students regularly receive offers in later rounds.

Results day myths

A few persistent myths cause unnecessary worry around results day. Here is what is actually true.

Myth: “Logging in early gives you a better chance at offers”

The time you view your results has no effect on offers. Offers are made in rounds, after the change of preference deadline, based on everyone’s results and preferences at that deadline. Viewing your ATAR at 9am or 9pm makes no difference.

Myth: “Courses lower on your list are harder to get into”

Your position on the list does not change your chance of getting into a course. You are offered the highest course on your list for which you are eligible. UAC explicitly addresses this misconception in its advice to applicants.

Myth: “If you miss the first round, you have missed out”

Every admissions centre runs several rounds through January and February, and places become available as students accept and decline offers. Many students receive their offer in a later round.

Myth: “Your ATAR is your percentage mark”

The ATAR is a rank, not a mark. An ATAR of 80 means you placed ahead of about 80 per cent of your age group, not that you scored 80 per cent in your subjects. See our guide to what an ATAR is and our ATAR misconceptions page.

Myth: “Every state releases ATARs on the same day”

The dates are coordinated within a national window, but they are not identical. In 2026 they run from 10 December in Victoria to 20 December in Western Australia.

Myth: “The ATAR decides your whole future”

The ATAR is the main selection tool for school leavers, but it is one of many ways into university. Pathways, transfers, later study and work experience all open doors, and many people’s careers have little connection to their Year 12 rank.

If you finish Year 12 in 2027

If you are in Year 11 now, your results day will be in December 2027. The exact dates will be announced by each admissions centre during 2027, but the pattern described on this page is very stable from year to year, so you can expect a similar mid-to-late December window and a similar sequence of offer rounds. Some 2027 dates are already published: the QCAA’s calendar, for example, lists Friday 19 November 2027 as Queensland’s Year 12 finishing date. The best preparation for next year is the same as for this year: understand how your state calculates the ATAR, choose subjects you can do well in, and keep an eye on your admissions centre’s key dates as they are released. Our guide to predicting your ATAR in Year 11 is a good starting point.

Keeping results day in perspective

Results day feels huge, and that is understandable after a long year. But the ATAR is a key for one door, not a verdict on your worth. Whatever the number, you will have options, and most students end up on a path that suits them.

Line up support for the day, whether that is family or friends, and remember that offers, pathways and second rounds all follow. The number is a starting point, not a finish line.

The number is one input among many

It can help to remember what the ATAR is actually for. It is a tool admissions centres use to sort large numbers of school leavers for courses with limited places. It says something useful about your Year 12 results relative to your age group. It says nothing about your character, your creativity, your kindness or how well you will do at university or work. Many people who are now successful in their fields had an ATAR that seemed disappointing on the day.

Expect mixed emotions

Even students who receive a strong result often feel a strange mixture of relief, flatness and anxiety about what comes next. Students who receive a lower result than they hoped may feel disappointment, embarrassment or worry about telling others. All of these reactions are normal. Give yourself time to feel them before making big decisions.

Comparing yourself with friends

Social media and group chats can make results day feel like a public competition. Remember that everyone took different subjects, had different circumstances and is aiming for different courses. Your friends’ ATARs tell you nothing about what is right for you. If comparisons are making you feel worse, it is fine to mute group chats for a day.

Where to get support

If results day leaves you feeling overwhelmed, reach out. Talk to family, friends, a teacher or your school counsellor. Universities’ future student teams can help with practical options, and admissions centres can answer questions about your results. If you need someone to talk to about how you are feeling, Kids Helpline (1800 55 1800) supports young people up to age 25, and Lifeline (13 11 14) is available any time.

Results day glossary

Results day comes with its own vocabulary. Here are the terms you are most likely to meet.

ATAR (Australian Tertiary Admission Rank). A rank from 0.00 to 99.95 showing your position relative to your age group, used for selecting school leavers into university courses.

Admissions centre. The organisation that processes university applications and calculates ATARs in your state: UAC (NSW and ACT), VTAC (Victoria), QTAC (Queensland), SATAC (South Australia and the Northern Territory), TISC (Western Australia). In Tasmania, the University of Tasmania manages admissions.

Curriculum authority. The organisation that sets and certifies Year 11 and 12 results: NESA (NSW), the VCAA (Victoria), the QCAA (Queensland), the SACE Board (South Australia and the NT), SCSA (Western Australia), TASC (Tasmania) and the BSSS (ACT).

Subject results. Your marks, study scores, grades or awards in each subject, released by the curriculum authority.

Selection rank. Your ATAR plus any adjustment factors for a particular course. It is the number used to decide offers.

Adjustment factors. Points added to your ATAR for a particular course, for reasons such as subject results, where you live or educational disadvantage. Previously often called bonus points.

Change of preference. The period when you can add, remove or reorder the courses on your application, usually closing shortly after results are released.

Preference list. The ordered list of courses on your application. You receive an offer for the highest course on the list for which you are eligible in each round.

Offer round. A scheduled release of offers by an admissions centre. There are several rounds from December to February.

Main round. The largest offer round for Year 12 students, which differs by state: December Round 2 for UAC, January round 1 for VTAC (with a December round for current Year 12s), the 23 December round for QTAC, the 24 December main round for TISC and the mid-January round for SATAC.

Early offer. An offer made before final results, based on criteria such as school recommendations or Year 11 results. May be conditional.

Conditional offer. An offer that depends on meeting a condition, such as achieving a minimum ATAR or passing a particular subject.

Deferral. Accepting an offer and postponing the start of the course, usually for a year.

Tertiary Entrance Statement (TES). In South Australia, the Northern Territory and the ACT, the statement that records your ATAR and, in SA and the NT, your university aggregate.

Senior Education Profile (SEP). In Queensland, the set of documents issued by the QCAA at the end of Year 12, including the Senior Statement and, if eligible, the QCE.

Record of Achievement. In NSW, the document listing every HSC course you completed and your results. South Australia also uses the term for its official transcript of SACE results.

WASSA. The Western Australian Statement of Student Achievement, issued by SCSA.

University Admissions Advice Letter (UAAL). In Western Australia, a letter emailed to students after results summarising their university admission status.

IBAS (IB Admissions Score). The ATAR-equivalent rank UAC calculates for International Baccalaureate students in NSW and the ACT.

Notional ATAR. An ATAR-equivalent rank calculated for applicants with other qualifications, such as the IB in Victoria.

My eQuals. A digital credentials platform that VTAC uses to issue official digital ATAR statements.

PRAS. Victoria’s Post Results and ATAR Service, the main contact for questions immediately after results release.

LUI (Learner Unique Identifier). The student number Queensland students need to set up their QTAC ATAR account.

WASN. The Western Australian student number used to log in to SCSA’s student portal.

SRS (Schools Recommendation Scheme). UAC’s early offer scheme for NSW and ACT Year 12 students.

EAS and SEAS. UAC’s Educational Access Schemes and VTAC’s Special Entry Access Scheme, which can add adjustment factors for students who have experienced disadvantage.

STAT. The Special Tertiary Admissions Test, used in some cases, for example to meet English requirements in Western Australia.

Results day is when conditions are checked, so read the offer carefully.

What is the PRAS in Victoria?

The Post Results and ATAR Service (PRAS) is Victoria’s main contact point for questions immediately after VCE results and ATARs are released. It can be reached on 1800 653 080, and its opening hours around results release are published on the PRAS website.

What should I have ready before calling a help line on results day?

Have your student number, your admissions centre application number and any relevant documents ready, and write your question down in a sentence or two. Help lines are busiest in the first hours after release, so check whether the organisation’s website answers general questions first.

Is my ATAR the same as my school’s ranking?

No. Your ATAR is a statewide percentile rank against your whole age group, calculated by the admissions centre after scaling. It is not your rank within your school, and it is not affected by how many students at your school did well.

Why might my ATAR be different from my online estimate?

Online calculators use the latest published scaling and the marks you enter, but each year’s scaling depends on that year’s students, exam results can differ from school results, school marks may be moderated, and eligibility rules affect which subjects count. Treat an estimate as a range rather than a single number.

Can my ATAR change after it is released?

Only in unusual circumstances, such as a corrected subject result after a check, or completing additional subjects later to qualify for a new ATAR where your state allows it. Results checks rarely change results, so plan your preferences around the ATAR you receive.

Do I need to accept an offer straight away?

Not straight away, but before the response date shown on the offer. QTAC, for example, gives about a week for its 23 December offers. If you are unsure, use the time to ask the university questions, but do not let the deadline pass, or the place may go to someone else.

Can I accept one offer and still get a better one later?

Usually, yes. Accepting an offer does not stop you being considered for higher preferences in later rounds. VTAC, for example, says applicants who receive an offer are still automatically considered for higher preferences. Check your admissions centre’s rules about managing your list after an offer.

Should I contact universities on results day?

If you have questions about a course, pathway or adjustment factors, yes. Universities’ future student teams usually extend their hours and run information sessions in the days after results. Prepare your questions in advance and note the answers, so you can compare options before your deadline.

What happens if I miss my change of preference deadline?

Your existing preference list is used for that round. You can usually still change your preferences for later rounds once offers from that round are released. Missing the deadline is not the end of the process, but it can mean missing the first ATAR-based round for a course you added later.

Do schools find out students’ ATARs?

Schools typically receive information about their cohort’s results after release, and some publish cohort highlights such as the share of students above a particular ATAR. Your own ATAR is released directly to you, and you can choose whether to share it.

Can I get an ATAR if I did the VCE Vocational Major?

No. Students completing the VCE Vocational Major do not receive an ATAR. They can still pursue further study through pathways such as vocational courses and alternative entry schemes, and universities can explain entry options that do not require an ATAR.

When will 2027 ATAR release dates be announced?

Each admissions centre publishes its dates for the following cycle during the year. The 2027 dates are likely to follow a similar mid-to-late December pattern. Some 2027 dates are already available, such as Queensland’s Year 12 finishing date of 19 November 2027 in the QCAA calendar.

Can results from Year 11 count towards my ATAR?

It depends on the state and the subject. In NSW, the ATAR uses the best ten units of scaled marks regardless of the year each course was completed, so accelerated courses can count. In Queensland, the ATAR uses results from Units 3 and 4, accumulated within five years. Check your admissions centre’s rules.

When do students who repeated subjects get their new ATAR?

Students who repeat or add Year 12 subjects receive their results and any new ATAR on the normal results day for the year they complete them. In Queensland, students who already have an ATAR can qualify for a new one by completing at least three General subjects, or two General subjects plus one Applied subject or VET qualification.

What is the earliest ATAR release in Australia in 2026?

Victoria’s, at 7am AEDT on Thursday 10 December 2026. That is 6am in Brisbane, 6.30am in Adelaide, 5.30am in Darwin and 4am in Perth.

Is there anything I can do to speed up my results?

No. Results are released at a set time for everyone in your state, after marking, certification, scaling and checking are complete. The best thing you can do is make sure your logins work and your preference list is ready, so you can act quickly and calmly once results arrive.

Where can I find official 2026 key dates?

Use your admissions centre’s key dates page: UAC for NSW and the ACT, VTAC for Victoria, QTAC for Queensland, SATAC for South Australia and the NT, TISC for Western Australia and the University of Tasmania for Tasmania. Curriculum authorities such as NESA, the VCAA, the QCAA, the SACE Board, SCSA and TASC publish the subject results dates.
