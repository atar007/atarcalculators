ATAR Misconceptions Busted

The ATAR attracts more myths than almost any part of school. Some cost students real marks. This guide separates the myths from the facts on scaling, subject choice, school rankings and what the ATAR actually measures.

By the ATARCalculators Editorial Team ·Reviewed 11 November 2026 ·Updated November 2026 ·16 min read

The ATAR is a percentile rank, not a mark out of 100. It shows your position relative to your age group, not a percentage of correct answers. Most ATAR misconceptions come from confusing the rank with a score, or assuming that subject choice, school reputation or the number of units studied determines the result. In reality, the ATAR is calculated from your scaled results across a fixed set of courses, and how well you perform matters far more than what you choose or where you study.

Key takeaways

  • The ATAR is a rank, not a mark. An ATAR of 70.00 means top 30%, not 70%.
  • Your HSC/VCE/QCE marks and ATAR are different measures and should not be compared directly.
  • Scaling reflects the strength of the cohort in a subject, not the perceived difficulty of the subject.
  • Your school does not affect your ATAR. Only your individual results matter.
  • VET subjects can count toward an ATAR and do not disadvantage you.
  • Studying more units does not automatically improve your ATAR.
  • Bonus points change your selection rank, not your ATAR.
  • The ATAR is one of many criteria universities use, not the only one.

What the ATAR actually is

The ATAR sits between 0 and 99.95 and shows a student's position relative to their age-matched peers across the state. It is not a mark. A student with an ATAR of 70.00 has not achieved 70% in their exams; they have performed better than roughly 70 per cent of students in their age group. It is a percentile ranking, not a measure of exam correctness.

Because the ATAR is a ranking, families often wonder what the "average" is. Unlike a typical score where the midpoint is 50, the average ATAR usually sits around 70.00. This is because not every student in the state receives an ATAR. The group who does is generally undertaking an academic pathway, which naturally shifts the average higher.

The ATAR is reported in increments of 0.05, from 0.00 to 99.95. ATARs below 30.00 are reported as "30 or less". The ATAR is calculated by each state's tertiary admissions centre (UAC in NSW, VTAC in Victoria, QTAC in Queensland, SATAC in SA/NT, TISC in WA and UTAS in Tasmania) and is used by universities to compare applicants who have completed different combinations of courses.

If you want to understand how the ATAR is calculated in your state, see our guides to how ATAR is calculated in QLD, how ATAR is calculated in VIC and how ATAR is calculated in NSW. You can also use our state-by-state ATAR calculators to estimate your rank.

Myth 1: The ATAR is a mark out of 100

This is the most persistent ATAR misconception, and it causes the most confusion. The ATAR is a rank, not a mark. An ATAR of 80.00 does not mean a student scored 80% on their exams. It means they performed better than approximately 80 per cent of their age group.

VTAC is explicit about this: "The ATAR isn't a reflection of you as a person, and there's no pass or fail ATAR. Everyone who receives an ATAR has passed their senior certificate. The ATAR is simply a rank that is used by institutions for some of their courses." A student with an ATAR of 75.00 has achieved results above 75 per cent of the Year 12 age group, not a score of 75 out of 100.

The confusion comes from the fact that the ATAR is a two-decimal number on a 0–100 scale, which looks like a percentage. But the calculation behind it is a ranking of aggregates, not a percentage of correct answers. Once you understand that the ATAR is a position, not a score, the rest of the ATAR system starts to make sense.

Myth 2: My ATAR should be close to my HSC marks

This is one of the most common sources of disappointment on results day. Your HSC marks and your ATAR are different measures of achievement and should not be compared. HSC marks show your standard in each course. The ATAR shows your ranked position across the state.

UAC's ATAR expert Dr Helen Tam explains the difference clearly: "Your HSC marks (your performance) and ATAR (your position) are different measures of achievement and therefore should not be compared." However, she notes that if you are in the middle group of students in all your courses (with marks typically in the late 70s), you may receive an ATAR of around 70.00, which is also where the median ATAR usually lies.

Sometimes marks in the 70s can mean a much lower ATAR depending on your courses and your position in those courses. The ATAR is a ranking against the whole state cohort, and the distribution of marks and ranks is not linear. A student with marks in the 70s might be in the middle of the state cohort (ATAR around 70), or they might be further down if their courses were taken by a stronger cohort.

Myth 3: Difficult subjects scale better

Scaling is not based on how difficult a subject is. It is based on how strong the cohort taking that subject is. This is the second-most persistent ATAR misconception, and it leads thousands of students to choose subjects they are not good at in the belief that it will lift their ATAR.

UAC is explicit: "Courses studied by students who perform well in all their courses will be scaled highly. Courses such as Mathematics Extension 2 and Physics traditionally scale well because of this. However, that doesn't mean every student in that course will get a high scaled mark; you need to achieve high HSC marks to gain any benefit from scaling."

VTAC makes the same point: "Choosing a study that you are not very good at, or engaged in, simply because it may be scaled up would be a mistake." Scaling rewards performance, not subject choice. A student who scores 25 in a high-scaling subject may receive a lower ATAR contribution than a student who scores 40 in a subject that scales down.

The best advice is to choose subjects you are interested in and good at. Students consistently perform better in subjects they enjoy and engage with, and strong performance in any subject can lead to a high ATAR. UAC has reported students studying Drama achieving ATARs of 99.95. The subject's reputation matters far less than your performance in it.

Myth 4: There is a "best" combination of subjects for a high ATAR

There is no best combination of subjects. Just about any combination of courses can lead to a good ATAR; it all depends on how well you have done in all your courses compared with other students. UAC has explicitly debunked this myth: "Last year, for example, we had more than one student who studied Drama with ATARs of 99.95. These students didn't necessarily take the courses that were perceived to be harder or scale well, but they did well in Drama and everything else that they studied because those subjects suited their interests and abilities."

The idea that there is a "correct" combination of subjects (usually advanced maths, physics, chemistry and English) is one of the most damaging ATAR misconceptions. It leads students to choose subjects they dislike and perform poorly in, which lowers their ATAR rather than raising it. The best combination is the one that matches your strengths and interests, while still meeting any prerequisites for the university courses you are considering.

Prerequisites are the real constraint, not scaling. If you want to study Engineering, you may need Mathematics Methods or Specialist Mathematics. If you want to study Medicine, you may need Chemistry. These prerequisites are set by the universities, not by the scaling system, and they are the subjects you should prioritise in your course selection.

Myth 5: My school affects my ATAR

The school you attend does not feature in the ATAR calculation. This is one of the clearest and most important facts about the ATAR. UAC states it plainly: "No, the school you attend doesn't feature in the ATAR calculation. The ATAR calculation is based only on the exam and moderated school assessment marks for each of your courses, as provided by NESA; no other information is used."

Your ATAR is calculated from your individual results in your courses, moderated against the state cohort. The reputation of your school, the number of high achievers at your school, or the school's historical performance does not affect your individual ATAR. A student at a small regional school with strong results will receive the same ATAR as a student at a large metropolitan school with the same results.

This is why the ATAR is often described as a fair and standardised measure: it compares students on their individual achievement, not on the advantages or disadvantages of their school. The moderation process ensures that school-based assessment is comparable across schools, so a student is not advantaged or disadvantaged by the school they attend.

Myth 6: VET subjects hurt your ATAR

VET subjects do not disadvantage students in the ATAR. This is a common misconception, particularly among students who are considering a vocational pathway alongside their academic subjects. In fact, up to two VET units can count toward an ATAR depending on your state's rules.

In Queensland, a VET course at Certificate III level or higher can be used as the fifth subject in a four General plus one VET pattern. In other states, VET subjects may be included depending on the rules of the senior secondary certificate. VET subjects are scaled in the same way as other subjects, based on the strength of the cohort taking them, and they can make a positive contribution to your ATAR.

The misconception that VET subjects "don't count" or "drag down" an ATAR leads some students to avoid them, even when a VET subject would suit their interests and career goals. The reality is that VET subjects can be a valuable part of a balanced pattern of study, and they do not penalise students who choose them.

Myth 7: Studying more units automatically improves your ATAR

Studying more units does not automatically improve your ATAR. The ATAR is calculated from a fixed number of units or scaled results. In NSW, the ATAR uses the best 10 units (including 2 units of English). In Victoria, the ATAR uses the primary four plus up to two increments (10% of a fifth and sixth study). In Queensland, the ATAR uses the best five scaled subject results.

Studying more subjects can provide a buffer if one result is weaker, because the weakest result may be excluded from the calculation. But extra subjects also require time and energy, and they can spread your effort across too many courses. The ATAR is not a "more is better" system; it is a best-results system. Studying additional units is only beneficial if those units are among your best results, or if they meet a prerequisite for a course you want to study.

Myth 8: Bonus points change your ATAR

Bonus points do not change your ATAR. They change your selection rank. This is one of the most important distinctions in the ATAR system, and it is widely misunderstood.

The ATAR is your raw percentile rank. The selection rank is your ATAR plus any adjustment factors you are eligible for. Adjustment factors (formerly called bonus points) can be awarded for equity, location, subject performance or special circumstances. They are added to your ATAR to produce your selection rank, and it is the selection rank that universities use when comparing applicants for a course.

QTAC is explicit: "Adjustment factors are additional points that may be added to your ATAR or other eligible qualifications to calculate your QTAC selection rank for specific courses. Adjustment factors do not change your ATAR. Instead, they are used to calculate your selection rank." The University of Sydney states the same formula: Selection rank = ATAR + adjustment factors.

This means a student with an ATAR of 75.00 who is eligible for 5 adjustment points has a selection rank of 80.00 for the relevant course. Their ATAR remains 75.00; the selection rank is what universities use. Understanding this distinction is essential for interpreting course cut-offs, which are usually expressed in terms of selection rank, not raw ATAR. Myth 9: The ATAR is the only thing universities use

The ATAR is one of many criteria universities use to select students. This misconception is so widespread that it has been the subject of policy analysis. The Australian Government's review of higher education noted that there is "a general misconception that ATAR is the main tool used to determine eligibility for entry into higher education", when in fact the ATAR is the entry mechanism only for a little over 30 per cent of all higher education enrolments for commencing students.

Many courses require prerequisites (specific subjects at a certain level), portfolios, interviews or additional assessments. The ATAR is used to rank applicants for some courses, but for many courses it is only one component of the selection process. VTAC describes the ATAR as "only one tool used for selection into tertiary courses, and not all courses use the ATAR."

This means that students who do not achieve the ATAR they hoped for may still be eligible for courses that use other selection criteria, or for courses that have lower ATAR requirements but strong pathways into competitive degrees. The ATAR is important, but it is not the only gatekeeper.

Myth 10: The ATAR determines your future

The ATAR does not determine your future. It is a rank used for university selection, not a verdict on your worth, your intelligence or your potential. VTAC is explicit: "The ATAR isn't a reflection of you as a person, and there's no pass or fail ATAR. Everyone who receives an ATAR has passed their senior certificate."

There are many pathways to university and to careers. Students who do not achieve the ATAR they hoped for can enter university through TAFE diplomas, foundation programs, internal transfers after a year of study, or mature-age entry. The ATAR is a starting point, not a finish line, and many successful people received modest ATARs or did not receive an ATAR at all.

The pressure around the ATAR is real. A 2024 survey of over 400 Australian secondary students found that 64% of current Year 12s said they would not achieve an ATAR to protect their mental health, and 45% said they would not achieve an ATAR to avoid exam stress. Parents and students who frame the ATAR as one step in a longer journey, rather than a verdict, make a genuine difference to wellbeing.

Myth 11: Only the final exam counts

In most states, the final exam is not the only thing that counts. A large part of your result comes from assessments across the year, and in some subjects the internal work is the majority of your mark. In Queensland, for example, external assessment typically accounts for 25% of a student's final result in most subjects, and 50% in mathematics and science subjects. The remaining 50–75% comes from school-based assessment.

Students who coast through the year, planning to "cram for the exam", throw away marks they have already been offered. Every assessment through the year is a chance to build your result, and the internal assessments are often worth more than the final exam. Treat every task as if it counts, because in most states it does.

Myth 12: You should pick subjects purely for scaling

You should not pick subjects purely for scaling. Scaling only rewards how you rank within a subject, so a subject that scales well does nothing for you if you score poorly in it. Interest and ability matter far more than the scaling table.

Students who choose subjects they dislike and struggle in, chasing scaling, often end up with weaker results than if they had followed their strengths. Pick subjects you can do well in and stay motivated for — that is what lifts your rank. VTAC's guidance is clear: "Choosing a study that you are not very good at, or engaged in, simply because it may be scaled up would be a mistake."

The facts about scaling

Scaling is the process that adjusts subject results so that subjects of different difficulty and different student cohorts can be compared fairly. It is not a bonus system, and it does not reward students for choosing "hard" subjects. Here is what actually happens.

Scaling adjusts for the strength of the cohort. Each state's admissions centre looks at how the students in each subject perform across all their subjects. If a subject is taken largely by students who do well everywhere, that subject will scale up. If a subject is taken by a broader cohort with a wider range of achievement, it may scale down.

Scaling rewards performance, not subject choice. A student who scores 40 in a subject that scales down will generally receive a higher ATAR contribution than a student who scores 25 in a subject that scales up. The scaling process is designed to ensure that students are neither advantaged nor disadvantaged by their subject choices.

Scaling is done afresh each year. There is no guarantee that a subject that scaled well last year will scale the same way this year. The strength of the cohort changes from year to year, and so does the scaling. Choosing subjects based on last year's scaling data is a mistake.

Scaling does not change your rank within a subject. If you are the top student in your subject, you will receive a high scaled score regardless of whether the subject scales up or down. Scaling changes how your result counts towards your ATAR, not how you performed within the subject.

What to do instead

If you are choosing subjects for Year 11 and 12, or if you are trying to understand your ATAR, here is what the evidence and the admissions centres recommend.

Choose subjects you are good at and interested in. Students perform better in subjects they enjoy and engage with. Strong performance in any subject can lead to a high ATAR, and choosing a subject only because it is believed to scale well can work against you.

Check the prerequisites for the courses you are considering. Prerequisites are the real constraint on subject choice, not scaling. If you want to study Engineering, you may need Mathematics Methods; if you want to study Medicine, you may need Chemistry. Check the requirements for the courses you are interested in before you finalise your subject selection.

Understand the difference between ATAR and selection rank. Your ATAR is your raw rank; your selection rank is your ATAR plus adjustment factors. Universities use the selection rank to make offers, and it is the selection rank that is compared against a course's cut-off.

Do not compare your ATAR to your HSC marks. They are different measures. HSC marks show your standard in each course; the ATAR shows your ranked position across the state. The two numbers will not be the same, and that is expected.

Remember that the ATAR is one pathway, not the only pathway. There are many routes to university and to careers, including TAFE, foundation programs, internal transfers and mature-age entry. The ATAR is important, but it is not the only factor in your future.

Common questions

Is the ATAR a mark out of 100?

No. The ATAR is a percentile rank between 0.00 and 99.95. An ATAR of 80.00 means you performed better than approximately 80 per cent of your age group, not that you scored 80 per cent on your exams.

Should my ATAR be close to my HSC marks?

No. HSC marks and the ATAR are different measures of achievement and should not be compared. HSC marks show your standard in each course; the ATAR shows your ranked position across the state. A student in the middle group of their courses with marks in the late 70s may receive an ATAR of around 70.00.

Do hard subjects scale higher?

Not automatically. A subject scales up because its students are strong across all their subjects, not because the content is difficult. A subject with a mixed cohort can scale modestly. A strong result in a subject you are good at almost always beats a weak result in a high-scaling one.

Does my school's reputation change my ATAR?

No. The school you attend does not feature in the ATAR calculation. Your ATAR is calculated only from your exam marks and moderated school assessment marks, as provided by your state's assessment authority. Your school affects teaching and support, not the rank itself.

Is the ATAR the only way into university?

No. The ATAR is the main path straight from Year 12, but universities also offer diplomas, TAFE pathways, enabling and foundation programs, portfolios, auditions and special entry schemes. Many students enter university through these alternative routes.

Does dropping a subject improve your ATAR?

Usually not. Your ATAR uses your best results, so a weak subject often counts for little already. Dropping it rarely lifts your rank, and it can leave you with no safety net if another subject goes badly. Keeping an extra subject you can do reasonably well in is often the smarter choice.

Does only the final exam count towards my ATAR?

No. In most states a large part of your result comes from assessments across the year, and in some subjects the internal work is the majority of your mark. Every assessment counts, not just the final exam.

Should I choose subjects only for scaling?

No. Scaling only rewards your rank within a subject, so a high-scaling subject helps nothing if you score poorly in it. Choose subjects you can do well in and stay motivated for. Strong performance in any subject can lead to a high ATAR.

Do VET subjects hurt your ATAR?

No. Up to two VET units can count toward an ATAR depending on your state's rules. In Queensland, a VET course at Certificate III level or higher can be used as the fifth subject in a four General plus one VET pattern. VET subjects do not disadvantage students.

Does studying more units improve your ATAR?

Not automatically. The ATAR is calculated from a fixed number of units or scaled results, such as the best 10 units in NSW or the best five subjects in Queensland. Extra subjects can provide a buffer if one result is weaker, but they also spread your time and energy.

What is the difference between ATAR and selection rank?

The ATAR is your raw percentile rank. The selection rank is your ATAR plus any adjustment factors you are eligible for, such as equity adjustments or subject bonuses. Universities use the selection rank when comparing applicants for a course, and the selection rank can be higher than the ATAR.

Does the ATAR determine my future?

No. The ATAR is a rank used for university selection, not a verdict on your worth or potential. There are many pathways to university and to careers, including TAFE, foundation programs, internal transfers and mature-age entry.
