How is ATAR calculated in QLD?

Your Queensland ATAR is a rank, not a mark. This guide shows how your QCE results turn into an ATAR, step by step, in plain English. No jargon.

In Queensland, your ATAR is worked out by QTAC from your scaled QCE subject results. QTAC scales each subject result, takes your best five, and ranks that total against your age group to give an ATAR from 0.00 to 99.95. QCAA sets the subjects and runs the assessment; QTAC turns those results into your ATAR. Queensland moved from the OP system to the ATAR in 2020.

Key takeaways

  • Your QLD ATAR is a rank from 0.00 to 99.95, not an average of your results.
  • It is built from your best five scaled subject results.
  • You must complete an English subject to be eligible, though it need not be in your best five.
  • QTAC scales your results and works out your ATAR. QCAA sets and runs the assessment.

A rank, not a mark

The most important thing to know is that your ATAR is a rank. An ATAR of 85 does not mean you scored 85%. It means you finished ahead of about 85% of your age group.

Because it is a rank, it lets universities compare students who took completely different subjects. That is the whole point of the ATAR.

What the QCE is

The QCE is the Queensland Certificate of Education, the Year 12 qualification in Queensland. Subjects are set and assessed by QCAA, the Queensland Curriculum and Assessment Authority.

You finish with a result in each subject. Those results are the raw material for your ATAR, but they are not your ATAR. That comes next.

How your subject result is built

For a General subject, most of your result comes from internal assessments across the year, set at your school and confirmed by QCAA. The rest comes from an external exam, written and marked by QCAA.

For most subjects the external exam is worth about a quarter of your result. For maths and science subjects it is worth half. So your work across the year carries real weight, long before the exam.

How QTAC scaling works

Once you have your subject results, QTAC scales them. Scaling adjusts each subject so that the same result means the same thing across subjects.

A subject scales up when the students taking it are strong across all their subjects. It scales down when the group is broader. Your position within the subject never changes — scaling only changes how the result counts towards your ATAR.

Your best five results form your ATAR

QTAC takes your best five scaled results and combines them into a Tertiary Entrance Aggregate. The eligible inputs are your best five General subjects, or your best four General subjects plus one Applied subject, or your best four General subjects plus a completed VET qualification at Certificate III or above.

Your strongest five do all the work. Anything beyond five does not count, so a spare subject is a useful safety net rather than a boost.

The English requirement

To be eligible for an ATAR in Queensland, you must satisfactorily complete an eligible English subject, which means a grade of C or better in one of English, Literature, English as an Additional Language, English and Literature Extension, or Essential English. This applies to everyone, and only a General English subject or Essential English can be counted, not both.

However, your English result only counts towards your ATAR if it is among your best five. So English is required for eligibility, but it does not have to be one of your top five results.

ATAR eligibility in Queensland

An ATAR in Queensland is not automatic; you have to meet specific eligibility rules set by QTAC on behalf of the Queensland universities. Understanding them early shapes your subject choices, because a gap in eligibility can stop an ATAR that your results alone would have produced. There are two parts: a valid combination of subjects for the aggregate, and the English requirement that sits over the top.

The eligible subject combinations

Your Tertiary Entrance Aggregate is built from one of three combinations: your best five scaled General subject results; your best four scaled General subjects plus one Applied subject; or your best four scaled General subjects plus a completed VET qualification at Certificate III or above. In every case the aggregate rests on at least four General subjects, with the fifth input being another General subject, an Applied subject, or a VET qualification. This is why most ATAR-track students study a full set of General subjects, usually six, so their best five have room to be strong.

The English requirement in full

To be eligible for an ATAR you must satisfactorily complete an eligible English subject, meaning a grade of C or better in one of English, Literature, English as an Additional Language, English and Literature Extension, or Essential English. The requirement applies to every ATAR-eligible student. Note the rule that a General English subject and Essential English cannot both be counted toward the aggregate; you choose one path. And English only counts toward your ATAR if it is among your best five scaled results, so it is an eligibility gate rather than a guaranteed contributor to the aggregate.

English is an eligibility gate. Even a student with five strong non-English subjects is not ATAR-eligible without a C or better in an eligible English subject. Make sure your English result clears the bar.

Precluded subjects and combinations

QTAC applies precluded-subject rules so that closely overlapping subjects do not both count at full value toward the aggregate. The clearest example is English: only a General English subject or Essential English may be included, not both. Similar rules apply to some closely related subject pairs. The practical effect is the same as elsewhere: check the current precluded list before finalising your subjects, so your load gives QTAC the full set of countable results you expect rather than two overlapping subjects that collapse into one.

A worked example

Say you take English, Maths Methods, Biology, Business and Physical Education. QTAC scales each result and takes your best five.

It combines them into a total, then compares that total with every other student’s. If it sits higher than 88% of them, your ATAR is about 88.00. Your strongest results do the heavy lifting.

Who does what in Queensland

Two bodies are involved, and it helps to keep them straight. QCAA runs the QCE: it sets the subjects, runs the assessment, and gives you your results. QTAC takes those results, scales them, and works out your ATAR.

So QCAA gives you your QCE. QTAC gives you your ATAR. We cover QTAC in detail in QTAC explained.

From OP to ATAR

Queensland used to use the OP, or Overall Position, a band from 1 to 25. It moved to the ATAR in 2020, bringing it into line with the rest of the country.

So if an older sibling talks about their OP, that system is gone. Every Queensland student now receives an ATAR. See ATAR vs OP vs ENTER for the history.

Adjustment factors and your selection rank

The ATAR QTAC calculates is not always the figure a course compares against its cut-off. For selection, universities often use a selection rank, which is your ATAR plus any adjustment factors you qualify for. Adjustment factors are applied after the ATAR is calculated, so they never change your ATAR itself; they lift the rank a course sees. Two students with the same ATAR can therefore have different selection ranks for the same course, and a published course ATAR is really a selection-rank threshold.

Educational access schemes

The main adjustment route is the Educational Access Scheme, which recognises circumstances that may have affected your study, such as financial hardship, illness, or disruption. Eligible students can receive adjustment factors that raise their selection rank for participating courses. Some adjustments are applied automatically from information QTAC holds; others need an application with supporting evidence by a deadline. If you think your circumstances may qualify, check the scheme early, because the deadlines can fall around results time.

Subject and other adjustments

Some universities award adjustment factors for studying particular subjects relevant to a course, such as a specified mathematics or science subject, or for other schemes they run. These subject adjustments are set by each university and vary between them, so the lift available for a given subject at one university may differ at another. Because adjustments are capped, they improve your selection rank within a limit rather than without bound.

Adjustments lift the rank, not the ATAR. Your ATAR is fixed once calculated. Adjustment factors are added on top to form the selection rank a course uses, so check each university’s schemes to see what you may gain.

Pathways into university without an ATAR

About half of Queensland’s Year 12 students receive an ATAR; the rest complete the QCE through other pathways, and many still go on to university. The ATAR is the best-known route, but Queensland universities run a range of alternative pathways for students who do not have a competitive ATAR, or none at all. Knowing they exist changes how a borderline or missing rank feels, because it turns a single result into one option among several.

Tertiary preparation and enabling programs

Tertiary preparation programs and enabling courses build university-level skills and, on successful completion, provide a basis for admission. Queensland universities and their colleges run these programs, often free or low-cost, for students who want a supported start. For a capable student whose QCE results do not reflect their ability, an enabling program can be a stronger beginning than scraping into a course on a bare rank.

VET and diploma pathways

A completed vocational qualification can open a university door. A diploma can provide a basis for admission and sometimes credit toward a related degree, and a Certificate III or IV can support entry to some courses. Starting in vocational study and moving into a degree is a well-worn Queensland route, assessed on the vocational results rather than on a school ATAR.

The STAT and mature-age entry

The Special Tertiary Admissions Test, or STAT, assesses general reasoning and comprehension rather than subject knowledge, and many Queensland courses accept it as a basis for admission for non-school-leaver applicants. For mature students, the STAT, previous study, work experience and enabling programs together provide routes that do not depend on a recent QCE result. An ATAR does not expire, so an older rank can still count too.

Pathways are normal, not a fallback of last resort. With only about half of Year 12 students receiving an ATAR, pathways carry large numbers into Queensland degrees each year. If the direct route falls short, ask the university which pathway leads into your target course.

The structure of a General subject

Most students building an ATAR take General subjects. Each one is assessed in four parts: three internal assessments across the year, set at your school, and one external exam at the end, set by QCAA.

So your result in a General subject is built mostly across the year, with the exam as the final piece. This means the work you do in Year 12, long before the exam, carries real weight towards your result.

How internal assessment is quality-assured

Queensland does not rank-moderate internal marks the way some states do. Instead, your school marks your internal assessments against QCAA’s published standards, and QCAA reviews and confirms that marking to keep it consistent across the state.

So your internal result reflects your work against a set standard, not just your position in your class. Strong, consistent internal assessment is one of the most reliable ways to build a good subject result.

The external exam and its weighting

Each General subject ends with an external exam, written and marked by QCAA. For most subjects it counts for about a quarter of your result. For maths and science subjects, it counts for half.

So exam preparation matters, and it matters most in maths and science, where the exam carries more weight. Past papers under timed conditions are the most reliable way to get ready.

Applied subjects and VET in your ATAR

Your best five results do not all have to be General subjects. Within set limits, you can include one Applied subject or a completed VET qualification among your five.

This gives you flexibility. A student who is strong in an applied or vocational area can have that count towards their ATAR, alongside their General subjects. The rules cap how many non-General results can count, so check the details for your combination.

Subject results are not your ATAR

Students often confuse a subject result with the ATAR. They are different. A subject result describes your performance in one subject. Your ATAR is a statewide rank, from 0.00 to 99.95, built from your best five scaled results.

You can have solid subject results and an ATAR that surprises you, because the ATAR depends on scaling and on how everyone else did. Subject results describe subjects; the ATAR ranks students.

Scaling in numbers

Scaling can move a result up or down. A result in a subject with a strong cohort might scale up, while the same result in a broad-entry subject might scale down. QTAC does this so every subject can be compared fairly.

This is why two students with the same raw results can end up with different ATARs. What matters is not just your result, but how the subject scales and how you performed within it.

Why you cannot calculate it exactly yourself

There is no formula you can run at home for your exact ATAR. It depends on how every other student in Queensland performs, and on the exact scaling for that year. Both are only known once all results are finalised.

This is why calculators give an estimate: they apply scaling to your results, which is close but not the final figure. See how accurate ATAR calculators are.

How many subjects should you take?

Your ATAR is built from your best five scaled results, so you need at least five eligible subjects, including an English subject for eligibility. Most students take six, which is a smart safety net.

An extra subject means one poor result can drop out of your best five without sinking your ATAR. It costs some effort across the year, but the protection it gives your rank is usually worth it.

From OP to ATAR: what changed

The switch changed how finely students are ranked. The OP had just 25 bands, while the ATAR runs from 0.00 to 99.95 in small steps, so it separates students far more precisely than the old system did.

For you, this means small differences in your results can matter more than they once would have. There is also no exact one-to-one conversion between an old OP and an ATAR, so if you see an OP-to-ATAR table, treat it as a rough guide only. See ATAR vs OP vs ENTER for the full story.

Inter-subject scaling, explained properly

The step most Queensland students find hardest to picture is inter-subject scaling, so it is worth slowing down. When QCAA sends QTAC your raw subject results, those results are not yet comparable across subjects, because the same raw score in two subjects can represent very different levels of achievement relative to the students who took each one. Inter-subject scaling is the process QTAC uses to convert raw results into scaled results, expressed on a 100-point scale to two decimal places, so that a scaled score in one subject means the same as a scaled score in another.

The logic is comparative. A subject scales up when the students taking it are strong across all their subjects, and it scales down when the cohort is broader. Importantly, scaling never changes your position within your own subject; if you were the top student in a subject, you remain the top student after scaling. What scaling changes is how your result counts toward your aggregate relative to other subjects. QTAC states plainly that inter-subject scaling will neither enhance nor diminish a student’s performance in their subjects; it only makes results comparable.

Your rank within a subject is untouched. Scaling moves the whole subject on the common scale; it does not reorder students within a subject. A strong result stays a strong result.

Why scaling is iterative

Scaling in Queensland is not a single pass but an iterative calculation overseen by a technical committee each year. The process looks at how the students in each subject performed across all their subjects, adjusts the subjects accordingly, then repeats until the figures stabilise. This is why the exact scaling for a year is not known until after results, and why QTAC publishes a detailed ATAR report each year rather than a fixed scaling table in advance. Any pre-results estimate of a subject’s scaling is informed guesswork based on past reports.

For students, the practical takeaway is simple: you cannot reliably predict a subject’s scaling before results, so you should not choose subjects on scaling alone. The published reports show past patterns, which a calculator can use to estimate, but the current year’s figures emerge only once the whole cohort’s results are in.

The QTAC calculation, step by step

It helps to see the whole calculation as an ordered sequence. First, QCAA adds together your Units 3 and 4 results at the completion of each subject to form your overall raw subject result. Second, QCAA provides those raw results, and any completed VET qualifications, to QTAC. Third, QTAC runs the inter-subject scaling process, converting raw results into scaled results on the common 100-point scale. Fourth, QTAC adds your best five scaled results, from the eligible inputs, into a Tertiary Entrance Aggregate. Fifth, students are placed in descending order of merit by aggregate, and sixth, they are assigned to ATAR bands, with the top students receiving 99.95 and the scale stepping down in intervals of 0.05.

From aggregate to ATAR

The Tertiary Entrance Aggregate is not itself the ATAR; it is the score QTAC ranks. Because the ATAR is a rank against the whole Queensland Year 12 age cohort, the same aggregate can map to a slightly different ATAR from year to year depending on how all students performed. QTAC assigns students to 0.05-wide bands across the range, so an ATAR reports your position in the cohort rather than your aggregate directly. The direction is constant, though: a higher aggregate always ranks ahead of a lower one in the same year.

Internal assessment and the external exam

For a General subject in Queensland, your overall result is built across Units 3 and 4, the two summative units of Year 12, and combines internal assessment with an external examination. Most General subjects have three internal assessments plus one external exam, with the external exam typically worth about a quarter of the result. For many mathematics and science subjects the external exam is worth half, reflecting the nature of those subjects. The internal assessments are set at your school and confirmed through QCAA’s processes, while the external exam is written and marked by QCAA.

The balance matters for how you work. Because internal assessment carries most of the weight in most subjects, consistent performance through the year builds your result long before the exam. In the mathematics and science subjects where the exam is worth half, strong exam preparation becomes correspondingly more important. Either way, both halves feed the raw subject result that QCAA sends to QTAC for scaling.

How internal assessment is kept fair

A common worry is that internal assessment set at different schools cannot be compared fairly. QCAA addresses this through quality-assurance processes, including confirmation of internal assessment marks, so that an internal result at one school means the same as the equivalent result at another. This happens before QTAC scales anything, so the raw subject results QTAC receives are already quality-assured across schools. Scaling then handles comparability across subjects, a separate problem from comparability across schools.

Two different fairness problems. QCAA’s confirmation keeps assessment comparable across schools; QTAC’s scaling keeps results comparable across subjects. Both are needed, and they happen at different stages.

How Applied subjects and VET count

Queensland’s system deliberately lets an Applied subject or a completed VET qualification stand in for one General subject in the aggregate, so that students on more vocational pathways are not shut out of an ATAR. The rule is that at least four of your five aggregate inputs must be General subjects; the fifth can be an Applied subject or a completed VET qualification at Certificate III or above. Applied subjects and VET qualifications are brought onto the common scale so they can be compared with General subjects, within the limits QTAC sets.

This matters for subject planning. A student combining strong General subjects with a Certificate III can still earn an ATAR, with the VET qualification counting as the fifth input if it is among their best results after scaling. But because only one non-General input can count, a student cannot build an aggregate mostly from Applied subjects or VET; the core must be General subjects. If you are combining a vocational qualification with your QCE and hoping it lifts your ATAR, check in advance how it will count and be scaled.

One non-General input, not more. Your aggregate rests on at least four General subjects. An Applied subject or a Certificate III or above can be the fifth, but it cannot replace the General core.

Numeric values behind VET and Applied results

To bring Applied subjects and VET onto the scale, QTAC assigns numeric equivalents to letter grades before scaling, commonly treating an A as 90, a B as 70, a C as 50, a D as 30 and an E as 10. These numeric equivalents give a starting point that scaling then adjusts for comparability. The detail is technical, but the implication is practical: a strong letter grade in an Applied subject or a solid VET result carries a meaningful value into the aggregate, provided it is among your best inputs.

From OP to ATAR: what changed

Until 2019, Queensland ranked students with the Overall Position, or OP, a band from 1 to 25, where 1 was the highest. The OP was underpinned by the Queensland Core Skills Test, a common test all OP-eligible students sat, which was used to scale school results. In 2020 Queensland issued its first ATARs, bringing the state into line with the rest of the country and replacing the OP’s 25 bands with the finer ATAR scale from 0.00 to 99.95 in steps of 0.05. The Queensland Core Skills Test was discontinued, and inter-subject scaling took over the role of making results comparable.

The change was more than cosmetic. The ATAR is a far finer rank than the OP’s 25 bands, and it is nationally comparable, so a Queensland student’s rank now means the same as the same rank interstate. For families who remember the OP, the key mental switch is that a low number was good under the OP but a high number is good under the ATAR, and that there is no simple one-to-one conversion between the two systems.

Why the national rank matters

Moving to the ATAR means a Queensland student can apply interstate using the same rank, without translating an OP into another system. A Queensland ATAR of 90 means the same standing as a Victorian or New South Wales ATAR of 90, even though each state builds its aggregate differently. For students, the upshot is that you need to understand Queensland’s method, because that is how your own ATAR is built, and to know that the result travels nationally once QTAC has calculated it.

QTAC compared with the other admissions centres

Every state and territory has a tertiary admissions centre, and they work in much the same way: collect applications, apply their system’s scaling and aggregate rules, calculate the ATAR, and run offer rounds. QTAC covers Queensland. New South Wales and the ACT use UAC, Victoria uses VTAC, South Australia and the Northern Territory share SATAC, and Western Australia uses TISC. The ATAR itself is national, so a rank calculated by QTAC means the same as the same rank from any other centre.

What differs is the machinery. Each centre applies its own certificate, subjects, aggregate rules and scaling. Queensland uses the QCE, a best-five aggregate with the option of one Applied or VET input, and inter-subject scaling. Western Australia, by contrast, uses a best-four aggregate with subject bonuses. The end result in every case is a position on the one national ATAR scale, built by different rules in each state.

Applying across state borders

If you want to study interstate, you generally apply through the admissions centre for the state where the university sits, using your QTAC-calculated ATAR as your rank. Many students apply through QTAC for Queensland courses and through another centre for courses elsewhere, running two separate applications. Each centre has its own fees, deadlines and preference rules, so treat them as independent processes and check both calendars.

Because the ATAR is nationally comparable, you do not need a different rank for each state; the same ATAR travels with you. What you do need is to meet each course’s own prerequisites and any state-specific requirements, which can differ even for similar degrees.

A short guide for parents and carers

If you are supporting a student through Year 12 in Queensland, a few points make the system easier to follow. QCAA runs the QCE: it sets the subjects, runs the assessment and reports results. QTAC takes those results, scales them, and calculates the ATAR and runs university applications. The two work together, but the ATAR and the application process are QTAC’s part, and queries about a subject result go to QCAA.

The most useful thing to understand is that the ATAR is a rank, not a mark. It reports where a student sits relative to their age group, not a percentage of questions answered correctly. A student can work hard, earn solid subject results, and still receive an ATAR that looks lower than those results because of how ranking, scaling and the best-five rule work. That is normal and is not a sign of failure.

It also helps to know that the ATAR is one route among many. With only about half of Year 12 students receiving an ATAR, pathways such as tertiary preparation programs, VET and the STAT carry large numbers into Queensland degrees. Encouraging a student to list a realistic spread of preferences, and to know the pathways exist, takes pressure off results day.

Where to turn. QTAC can answer questions about applications, offers and the ATAR; QCAA about the QCE and subject results; and the school’s careers adviser about subject choices and pathways. You do not have to work it out alone.

A rough timeline through Year 12

Through the year, students complete internal assessments across Units 3 and 4 and sit external exams. Applications through QTAC open well before results, so students register preferences while finishing the year, and some receive early offers. In mid-December QCAA releases subject results and QTAC releases ATARs; offer rounds follow in December and January. Exact dates shift, so check QTAC’s published key dates rather than last year’s calendar.

Common myths about the Queensland system

Several beliefs about the Queensland system mislead students every year. The first is that the ATAR is a mark out of 100. It is not; it is a rank. An ATAR of 85 means you are ranked near the top 15 per cent of your age group, not that you earned 85 per cent. Mistaking the rank for a percentage leads students to misread both their own result and a course’s cut-off.

The second myth is that all your subjects count. In Queensland only your best five scaled results form your aggregate, so a weak sixth subject can simply drop out. The third myth is that you should chase subjects that scale up; scaling rewards a strong result, and a strong result is easier in a subject that suits you, so chasing a high-scaling subject you struggle in usually backfires.

The fourth myth is that the ATAR is the only way into a Queensland university. With about half of Year 12 students not receiving an ATAR, pathways such as tertiary preparation programs, VET and the STAT carry many into the same degrees. Treating the ATAR as the single door creates needless pressure and hides the routes that help when a rank falls short.

Rank, not mark. If you remember one thing about the ATAR, make it this: it reports where you sit relative to your age group, which is why it can look different from your subject results.

Common mistakes to avoid

A few errors come up every year, and all are avoidable. Treating an estimate as a result is the first: a calculator gives a range built on assumptions about scaling that QTAC has not published, so plan around a band, not a number. Listing too few preferences is the second: a student with only a dream course and nothing below it has no safety net if the cut-off rises. Missing the English requirement or a prerequisite is the third: neither is rescued by a high ATAR, so check both early. And missing a QTAC deadline, especially a preference-change deadline before an offer round, is the one error that can cost a place outright.

A checklist before results day

A short checklist keeps the process under control. Before results, make sure your QTAC application is lodged with a realistic spread of preferences, first choice first. Check that you have at least four strong General subjects plus a valid fifth input, a C or better in an eligible English subject, the prerequisites for your target courses, and no surprise precluded combinations. Note the key dates for results, offers and preference deadlines. Know which pathways apply to your preferred courses in case your ATAR falls short. With those in place, results day is about reading an outcome, not scrambling to react to one.

Putting it all together

QTAC’s job is to turn your QCE results into a fair, nationally comparable rank and, with the universities, to run the application and offer process for Queensland. It applies inter-subject scaling so subjects can be compared, adds your best five scaled results into a Tertiary Entrance Aggregate, ranks that aggregate against the Year 12 cohort to assign your ATAR, and the universities add any adjustment factors to form your selection rank. Understanding each step turns a confusing process into a sequence you can plan for.

The practical takeaways are simple. Choose subjects you can do well in, keep at least four strong General subjects plus a valid fifth input, meet the English requirement with a C or better in an eligible subject, and watch for precluded combinations. Check your target courses’ prerequisites early. Lodge your QTAC application on time with a realistic spread of preferences, first choice first, and revise it when your real ATAR arrives. And remember the pathways, so a disappointing ATAR is a detour rather than a dead end.

Where to start now

Begin with your target courses. List the ones you are seriously considering, check their prerequisites and recent cut-offs, and confirm your subjects satisfy them and that you are on track for a C or better in an eligible English subject. Then note QTAC’s key dates for applications, preference changes, results and offers. Draft a preference list that spreads across your likely ATAR range, with your genuine first choice at the top. Identify the pathways into your target courses in case your rank falls short. With those in place, the rest of the process unfolds in a predictable order, and results day becomes a step you have prepared for rather than a moment you dread.

A full worked example, start to finish

Imagine a student who takes six General subjects and sits all their external exams. QCAA combines each subject’s Units 3 and 4 results into a raw subject result and sends six raw results to QTAC. QTAC runs inter-subject scaling, producing six scaled results on the 100-point scale. Suppose the scaled results come out as 82.40, 78.15, 75.60, 71.30, 68.90 and 55.20. QTAC takes the best five, which are everything except the 55.20, and adds them into a Tertiary Entrance Aggregate. The weakest subject simply drops out, because the student had a sixth subject to spare.

That aggregate is then ranked against every other Queensland Year 12 student’s aggregate, and the student is assigned an ATAR band accordingly. The exact ATAR depends on how the whole cohort performed, which is why the same aggregate can map to a slightly different ATAR in different years. If the student later qualifies for adjustment factors through the Educational Access Scheme or a subject adjustment, those are added to the ATAR to form a selection rank, which is what each course compares against its cut-off.

The example shows the system’s logic in one picture: scale for comparability, take the best five, rank against the cohort, and add adjustments at the end. Each step has a clear purpose, and none of them is a mystery once you see where it sits in the sequence.

The spare subject earned its place. Because the student took six subjects, the weakest scaled result dropped out of the best five. That is exactly why a broad load protects you against one underperforming subject.

What this means for choosing subjects

Put together, the process points to a simple strategy. Take an English you can do well in, since it is required for eligibility. Add subjects you are strong in, because your best five carry your ATAR. Include a high-scaling subject only if you can score well in it.

Then take a sixth subject as a buffer, so one weak result can drop out. Do that, and the machinery of scaling and your best five works in your favour rather than against you.

QTAC scales each subject based on how strong the students taking it are across all their subjects. A strong cohort scales a subject up; a broader one scales it down. Your position within the subject never changes.

QCAA sends your scaled-ready Units 3 and 4 results to QTAC, which runs inter-subject scaling, adds your best five scaled results into an aggregate, and ranks that aggregate into an ATAR from 0.00 to 99.95.

What is the best five rule?

Your ATAR is built from your best five scaled results: five General subjects, or four General plus one Applied subject, or four General plus a completed VET qualification at Certificate III or above.

Does every subject count toward my ATAR?

No. Only your best five scaled results count. Anything beyond five does not add to your aggregate, so a sixth subject is a safety net rather than a boost.

What is inter-subject scaling?

The process QTAC uses to convert raw subject results into scaled results on a common 100-point scale, so results in different subjects can be compared fairly. It does not change your rank within a subject.

Why is scaling needed?

Because the same raw result in two subjects can represent different levels of achievement relative to each subject’s students. Scaling puts subjects on a common footing so your rank does not depend on which subjects you chose.

Does scaling change my position in a subject?

No. Scaling moves the whole subject on the common scale but never reorders students within a subject. If you were top of your subject, you stay top after scaling.

Can I predict my scaling?

Only roughly. QTAC does not publish the current year’s scaling before results, and the process is iterative, so any estimate is informed guesswork based on past ATAR reports.

Is the ATAR a mark out of 100?

No. It is a rank. An ATAR of 85 means you finished ahead of about 85 per cent of your Queensland Year 12 age cohort, not that you scored 85 per cent.

The highest ATAR is 99.95, reported in steps of 0.05. In recent years about 37 Queensland students have achieved the maximum each year.

There is no exact one-to-one conversion, because the OP’s 25 bands and the ATAR’s fine scale rank differently. The ATAR is a far more granular, nationally comparable rank.

No. Many rank applicants on selection rank alone. Others, especially in science, engineering and health, have specific prerequisites. Check each course.

How do I plan subjects for prerequisites?

Work backward from your target courses: list their prerequisites, then choose Year 11 and 12 subjects that satisfy them. Doing this early avoids discovering a gap after results.

Can I apply with incomplete prerequisites?

You can apply, but you may need to complete the prerequisite before enrolling, through a bridging option or later study. Ask the university how it handles this.

Does QTAC check my eligibility?

QTAC calculates the ATAR for students who meet the eligibility requirements and publishes course prerequisites, which the universities apply. It is your responsibility to meet the requirements, so check early.

What if my course is very competitive?

List it first anyway, add realistic options below it, and consider pathways such as transfer or a related course if the cut-off is beyond your likely rank. You lose nothing by ranking it first.

Can I reapply next year if I miss out?

Yes. You can apply again in a later cycle, often with improved credentials such as a STAT result, bridging study or a pathway. An ATAR does not expire, so it can still count.

Does missing out this year hurt next year?

No. A fresh application is assessed on its merits. Many students enter university a year or more after Year 12 through pathways, without disadvantage to their later study.

Who sets the course cut-offs each year?

The universities set them in light of demand and places, and QTAC publishes and applies them during the offer rounds. They are not fixed and can move year to year.

Can I compare two courses’ cut-offs?

Yes. Look at each course’s most recent cut-off and remember it can shift. Use it as a guide, place your preferences across your likely range, and rank by what you want most.

Where can I get tailored advice on cut-offs?

QTAC’s enquiries team, the universities’ admissions staff and your school’s careers adviser can advise on cut-offs and your chances, which is more reliable than general rules of thumb.
