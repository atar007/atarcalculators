UTAS explained

Tasmania works a little differently from other states: it has no separate tertiary admissions centre. Instead, the University of Tasmania, UTAS, is the body Tasmanian students go through for their ATAR and university place. Here is how it works.

What is UTAS?

UTAS stands for the University of Tasmania. It is the state’s university, and because Tasmania does not run a separate tertiary admissions centre, UTAS also plays the role that a body like UAC or VTAC plays in other states.

So in Tasmania, the university and the admissions process are closely linked. When Tasmanian students ask how they get their ATAR and their university place, UTAS is central to both answers.

This is a genuine difference from the mainland, where a separate admissions centre sits between students and the universities. In Tasmania, UTAS is that point of contact.

UTAS as Tasmania’s Tertiary Admissions Centre

In every state, a tertiary admissions centre sits at the centre of university entry. On the mainland, these are separate organisations: UAC for NSW and the ACT, VTAC for Victoria, QTAC for Queensland, SATAC for South Australia and the Northern Territory, and TISC for Western Australia. In Tasmania, the University of Tasmania itself operates the state’s Tertiary Admissions Centre for university entrance. That is why you will often see UTAS described as both a university and the Tasmanian TAC.

Because UTAS acts as the TAC, it is UTAS that sets the requirements for receiving an ATAR in Tasmania. TASC supports the calculation under an agreement with the University and issues eligible students with an ATAR Statement at the end of their Year 12 or Year 13 school year.

What this means for you

  • One main point of contact. For most Tasmanian school leavers, UTAS is both the place you apply to and the body that sets the rules for your ATAR.
  • Direct communication. Questions about entry, offers and pathways usually go straight to the University rather than through a separate centre.
  • The same national ATAR. Even though the arrangement is different, the ATAR you receive is the same national rank used in every other state.

A sense of scale

UTAS is a significant part of Australia’s admissions system. In January 2025, the national group of admissions centres reported that the University of Tasmania, as the Tasmanian TAC, had made 25,065 offers for Semester 1 2025 courses. The University also notes that about 40 per cent of its students come directly from Year 12, so most of its students arrive by other routes, such as pathways, later applications or interstate and international entry.

Why Tasmania works differently

Tasmania is the only state where the main university also operates the Tertiary Admissions Centre. On the mainland, several universities in each state share a separate admissions centre, which processes applications for all of them and coordinates offer rounds. In Tasmania, the University of Tasmania is the state’s university, so it makes sense for it to run the admissions process for Tasmanian school leavers directly.

For students, the practical effect is simplicity. Rather than navigating a centre that represents many institutions, Tasmanian students deal with the University itself for entry requirements, early offers, ATAR-based offers and enrolment. TASC, the state’s senior secondary authority, sits alongside it, looking after courses, assessment and certification.

What UTAS is not

  • It is not the body that assesses your Year 12 courses; that is TASC.
  • It is not the only university you can apply to; Tasmanian students can apply to universities across Australia using their ATAR.
  • It is not a separate admissions centre in the mainland sense; it is a university that also operates the state’s Tertiary Admissions Centre.

Where UTAS fits in the national system

Australia’s tertiary admissions centres work together so that the ATAR means the same thing everywhere. The University of Tasmania, as the Tasmanian TAC, takes part in that national system. When the centres report national offer figures, the University of Tasmania is listed alongside UAC, VTAC, QTAC, SATAC and TISC.

How UTAS’s dual role helps students

  • Clear information. Because the University sets both the ATAR rules and its own entry requirements, students can get answers about both from one place.
  • Early entry. The University can run its own early-entry scheme, the Schools Recommendation Program, tailored to Tasmanian schools.
  • Fast offers for Tasmanians. First-round ATAR-based offers to Tasmanian Year 12 applicants arrive in mid-December, soon after results.
  • Connections with schools. Programs such as HAP and UCP let school students take university units that can count towards their TE Score.

What to expect when you start at UTAS

Orientation

Before semester begins, the University runs orientation activities to help new students find their way, meet others and learn about support services.

A different style of learning

University study usually involves more independent learning than school. You will attend classes, complete readings and assessments, and manage your own time across several units.

Support services

Like other universities, UTAS offers support for study skills, wellbeing and other needs. New students who use these services early often settle in more quickly.

Your results now

Once you start, your university results become the main measure of your progress. They matter for transfers, scholarships and further study far more than your ATAR does.

What does UTAS do?

UTAS manages university admissions for Tasmanian students. It handles applications, sets entry requirements for its courses, and makes offers. It also uses your ATAR, alongside other pathways, to decide entry.

Because it is the state’s main university, UTAS is where most Tasmanian school leavers apply. Rather than applying through a separate centre and then to universities, Tasmanian students deal largely with UTAS directly.

So UTAS is both the destination and, in effect, the gateway. Understanding how it works helps you see how your TCE results become a university place.

The University’s role in more detail

The University’s admissions work spans the whole journey from Year 12 to enrolment:

  1. Setting entry rules. UTAS sets its General Entry Requirements and any extra subject requirements for particular courses.
  2. Running early entry. Through the Schools Recommendation Program, UTAS makes early offers to Year 12 students based on their teachers’ recommendations.
  3. Using the ATAR. Once ATARs are released, UTAS makes first-round offers to Tasmanian Year 12 applicants based on their ATAR.
  4. Assessing other applicants. It also assesses interstate, International Baccalaureate, mature-age and other applicants.
  5. Enrolling students. Successful applicants accept their offers and enrol in their units.

What TASC does, step by step

TASC, the Office of Tasmanian Assessment, Standards and Certification, is responsible for senior secondary standards and certification in Tasmania. Its work underpins every Tasmanian ATAR.

Accrediting courses

TASC accredits the senior secondary courses taught in Tasmanian schools and colleges, including the Level 3 and Level 4 courses that count towards the TE Score.

Overseeing assessment

TASC oversees how courses are assessed, including external assessment such as written examinations for many Level 3 and 4 courses. It also sets dates and rules, such as the dates after which schools cannot move students into or between Level 3 or 4 courses for the year.

Awarding the TCE

TASC awards the Tasmanian Certificate of Education, which is one of the requirements for an ATAR.

Scaling

TASC determines the scaling process and applies it to Level 3 and Level 4 courses and to approved HAP and UCP units, publishing scaling reports each year.

Issuing results and ATAR Statements

TASC issues each student’s results at the end of the year and, under its agreement with the University, issues eligible students with an ATAR Statement.

Supporting students

TASC publishes plain-English guides, such as “Understanding the ATAR” and “Focus on: TE Score and ATAR Calculation”, which explain the rules in detail.

From TE Score to ATAR, in more detail

Comparison with your peers

The University’s fact sheet explains that your TE Score is compared with the TE Scores achieved by your peers, and you are then issued with an ATAR. The ATAR shows where you rank in your year group, on the national scale from 0 to 99.95.

What an ATAR of 80 means in Tasmania

The University explains that an ATAR of 80.00 indicates that a student has an overall TE Score equal to, or better than, 80 per cent of Year 12 school leavers in Tasmania that year. That matches the national meaning of the ATAR as a rank.

Why a TE Score is not an ATAR

A TE Score is a total of scaled course scores, while the ATAR is a rank. Two students with similar TE Scores receive similar ATARs, but the same TE Score can correspond to slightly different ATARs in different years, because the ranking depends on that year’s students.

What your ATAR Statement shows

Your ATAR Statement includes your TE Score, your ATAR and the courses and course results used to calculate them, so you can see exactly which results counted.

TASC and UTAS: a detailed comparison

Because confusing the two bodies is the most common mistake Tasmanian students make, it is worth setting out their roles in full.

TASC’s responsibilities

  • Accrediting senior secondary courses at all levels, including Level 3 and Level 4.
  • Overseeing assessment, including external examinations.
  • Setting course movement and other deadlines.
  • Scaling Level 3 and 4 courses and approved HAP and UCP units.
  • Awarding the Tasmanian Certificate of Education.
  • Issuing Statements of Results and, under agreement with the University, ATAR Statements.
  • Running the exam inspection process.
  • Publishing guides on the ATAR, the TE Score and scaling.

UTAS’s responsibilities

  • Operating Tasmania’s Tertiary Admissions Centre for university entrance.
  • Setting the requirements for receiving an ATAR in Tasmania.
  • Setting its own course entry requirements and prerequisites.
  • Running the Schools Recommendation Program and other entry schemes.
  • Offering HAP and UCP units to school students.
  • Making offers and enrolling students.
  • Providing pathways and support for students who do not enter directly.

Where they overlap

Both bodies publish information about the ATAR, which is why students sometimes find the same topic explained on both the TASC and University websites. TASC explains the courses, assessment and calculation; the University explains entry, offers and what the ATAR means for admission. The 2025 calculation change was decided by the University as the TAC and communicated by both bodies.

A simple rule of thumb

If your question is about a course, an exam, a result or your certificate, start with TASC. If it is about getting into university, an offer or enrolment, start with the University. If it is about how your ATAR is calculated, either can help, and your ATAR Statement shows the detail.

Common pitfalls to avoid

A few avoidable mistakes come up again and again for Tasmanian students. Knowing them in advance helps you plan better.

Treating Year 11 as a practice year

Under the 2025 rule, strong Level 3 results in Year 11 can count towards your TE Score. Treating Year 11 as a throwaway year can cost you results that would have counted among your best.

Overlooking prerequisites

A strong ATAR cannot replace a missing prerequisite. Students who do not check course requirements early can find they are not eligible for a course despite a good result.

Confusing TASC and UTAS

Sending a question to the wrong body can waste time. Remember that TASC handles courses, assessment and the TCE, while UTAS handles university entry.

Relying on old advice

Advice based on the pre-2025 rules may no longer apply. Check current information, especially about which years’ results can count.

Missing the Schools Recommendation Program

Students who do not consider the SRP can miss a chance at an early offer. It is worth looking into early in Year 12.

Taking only the minimum courses

Taking only four Level 3 courses leaves no buffer if one result is lower than expected. Taking five gives you more room.

Leaving applications late

Missing application, interview or enrolment deadlines can cost you a place. Note the key dates and act on time.

Forgetting about pathways

If your ATAR falls short, pathways can still lead to your preferred degree. Students who do not explore them may give up on a goal unnecessarily.

UTAS vs TASC

Students often confuse UTAS with TASC, but they do different things. TASC, the Office of Tasmanian Assessment, Standards and Certification, sets the senior secondary courses, oversees their assessment, and awards the TCE.

UTAS comes at the university end. It uses the results TASC produces to work out entry, through your ATAR and other pathways, and it manages your application and offer. In short: TASC runs the courses and assessment; UTAS handles university entry.

So the two bodies are a relay. TASC assesses your subjects, and UTAS turns those results into a university place. They are separate organisations with separate roles.

Where students get confused

The names are similar, and both organisations publish information about the ATAR, so it is easy to mix them up. A simple way to remember: TASC is about your senior secondary courses and results; UTAS is about university entry. If your question is about a course result, an exam or your TCE, it is a TASC question. If it is about an offer, entry requirements or enrolment, it is a UTAS question. Questions about how the ATAR is calculated may involve both.

Your results and your ATAR Statement

At the end of the year, TASC issues your results. If you are eligible for an ATAR, an ATAR Statement is included with your Year 12 results. TASC explains that the statement shows your TE Score and ATAR, along with the courses and course results used to calculate them. Your Statement of Results also lists all the courses you studied that year, with their size in TCE credit points and your award in each.

Understanding your TCE results

Before your ATAR is calculated, TASC issues your results. Knowing how to read them helps you understand where your TE Score comes from.

Your Statement of Results

TASC’s results newsletter explains that the Statement of Results lists all the courses you studied in the year, including each course’s code and title, its size in TCE credit points and its level, from Preliminary Level to Level 4.

Awards

Each course receives an overall award, ranging from the highest awards, Exceptional Achievement and High Achievement, through Satisfactory Achievement, down to lower awards. A Satisfactory Achievement or better is needed for a Level 3 or Level 4 course to count towards ATAR eligibility. TASC publishes an “Understanding Assessment” guide explaining how your overall award is worked out.

Course scores and the TE Score

For Level 3 and Level 4 courses, your results produce course scores, which are scaled and may be counted towards your TE Score. If you are eligible for an ATAR, your ATAR Statement shows your TE Score, your ATAR and the courses and results used.

If something looks wrong

If a result does not match what you expected, talk to your school. TASC also runs a process for inspecting written examination material, with deadlines each year.

Scaling in Tasmania, in more depth

Scaling is central to how your TE Score is built, so it is worth understanding beyond the basics.

What scaling compares

TASC describes scaling as a complex mathematical process that analyses the results achieved by senior secondary students. It aligns results from different units and courses onto a common scale so that they can be fairly compared. The aim is that a given course score represents a similar level of achievement whichever course it comes from.

Why a course score is not a raw mark

Your course score for a Level 3 or 4 course is the result after scaling, not your raw assessment mark. TASC’s student fact sheet describes scaled courses as reflecting how difficult it was to achieve a particular award compared with awards achieved in other courses. Two students with the same award in different courses can therefore end up with different course scores.

HAP and UCP scaling

TASC applies essentially the same scaling method to approved HAP and UCP units, except that University of Tasmania results are used. Because these units can be smaller than a full course, only the appropriate fraction of the scaled score counts: an 8-point unit contributes 8/15 of its course score to the TE Score.

Scaling changes each year

Because scaling depends on the students taking each course, it is recalculated each year. Patterns from past years are a useful guide, but they are not a guarantee of how a course will scale in your year. TASC publishes scaling reports so that students and schools can see the established process.

What scaling means for your choices

The practical message is the same as elsewhere: your result within a course matters more than how the course scales. Choose Level 3 and 4 courses you are good at and interested in, and treat scaling information as a guide rather than a reason to avoid a subject you would do well in. Our guide to the best scaling subjects in Tasmania explains more.

How your ATAR is worked out

Your ATAR in Tasmania is built from your best pre-tertiary subjects, the Level 3 and Level 4 courses designed to count towards university entrance. Each is scaled so that a score in one subject is comparable to a score in another.

Scaling reflects how strong the students taking a subject are across all their subjects, not how hard the subject feels. A subject scales up when its cohort is strong, and down when it is broad. See how scaling works.

Your scaled pre-tertiary scores are then combined into a rank. So it is your scaled results in your best pre-tertiary subjects, not your raw marks, that drive your ATAR.

The terms you will see

Different documents use slightly different wording for the same idea. You may see “Tertiary Entrance rank”, “Tertiary Entrance Score”, “TE Score” or “TES”. In current TASC and University materials, the TE Score is the total of your best scaled Level 3 and 4 course scores, and the ATAR is the rank produced from it. When you read older material, check whether it describes the rules before or after 2025.

Why the best results are used

Using only your best results means your ATAR reflects your strongest work. It also means you can take an extra course you are interested in without risk, because a weaker result simply will not count. That is one of the reasons the University can describe the TE Score as being built from your best four to five eligible results.

Understanding the points system

The points system is a simple way to make sure every TE Score is built from a comparable amount of study.

Why points, not just courses

Not every unit is the same size. A full Level 3 or 4 course is worth 15 points, but a one-semester HAP or UCP unit is worth 8. Counting points rather than courses lets the system combine full courses and smaller units fairly.

The 60 to 75 point range

Your TE Score is built from a total of 60 to 75 points. Four full courses give 60 points, the minimum. Five full courses give 75 points, the most common total. Combinations that include 8-point units can involve more units while staying within the range.

Worked point totals

  • Five Level 3 courses: 5 × 15 = 75 points.
  • Four Level 3 courses: 4 × 15 = 60 points.
  • Four Level 3 courses plus a HAP unit: 60 + 8 = 68 points, with the HAP unit contributing 8/15 of its score.
  • Three Level 3 courses plus a full-year UCP unit: 3 × 15 + 15 = 60 points, with the UCP unit treated like a course.

Why the range has a cap

Capping the total at 75 points keeps every student’s TE Score built from a comparable amount of study, whether they took exactly five courses or a mix of courses and units. Extra study beyond the cap does not add to the total, but it can replace a weaker result among your best.

The Tertiary Entrance rank

From 2025, Tasmania combines your best Level 3 and 4 course scores from any two years of senior study (Years 11, 12 or 13) into a Tertiary Entrance (TE) Score, which is expressed as an ATAR. For most students, that means their strongest five Level 3 and 4 courses carry their result.

Because only your best results count, attempting more pre-tertiary subjects does not penalise you. The rank uses the combination that works most in your favour, which gives you room to try more subjects without risk.

The higher your combined scaled score across those best five, the higher your ATAR, since the ATAR is that combined score expressed as a rank.

How the TE Score is built from 2025

Tasmania’s ATAR is based on your Tertiary Entrance Score, usually called your TE Score. TASC describes it as a measure of your overall academic achievement in Years 11 and 12. From 2025, the University changed how the TE Score is put together.

The 2025 rule

TASC announced that, from 2025 onwards, an ATAR is calculated using an eligible student’s best results in Level 3 and 4 courses over two years of senior secondary education, using any combination of two years from Years 11, 12 or 13. The University describes the TE Score in the same way: it combines your best Level 3 or Level 4 course scores from two senior secondary years.

Points, not just courses

The TE Score is built by adding course scores up to a set total of points. TASC explains that Level 3 and Level 4 TASC accredited courses, and NIAL courses, provide 15 points each, while University of Tasmania High Achiever Program units provide 8 points for a unit with one semester of study. The best course scores are added together until they reach a total of 60 to 75 points.

  • Four courses: you can receive a TE Score with only four Level 3 and 4 courses over two years, which gives the minimum total of 60 points.
  • Five courses: using five Level 3 and 4 courses over two years, for a total of 75 points, is the most common way to receive a TE Score.

From TE Score to ATAR

Your TE Score is then compared with the TE Scores achieved by your peers, and you are issued with an ATAR between 0 and 99.95. The University gives an example: an ATAR of 80.00 means a student has an overall TE Score equal to, or better than, 80 per cent of Year 12 school leavers in Tasmania that year.

In short
Best Level 3 and 4 course scores from any two years of senior study, each worth 15 points, added up to 60 to 75 points, form your TE Score, which is then ranked to give your ATAR.

TE Score examples

TASC publishes worked examples showing how TE Scores are built. The illustrations below follow the same structure, with invented scores, to show how the rules work in practice.

Example 1: five courses over Years 11 and 12

Mia completes two Level 3 courses in Year 11 and four in Year 12. Her TE Score uses her best five course scores across those two years, for a total of 75 points. Her weakest Year 12 course is left out because her two Year 11 courses scored more highly. Under the rules that applied before 2025, she would have needed at least three of her counted courses from her final year; under the 2025 rule, her best results from the two years can be combined.

Example 2: four courses

Jack completes only four Level 3 and 4 courses with a Satisfactory Achievement or better across Years 11 and 12. He can still receive a TE Score, using those four courses for the minimum total of 60 points. TASC notes that some students use four courses, for example when one course result is below Satisfactory Achievement and cannot count.

Example 3: including a HAP unit

Ava takes a University of Tasmania High Achiever Program unit in Year 12 alongside her Level 3 courses. The one-semester unit provides 8 points, so it can contribute part of her total. TASC explains that a single 8-point unit cannot by itself replace a full course, because 15 points are needed to reach the minimum total of 60.

Example 4: a Year 13 student

Liam studies across Years 11, 12 and 13. Under the 2025 rule, his TE Score uses his best results from any two of those three years. TASC’s published examples include a Year 13 case to show how this works.

ATAR eligibility in Tasmania

Not every Tasmanian Year 12 student receives an ATAR. The requirements are set by the University of Tasmania as the state’s Tertiary Admissions Centre.

The requirements

TASC’s 2025 results newsletter sets out the requirements. To achieve an ATAR in Tasmania, you need to complete at least two years of post-Year 10 study and:

  1. achieve the TCE; and
  2. achieve an award of Satisfactory Achievement or better in at least four TASC Level 3 or Level 4 courses, or equivalent, including HAP and UCP courses.

General Entry Requirements at UTAS

The University’s General Entry Requirements are closely linked. You meet them if you have completed the TCE and have Satisfactory Achievement results in at least four, and a maximum of five, Level 3 or Level 4 subjects accredited by TASC, among other routes. Some courses also require specific TCE Level 3 or Level 4 subjects, or interstate equivalents, in addition to the General Entry Requirements.

If you are not eligible

Students who do not meet the ATAR requirements can still reach university. The University emphasises that it has a place for students through a range of routes, including the Schools Recommendation Program and alternative pathways for those who do not meet minimum entrance requirements.

What changed in 2025

The 2025 change was described by TASC as a minor change to the ATAR calculation process, decided by the University of Tasmania as the Tertiary Admissions Centre.

Before 2025

Under the earlier rules, an eligible student’s ATAR was calculated using the best three scores from their final year of study, usually Year 12, and the two best scores from that same year or one other year. TASC’s 2024 newsletter also noted that at least three of the four or more qualifying courses had to be in the final year.

From 2025

From 2025, the ATAR uses an eligible student’s best results in Level 3 and 4 courses over two years of senior secondary education, from any combination of two years from Years 11, 12 or 13.

Why the change was made

The University’s fact sheet explains that the earlier approach could be challenging for some students, for example those who perform better in Year 11 than Year 12, or who do not complete as many subjects in Year 12, making it harder to achieve the highest possible ATAR. The University phased in the change to allow time to communicate it, recognising that students then in Year 12 had already made decisions about which courses they would take in their final year.

What it means in practice

  • Strong Year 11 results in Level 3 courses can now count more fully.
  • Students who take fewer Level 3 courses in Year 12 are less disadvantaged.
  • Older calculators and advice based on the pre-2025 rules may no longer be accurate.

What a Tasmanian ATAR measures, and what it does not

A rank, not a mark

Your ATAR is a rank between 0 and 99.95. It shows where your TE Score sits compared with other Tasmanian students that year. The University explains it with an example: an ATAR of 80.00 means your TE Score is equal to or better than that of 80 per cent of Year 12 school leavers in Tasmania that year. It is not a percentage of marks and not an average of your course results.

A measure of your best results

Because the TE Score uses your best Level 3 and 4 results from two years, your ATAR reflects your strongest academic work rather than every course you took. A weaker course outside your best results does not lower your ATAR.

A national currency

Every state produces ATARs on the same scale, and universities across Australia accept them. A Tasmanian ATAR is used on the same basis as one from any other state.

What it does not measure

  • It does not measure your ability in courses outside your best results.
  • It does not capture interests, skills or qualities that sit outside formal assessment.
  • It does not decide your whole future; it is a tool for university entry.

Why other measures matter too

The University’s Schools Recommendation Program reflects this. By assessing applicants on their teachers’ recommendations rather than an ATAR, it recognises that a single number does not capture everything about a student’s readiness for university.

How the national ATAR system fits together

The ATAR works across Australia because the admissions centres cooperate. Understanding this helps you see why a Tasmanian ATAR travels so easily.

A shared scale

Every centre produces ATARs on the same 0 to 99.95 scale, representing a student’s position in their state’s age group. Although each state calculates its aggregate differently, the final rank means the same thing everywhere.

Cooperation between centres

The admissions centres work together, publish combined information and report national offer figures. In December 2025, for example, they jointly announced ATAR release dates and prepared for the ATAR’s calculation across the country. The University of Tasmania takes part in this as the Tasmanian TAC.

Student mobility

Because ATARs are comparable, students can apply across state borders. A Tasmanian student can apply to a mainland university, and a mainland student can apply to UTAS, each using their own ATAR. The admissions centres describe the ATAR as a key enabler of student mobility.

What this means for you

Whether you plan to stay in Tasmania or study elsewhere, your ATAR is a national currency. Build it through strong results in your best Level 3 and 4 courses, and it will serve you wherever you apply.

A Tasmanian ATAR in context

It can help to see how a Tasmanian ATAR sits alongside results elsewhere, while remembering that the ATAR means the same position in the age group everywhere.

The same meaning

An ATAR of 80 in Tasmania, as the University explains, means a TE Score at or above that of 80 per cent of Tasmanian Year 12 school leavers that year. An ATAR of 80 in another state means the same position among that state’s students. The rank is national.

Different paths to the same rank

A Tasmanian student reaches an ATAR of 80 through a TE Score built from Level 3 and 4 courses over two years. A NSW student reaches the same ATAR through scaled HSC marks in 10 units, and a Victorian student through scaled VCE study scores. The calculations differ, but the rank is comparable.

Is a Tasmanian ATAR comparable?

Yes. Although Tasmania calculates entrance through UTAS using TCE results, the ATAR itself is national. An ATAR of 85 from Tasmania means the same as an ATAR of 85 from any other state: you finished ahead of about 85 percent of the relevant age group.

So a Tasmanian ATAR is directly comparable to an interstate one, and it is recognised by universities across Australia. If you plan to study on the mainland, your Tasmanian ATAR travels with you and is assessed the same way as a local one.

HAP and UCP units

Tasmania has a feature no other state shares in the same way: some University of Tasmania units completed while still at school can count towards your ATAR.

What they are

The High Achiever Program (HAP) and the University Connections Program (UCP) let senior secondary students study University of Tasmania units. TASC’s scaling process applies to approved HAP and UCP units as well as to TASC-accredited Level 3 and Level 4 courses.

How many points they provide

  • HAP units provide 8 points for a unit with one semester of study.
  • UCP units provide 8 points for a one-semester unit or 15 points for a full-year unit.

Because a 15-point UCP unit is worth the same as a Level 3 or Level 4 course, TASC explains that it can be used interchangeably with, or instead of, a Level 3 or 4 course in an overall TE Score. An 8-point unit, by contrast, counts as 8/15 of a course: TASC’s scaling report notes that only 8/15 of the course score achieved is considered in the TE Score calculation.

How they are scaled

TASC explains that HAP and UCP units are scaled using essentially the same method as other courses, except that University of Tasmania results are used. TASC publishes a separate HAP and UCP scaling report each year.

Should you take one?

HAP and UCP units can be a rewarding way to experience university study and, in some cases, contribute to your TE Score. Whether they help your ATAR depends on your result and how the unit fits with your other courses. Talk to your school and the University about whether a unit suits you.

Comparing Tasmanian results with other states

Different calculations, same scale

Each state builds its aggregate differently. NSW uses scaled marks in 10 units, Victoria uses scaled study scores, Queensland and Western Australia use a Tertiary Entrance Aggregate, South Australia and the NT use a 90-credit university aggregate, and Tasmania uses the TE Score. In every case, the aggregate is turned into an ATAR on the same 0 to 99.95 scale.

What is distinctive about Tasmania

  • The University operates the Tertiary Admissions Centre.
  • Results from two years of senior study can count, from Years 11, 12 or 13.
  • Approved University units completed at school can contribute.
  • The TE Score uses a points total of 60 to 75.

If you move between Tasmania and another state during senior school, check how your results will be treated, because each system has its own rules about which results count. If you complete Year 12 in one state and apply to university in another, your ATAR is used directly.

Applying to UTAS: a closer look

Choosing your courses

Start by exploring the University’s courses and their entry requirements. Note any subject prerequisites, interviews, auditions or portfolio requirements, and check which campus offers each course.

Early entry or ATAR-based entry

If you are in Year 12, decide whether to apply through the Schools Recommendation Program for an early offer. Even if you do, you will still receive your results and, if eligible, an ATAR Statement in December.

Additional requirements

For courses that need an interview or audition, such as Music, Performing Arts and Fine Arts, the University notes that these begin in November. Prepare your materials early.

After results

Once results are released, the University makes first-round ATAR-based offers to Tasmanian Year 12 applicants in mid-December. Check your email and the University’s applicant portal for offers.

Accepting and enrolling

Accept your offer before the deadline, meet any conditions and enrol in your units once enrolment opens.

If things do not go to plan

If you do not receive the offer you hoped for, contact the University to discuss related courses and pathways. Many students reach their preferred course by another route.

Planning around prerequisites in detail

Because the University notes that some courses require specific TCE Level 3 or Level 4 subjects in addition to its General Entry Requirements, prerequisites deserve careful planning.

Find the prerequisites early

Look up the courses you are interested in while you are choosing your Year 10 and Year 11 subjects. Note any required or assumed Level 3 or 4 subjects, and plan to take them in time.

Distinguish required from assumed

A required subject is one you must have completed. An assumed subject is one the course expects you to know; if you have not studied it, you may still be eligible but may need a bridging option. Check how each course describes its requirements.

Allow for sequences

Some subjects build on earlier ones, especially in mathematics and the sciences. Make sure you take the earlier courses in time to reach the Level 3 or 4 course you need.

Keep options open

If you are unsure what you want to study, taking a broad set of Level 3 courses, including a mathematics course, keeps the most options open.

Check interstate equivalents

If you studied interstate, the University notes that interstate equivalents of TCE subjects may satisfy prerequisites. Check how your subjects map across.

Accommodation

The University’s guides for students and parents include information on accommodation. Look into University-managed and private options early, especially if you are moving from interstate or from a regional part of Tasmania.

Scholarships and costs

The University publishes information on scholarships, fees and costs. Some scholarships support students from particular backgrounds or regions, and some have early deadlines, so check them while you apply.

Campuses

The University has campuses in different parts of Tasmania. Check which campus offers your course and what student life is like there.

Support for new students

New students have access to orientation, academic support and wellbeing services. Using them early helps you settle in.

Applying to UTAS

Tasmanian students generally apply to UTAS directly. You list the courses you want, and UTAS assesses your application against its entry requirements, using your ATAR and any other pathways you qualify for.

Because UTAS is the state’s university, this is a more direct process than the multi-preference systems on the mainland. Still, it is worth applying for the courses you genuinely want and understanding each one’s requirements.

UTAS also considers applicants from interstate and offers a range of courses, so its admissions process is not limited to Tasmanian school leavers.

Steps in the application process

  1. Explore courses. Look at UTAS courses, their entry requirements and any prerequisite subjects.
  2. Consider early entry. Apply through the Schools Recommendation Program if you are in Year 12 and eligible.
  3. Check additional requirements. Some courses, such as Music, Performing Arts and Fine Arts, use interviews or auditions, which UTAS notes begin in November.
  4. Receive your results. Your TCE results and, if eligible, your ATAR Statement arrive in December.
  5. Receive offers. UTAS makes first-round offers to Tasmanian Year 12 applicants based on their ATAR in mid-December.
  6. Accept and enrol. Accept your offer and enrol in your units. UTAS notes that Year 12 enrolment opens after ATARs are released.

Prerequisites matter

UTAS notes that specific TCE Level 3 or Level 4 subjects, or interstate equivalents, may be required for entry to some courses in addition to the General Entry Requirements. Check these when you choose your senior courses, because an ATAR cannot replace a missing prerequisite.

After you receive an offer

Receiving an offer from UTAS is an exciting moment, but there are a few steps before you start.

Read your offer carefully

Your offer letter sets out the course, the campus, the start date and any conditions. Conditions might include finishing Year 12 or passing specific subjects that are prerequisites for your course. Make sure you understand what you need to do and by when.

Accept your offer

Follow the instructions to accept your offer before the deadline. If you received early offers through the Schools Recommendation Program, the University explains that you can accept more than one, then decide which course to enrol in later in December.

Meet any conditions

If your offer is conditional, the conditions must be met before you can enrol. For Year 12 students, that usually means waiting for your results in December.

Enrol in your units

Once your results are received and any conditions are met, you can enrol in your units. For Year 12 students, the University notes that enrolment opens from mid-December, after ATARs are released, and that it emails students when enrolments open.

Prepare for orientation

Before semester starts, look out for orientation activities, information about your course and support services, and practical matters such as accommodation, transport and study materials.

If you change your mind

If you decide you would prefer a different course, contact the University. Depending on timing and availability, you may be able to change your course or accept a different offer.

Applying to mainland universities from Tasmania

Many Tasmanian students apply to universities on the mainland as well as, or instead of, UTAS. Your Tasmanian ATAR is accepted everywhere, but the process is different from applying to UTAS.

Apply through the relevant admissions centre

To apply to universities in other states, you usually apply through that state’s admissions centre: UAC for NSW and the ACT, VTAC for Victoria, QTAC for Queensland, SATAC for South Australia and the Northern Territory, and TISC for Western Australia.

Preference lists

Mainland admissions centres use preference lists, where you rank the courses you want and receive an offer for the highest preference you are eligible for. The number of preferences differs: UAC allows five, QTAC and TISC allow six, and VTAC allows eight.

Timing

Your Tasmanian ATAR needs to reach the mainland admissions centre before it can be used in an offer round, and each centre has its own offer dates. Check when the centre you are applying to expects to include Tasmanian ATARs.

Adjustments

Mainland universities have their own adjustment factor schemes, such as subject, regional and equity adjustments. Check whether you are eligible as a Tasmanian applicant.

More misunderstandings, cleared up

“UTAS only takes students with high ATARs”

UTAS offers courses with a range of entry requirements, an early-entry program based on teachers’ recommendations and pathways for students who do not meet minimum requirements. It describes itself as having a place for students whatever happens.

“If I take more Level 3 courses, my ATAR will go down”

Your TE Score uses your best results up to 60 to 75 points. Extra courses only count if they are among your best, so they cannot lower your TE Score directly.

“Scaling means hard courses always help”

Scaling aligns results from different courses onto a common scale. A course only helps your TE Score if your result in it is among your best. Choose courses you can do well in.

“The ATAR Statement and my results are the same document”

Your Statement of Results lists all your courses and awards. The ATAR Statement, issued to eligible students, shows your TE Score, ATAR and the courses used.

“Interstate students get offers at the same time as Tasmanians”

Tasmanian Year 12 applicants receive first-round ATAR-based offers in mid-December, while interstate Year 12 and IB applicants receive first-round offers progressively in January.

“Old advice about the ATAR still applies”

Advice based on the pre-2025 rules, such as needing three counted courses from your final year, may no longer apply. Check current information from TASC and the University.

The Schools Recommendation Program in detail

The Schools Recommendation Program (SRP) is the University’s main early-entry scheme for Year 12 students, and one of the most important things for Tasmanian students to understand.

How it works

The SRP gives Year 12 students the chance to receive an early offer for a place at university. Your application is assessed on the recommendation of those who know you best, your teachers, rather than on an ATAR result. The University recommends applying for early entry through the SRP.

Why it helps

An early offer can give you peace of mind and the freedom to focus on finishing Year 12. The University also notes that you can accept multiple offers, securing your place in the courses you have been offered and taking your time to decide which one to enrol in later in December.

Conditions

You still need to finish your Year 12 studies. Some offers have conditions, such as passing specific subjects if your chosen degree has prerequisites, and these are set out in your offer letter. The University emphasises that full participation in Year 12 is important preparation for successful university study.

Enrolling after an early offer

From mid-December, after the University receives your Year 12 results, you can enrol in your units, and the University emails you when enrolments open. Any conditions on your offer must be met before you can enrol.

Tip
If you are in Year 12 in Tasmania and interested in UTAS, look at the SRP early in the year. It is one of the clearest ways to secure a place before your ATAR is released.

Deciding whether to take a HAP or UCP unit

Benefits

  • A taste of university. Studying a University of Tasmania unit while at school can build confidence and familiarity with university expectations.
  • A possible TE Score contribution. Approved units are scaled by TASC and can contribute 8 or 15 points to your TE Score.
  • Depth in an area you love. Units can extend your learning in a subject you are passionate about.

Things to weigh

  • Workload. A university unit adds study time alongside your school courses.
  • Points. An 8-point unit contributes only 8/15 of its course score to the TE Score, and cannot replace a full course on its own.
  • Results. Like any course, a unit only helps your TE Score if your result is among your best.

Questions to ask

  • Is this unit approved for scaling and inclusion in the TE Score?
  • Is it a one-semester or full-year unit, and how many points does it provide?
  • How will the extra workload fit with my other courses?

How TASC scaling works

Scaling is the step that makes course scores from different subjects comparable before they are added into your TE Score.

Who scales and what is scaled

TASC determines the scaling process. It applies to all TASC-accredited senior secondary courses at Level 3 or Level 4, including NIAL courses, and to approved University of Tasmania HAP and UCP units.

Why scaling is needed

TASC explains that scaling allows results from different courses to be fairly compared with each other. It aligns results from different units and courses onto a common scale using statistical techniques. TASC’s student fact sheet describes scaled courses as reflecting how difficult it was to achieve a particular award compared with awards achieved in other courses.

What scaling means for you

  • The same award in two different courses can produce different course scores.
  • Scaling does not change who did better within a course.
  • Scaling is recalculated each year, so past patterns are a guide, not a guarantee.

TASC publishes scaling information each year, including a HAP and UCP scaling report. Our guides to the best scaling subjects in Tasmania and TCE scaling explain more.

Estimating your Tasmanian ATAR step by step

An estimate is a planning tool, not a promise. Here is how to build a sensible one under the 2025 rules.

Step 1: List your Level 3 and 4 courses across two years

Write down every Level 3 and Level 4 course you have taken or will take in Years 11 and 12 (or Year 13), along with any HAP or UCP units. Note each course’s point value: 15 points for Level 3 and 4 courses, NIAL courses and full-year UCP units, and 8 points for one-semester HAP or UCP units.

Step 2: Note your awards or expected awards

For completed courses, use your actual results. For courses still in progress, use realistic expectations based on your school assessments and teacher feedback. Remember that a course needs Satisfactory Achievement or better to count.

Step 3: Apply scaling

Each course’s result is scaled into a course score. A calculator built for Tasmania applies recent scaling to your results; you can also look at TASC’s scaling information.

Step 4: Pick your best courses up to 60 to 75 points

Choose your highest scaled course scores until you reach a total of 60 to 75 points. For most students, that means their best five courses.

Step 5: Convert to an estimated ATAR

Your TE Score is compared with recent TE Scores to estimate your ATAR. Because the comparison depends on each year’s students, treat the result as a range.

Step 6: Run scenarios

Run cautious, realistic and optimistic versions of your remaining results, and re-run your estimate as results come in. Our TCE ATAR calculator can help.

Putting it all together

UTAS is unusual among Australian universities because it is both the state’s university and the operator of the state’s Tertiary Admissions Centre. That gives Tasmanian students a direct relationship with the institution that sets the ATAR rules, makes offers and enrols students.

From 2025, your Tasmanian ATAR is built from your best Level 3 and 4 results from any two years of senior study, each worth 15 points, combined into a TE Score of 60 to 75 points and ranked against your peers. TASC accredits and assesses your courses, scales them, awards the TCE and issues your ATAR Statement. UTAS sets the ATAR requirements, runs the Schools Recommendation Program, makes first-round ATAR-based offers in mid-December and enrols you.

Above all, there is more than one way in. Whether through an early offer, your ATAR, a pathway or later study, the University emphasises that it has a place for students. Plan your Level 3 courses across two years, check the requirements of the courses you want and talk to the University early.

Early entry compared with ATAR entry

Many Tasmanian students weigh up whether to rely on the Schools Recommendation Program, their ATAR, or both. Here is how the two main routes compare.

Schools Recommendation Program

  • Based on: teachers’ recommendations rather than an ATAR.
  • Timing: during Year 12, before results.
  • Certainty: an early offer can give peace of mind.
  • Conditions: you must finish Year 12 and meet any conditions, such as prerequisite subjects.
  • Flexibility: you can accept multiple early offers and choose later.

ATAR-based entry

  • Based on: your ATAR and the course’s requirements.
  • Timing: mid-December for Tasmanian Year 12 applicants.
  • Certainty: depends on your results meeting the course’s requirements.
  • Reach: works for any course whose requirements you meet.

Using both

The two routes work well together. Applying through the SRP can secure an early offer, while a strong ATAR can open courses you did not receive early offers for. You can then choose the course you most want.

Which to rely on

If an early offer for a course you are happy with gives you peace of mind, the SRP is valuable. If you are aiming for a course with specific requirements, your ATAR still matters. For most students, applying through the SRP and working towards a strong ATAR is a sensible combination.

Guaranteed entry and school recommendations

Because UTAS is the state’s university and manages its own admissions, it offers entry schemes beyond a raw ATAR cutoff. These can include guaranteed entry to certain courses at set ATARs, and programs that recognise a school’s recommendation of a student.

So getting into UTAS is not only about clearing a single number. If you are aiming for a UTAS course, it is worth checking whether a guaranteed-entry threshold or a school-recommendation program applies, since these can give you a clearer, earlier sense of your place.

Guaranteed entry and the ATAR

Where a course has a guaranteed entry threshold, meeting it, along with any other requirements, gives you certainty. Where a course does not, offers depend on the course’s requirements and the number of places. Check each course’s current entry information on the University’s website.

Mature-age and non-school-leaver entry

UTAS admits many students who are not coming straight from Year 12. With only about 40 per cent of its students coming directly from Year 12, most UTAS students enter through other routes.

Applying as a non-school leaver

The University’s key dates show that enrolment for the coming year opens earlier for non-school leavers, while Year 12 students’ enrolment opens after ATARs are released. Non-school leavers are usually assessed on their previous study, qualifications, work experience or completion of a preparation program.

Using an old ATAR

If you received an ATAR in the past, it does not expire, and it may be considered alongside more recent study or experience.

Pathways for adults

The University offers alternative pathways for applicants who do not meet minimum entrance requirements. Contact the University to find the route that suits your situation.

Common questions

What is UTAS?

UTAS is the University of Tasmania, the state’s university. Because Tasmania has no separate tertiary admissions centre, UTAS also manages admissions for Tasmanian students, playing the role a body like UAC or VTAC plays elsewhere.

What does UTAS do?

UTAS manages university admissions for Tasmanian students: it handles applications, sets entry requirements, uses your ATAR and other pathways to decide entry, and makes offers. It is both the state’s main university and, in effect, the admissions gateway.

How does UTAS work out my ATAR?

Your ATAR is built from your best Level 3 and 4 course scores over two years of senior study (usually your best five), each scaled and combined into a TE Score, which is expressed as an ATAR up to 99.95. UTAS then uses that ATAR for entry.

Is UTAS the same as TASC?

No. TASC sets the senior secondary courses, oversees assessment and awards the TCE. UTAS comes at the university end, using those results to decide entry and manage applications. They are separate bodies with separate roles.

Is UTAS a tertiary admissions centre?

Yes, in effect. The University of Tasmania operates Tasmania’s Tertiary Admissions Centre for university entrance, so it plays the role that separate centres such as UAC, VTAC, QTAC, SATAC and TISC play on the mainland. It also sets the requirements for receiving an ATAR in Tasmania.

Who sets the ATAR rules in Tasmania?

The requirements to receive an ATAR in Tasmania are set by the University of Tasmania, as the state’s Tertiary Admissions Centre. TASC supports the calculation under an agreement with the University and issues eligible students with an ATAR Statement.

Who issues my ATAR Statement?

TASC issues eligible students with an ATAR Statement at the end of their Year 12 or Year 13 school year, under an agreement with the University of Tasmania. The statement is included with your end-of-year results.

What is on my ATAR Statement?

TASC explains that the ATAR Statement includes your TE Score and ATAR, along with the courses and course results used to calculate them. It lets you see exactly which of your results counted towards your ATAR.

What is a TE Score?

The Tertiary Entrance Score is the total of your best scaled Level 3 and 4 course scores from two years of senior study. TASC describes it as a measure of your overall academic achievement in Years 11 and 12. It is ranked against your peers to produce your ATAR.

How is the TE Score calculated from 2025?

From 2025, the TE Score combines your best Level 3 or Level 4 course scores from any two senior secondary years, chosen from Years 11, 12 or 13. Each Level 3 or 4 course provides 15 points, and the best scores are added up to a total of 60 to 75 points.

How many courses count towards a Tasmanian ATAR?

Four or five. You can receive a TE Score with four Level 3 and 4 courses over two years, giving the minimum 60 points, and using five courses for 75 points is the most common way. The University’s fact sheet describes the TE Score as your best four to five eligible results from any two years of study.

How many points is a Level 3 course worth?

TASC explains that each Level 3 and Level 4 TASC accredited course, and each NIAL course, provides 15 points towards the TE Score total.

How many points is a HAP unit worth?

A University of Tasmania High Achiever Program unit with one semester of study provides 8 points towards the TE Score total, according to TASC.

How many points is a UCP unit worth?

University Connections Program units provide 8 points for a one-semester unit or 15 points for a full-year unit. A 15-point UCP unit can be used interchangeably with, or instead of, a Level 3 or 4 course in a TE Score.

Can a single HAP unit replace a Level 3 course?

Not on its own. Because a HAP unit provides 8 points, TASC explains that one such unit would not provide enough points by itself, since 15 points are needed to reach the minimum total of 60. Only 8/15 of the unit’s score is considered in the TE Score calculation.

Do Year 11 results count towards my ATAR in Tasmania?

They can. From 2025, your ATAR uses your best Level 3 and 4 results from any two years of senior secondary study, chosen from Years 11, 12 or 13, so strong Year 11 results in Level 3 courses can count.

Can Year 13 results count?

Yes. TASC explains that the 2025 rule uses a combination of two years from Years 11, 12 or 13, so students who study in Year 13 can have results from that year counted.

What were the rules before 2025?

Before 2025, an eligible student’s ATAR was calculated using the best three scores from their final year of study, usually Year 12, and the two best scores from that same year or one other year. TASC’s 2024 newsletter also noted that at least three qualifying courses had to be in the final year.

Why did UTAS change the ATAR calculation?

The University’s fact sheet explains that the earlier approach could be challenging for some students, such as those who perform better in Year 11 than Year 12 or who do not complete as many subjects in Year 12, making it harder to achieve the highest possible ATAR.

Was the 2025 change big?

TASC described it as a minor change to the ATAR calculation process. It changed which course results can be used, allowing the best results from any two years of senior study, but the overall structure of scaled course scores forming a TE Score that is ranked into an ATAR remains.

What do I need to get an ATAR in Tasmania?

You need to complete at least two years of post-Year 10 study, achieve the TCE, and achieve Satisfactory Achievement or better in at least four TASC Level 3 or Level 4 courses or equivalent, including HAP and UCP courses.

What is Satisfactory Achievement?

Satisfactory Achievement is a TASC award level. You need Satisfactory Achievement or better in at least four Level 3 or Level 4 courses to be eligible for an ATAR, and a course result below Satisfactory Achievement cannot be used in your TE Score.

What are UTAS’s General Entry Requirements?

You meet the University’s General Entry Requirements if you have completed the TCE and have Satisfactory Achievement results in at least four, and a maximum of five, Level 3 or Level 4 subjects accredited by TASC, among other routes. Some courses also require specific subjects.

Do some UTAS courses need specific subjects?

Yes. The University notes that specific TCE Level 3 or Level 4 subjects, or interstate equivalents, may be required for entry to some courses in addition to the General Entry Requirements.

What does an ATAR of 80 mean in Tasmania?

The University explains that an ATAR of 80.00 indicates an overall TE Score equal to, or better than, 80 per cent of Year 12 school leavers in Tasmania that year.

Is a Tasmanian ATAR the same as a mainland ATAR?

Yes. The ATAR is a national rank from 0 to 99.95. A Tasmanian ATAR is accepted by universities across Australia, and an interstate ATAR is accepted by UTAS.

Who scales Tasmanian courses?

TASC determines the scaling process and applies it to all TASC-accredited Level 3 and Level 4 courses, including NIAL courses, and to approved University of Tasmania HAP and UCP units.

Why are Tasmanian courses scaled?

TASC explains that scaling allows results from different courses to be fairly compared with each other, aligning them onto a common scale using statistical techniques.

How are HAP and UCP units scaled?

TASC explains that HAP and UCP units are scaled using essentially the same method as other courses, except that University of Tasmania results are used. TASC publishes a separate HAP and UCP scaling report.

What is the Schools Recommendation Program?

The Schools Recommendation Program is the University’s early-entry scheme for Year 12 students. Applications are assessed on the recommendation of teachers rather than an ATAR result, and successful students can receive an early offer.

Can I accept more than one early offer from UTAS?

Yes. The University explains that you can accept multiple offers, securing your place in the courses you have been offered and taking time to decide which one to enrol in later in December.

What is scaling in Tasmania?

TASC describes scaling as a complex mathematical process that analyses senior secondary students’ results and aligns results from different courses onto a common scale, so that they can be fairly compared and added into a TE Score.

Is my course score the same as my raw mark?

No. Your course score is the result after scaling, not your raw assessment mark. Two students with the same award in different courses can end up with different course scores.

Why does Tasmania use a points system?

Because units differ in size. A full Level 3 or 4 course is worth 15 points and a one-semester HAP or UCP unit is worth 8. Counting points lets the system combine full courses and smaller units fairly.

What is the point range for a TE Score?

A TE Score is built from a total of 60 to 75 points. Four full courses give 60 points, five give 75, and combinations including 8-point units can involve more units within the range.

How many points do I get for five Level 3 courses?

Five Level 3 or 4 courses at 15 points each give 75 points, the most common total for a TE Score.

Four full courses give 60 points, and a one-semester HAP unit adds 8 points, for 68 points, with the HAP unit contributing 8/15 of its score.

Can a full-year UCP unit replace a course?

Yes. A full-year 15-point UCP unit is worth the same as a Level 3 or 4 course and can be used in its place in the TE Score.

What happens if I study more than 75 points?

Extra study beyond the cap does not add to your total, but it can replace a weaker result among your best, which is why taking more courses gives you a buffer.

Why is the point total capped at 75?

Capping the total keeps every student’s TE Score built from a comparable amount of study, whether they took five courses or a mix of courses and units.

Does the order of my courses matter?

Does a bigger course give more points?

Full Level 3 and 4 courses each provide 15 points. Smaller units, such as one-semester HAP or UCP units, provide 8 points. The points reflect the size of the study.

Scaling reflects how students in a course perform, not a judgement that a course is difficult. A course only helps your TE Score if your result in it is among your best.

Yes. University study usually involves more independent learning, with classes, readings and assessments across several units, and more responsibility for managing your own time.

Does UTAS help with study skills?

Like other universities, UTAS offers support for study skills and wellbeing. Using these services early can help new students settle in.

Do my UTAS results matter more than my ATAR once I start?

Yes. Once you start, your university results become the main measure of your progress for transfers, scholarships and further study.

How do I prepare for my first semester at UTAS?

Accept your offer, meet any conditions, enrol in your units, attend orientation and familiarise yourself with your course and support services.

Can I get credit at UTAS for HAP or UCP units?

Units completed through HAP or UCP are University of Tasmania units. Whether they give credit towards a degree depends on the course. Check with the University.

Do I need to be a high achiever for UCP?

The University Connections Program is a separate program from the High Achiever Program. Check the University’s information for each program’s eligibility.

Is the ATAR the only thing UTAS looks at?

No. UTAS also considers teachers’ recommendations through the SRP, subject prerequisites, interviews or auditions for some courses, and other pathways for students who do not meet minimum requirements.

Can I apply to UTAS after receiving my ATAR?

Yes. You can apply after receiving your ATAR, and Tasmanian Year 12 applicants receive first-round ATAR-based offers in mid-December. Check the University’s application dates.

Are there late offers at UTAS?

Admissions centres generally make further offers into February. The University also publishes dates for later intakes, such as Semester 2. Check its key dates.

What if I need to change campus?

Contact the University. Whether you can change campus depends on where your course is offered and on availability.

Is it possible to study online at UTAS?

Some University of Tasmania courses and units are offered online. Check the course information for study modes.

Should I visit UTAS before applying?

Visiting campuses or attending information events can help you decide. The University runs events for students and parents through the year.

Who should I contact about my TCE results?

Contact your school or TASC about your TCE results, awards and exam inspections. Contact the University about entry requirements, offers and enrolment.

Who should I contact about my ATAR?

Questions about how your ATAR was calculated may involve both TASC and the University. Your ATAR Statement shows the courses and results used, which is a good starting point.

Is the ATAR Statement sent automatically?

If you are eligible for an ATAR, an ATAR Statement is included with your Year 12 end-of-year results from TASC.

How should I plan Level 3 courses for health science?

Check whether the courses you want require or assume subjects such as chemistry or biology, plan to take them across Years 11 and 12, and aim for at least five Level 3 or 4 courses so your TE Score can use your best results.

How should I plan Level 3 courses for engineering?

Engineering often assumes higher-level mathematics and physics. Spread your mathematics across Years 11 and 12 to build skills progressively, and check the University’s current requirements.

How should I plan for arts and humanities?

Arts and humanities courses often have few prerequisites. Choose Level 3 courses you enjoy across both years, since your best results from two years can count.

What should I bring to a careers adviser meeting?

Bring a list of courses you are interested in, your current and planned Level 3 and 4 courses, and any questions about the SRP, HAP or UCP.

What should I ask my careers adviser about UTAS?

Ask whether your courses meet the ATAR and TCE requirements and your preferred courses’ subject requirements, whether you should apply for the SRP, what range to expect for your ATAR and what options you have if you fall short.

Should I move to Tasmania for university?

It depends on your goals and circumstances. Check the course and campus, accommodation, costs and support, and plan for January offer timing for interstate applicants.

How do I find accommodation at UTAS?

Look into University and private accommodation options early, and check the University’s accommodation information for the campus you will attend.

Will my interstate subjects count for UTAS prerequisites?

The University notes that specific TCE Level 3 or 4 subjects, or interstate equivalents, may be required. Check how your state’s subjects match the course’s requirements.

Does my ATAR predict how I will do at UTAS?

It is only one indicator. Engagement, time management, use of feedback and support services matter a great deal once you start, and many students who enter through other routes do very well.

What is the best way to succeed in first year?

Attend and engage in classes, manage your time, use feedback, use support services early and build connections with other students and staff.

Can I apply to UTAS from overseas?

Yes. International applicants apply through the University’s international admissions process, which publishes its own guidance and recommended timeframes.

Does UTAS accept the IB?

Yes. IB applicants can apply, and first-round offers to IB applicants are made progressively in January.

Is there a cost to apply to UTAS?

Check the University’s current application information for any fees. Application processes and costs can change from year to year.

Do I need to submit my ATAR to UTAS myself?

For Tasmanian Year 12 students, the University receives results through the Tasmanian system. Interstate applicants should check how their results are provided to the University.

Can I apply to UTAS before my results are known?

Yes. You can apply during Year 12, including through the Schools Recommendation Program, and ATAR-based offers follow once results are released.

What if I am unsure what to study?

Choose a flexible course that interests you, talk to your careers adviser and the University, and remember that many students change direction after starting.

Is it common to change course at UTAS?

Many students change courses, and universities usually allow internal transfers based on university results. Check the requirements for the course you want to move into.

Can I get help choosing a course at UTAS?

Yes. The University’s schools relationship and admissions teams, along with its information events, can help you explore courses.

Do UTAS courses have quotas?

Some courses have limited places. The University’s key dates refer to non-quota courses for some deadlines, which suggests that quota courses may have different arrangements. Check each course’s information.

Where can I see UTAS’s undergraduate admission requirements?

On the University’s website, which sets out undergraduate admission requirements for domestic Year 12 students, including alternative pathways.
