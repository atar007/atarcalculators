SATAC explained

If you study in South Australia or the Northern Territory, SATAC is the body that turns your SACE results into an ATAR and handles your university applications. Here is what SATAC does, in plain English.

SATAC, the South Australian Tertiary Admissions Centre, scales SACE results, calculates the ATAR and manages university applications for both South Australia and the Northern Territory. The SACE Board sets and assesses the courses and awards the SACE; SATAC then scales those results, adds your best subjects into a university aggregate, and ranks it into an ATAR. Because SA and NT both use SATAC, their ATARs are directly comparable.

Key takeaways

  • SATAC is the South Australian Tertiary Admissions Centre.
  • It calculates the ATAR for both SA and the NT.
  • It scales SACE results and runs university applications.
  • The SACE Board sets and marks the courses and awards the SACE.
  • Your ATAR comes from a university aggregate of your best scaled subjects.
  • SA and NT ATARs are directly comparable, because both use SATAC.

Why SATAC exists

Universities need a fair, consistent way to compare the thousands of students who apply each year, across many different combinations of subjects. A central admissions centre does that job: it scales results, builds a common rank, and manages applications so that students apply once rather than to each institution separately. In South Australia and the Northern Territory, that centre is SATAC.

What SATAC is not

  • It is not the body that sets or marks your subjects; that is the SACE Board.
  • It is not a university; it processes applications on behalf of universities and TAFE SA.
  • It is not limited to South Australia; it serves the Northern Territory too.

SATAC in the national system

SATAC is one of Australia’s tertiary admissions centres, alongside UAC in NSW and the ACT, VTAC in Victoria, QTAC in Queensland, TISC in Western Australia and the University of Tasmania as the Tasmanian centre. These centres work together so that the ATAR means the same thing across the country, and they report national offer figures each year.

What is SATAC?

SATAC stands for the South Australian Tertiary Admissions Centre. It is the organisation that processes tertiary admissions and calculates the ATAR for students in South Australia and the Northern Territory.

In most states there is a central admissions centre that turns senior-school results into a university rank. For SA and the NT, that centre is SATAC. It sits between your SACE results and your university offer.

So when SA or NT students ask who works out their ATAR and where their university offers come from, the answer to both is SATAC.

SATAC’s two roles in detail

SATAC describes itself as the body that calculates the scores and ranks used for tertiary entrance selection, including the university aggregate and the ATAR, and that processes applications for universities and TAFE SA across South Australia and the Northern Territory. Those two roles sit at the heart of every SA and NT student’s journey to university.

  1. Calculating the ATAR. SATAC scales each Stage 2 SACE or NTCET subject, builds a university aggregate from your best results, and ranks that aggregate into an ATAR. It sends your Tertiary Entrance Statement, showing your aggregate and ATAR, with your Year 12 results.
  2. Processing applications. SATAC runs the online application system for its member universities and TAFE SA. You list your course preferences, and SATAC makes offers on the institutions’ behalf in rounds.

Who SATAC serves

SATAC processes applications for the universities in South Australia and the Northern Territory and for TAFE SA, and it calculates the aggregate and ATAR for SACE and NTCET students. It also processes selected equity scholarships on behalf of the University of South Australia.

A sense of scale

SATAC handles a large volume of work each year. In the 2024/2025 cycle it responded to more than 39,000 enquiries by phone and email, with January the peak period. In January 2025, the national admissions centres reported that SATAC, covering South Australia and the NT, had made 32,543 offers for Semester 1 2025 courses.

SATAC and TAFE SA

SATAC processes applications for TAFE SA as well as for universities, so you can apply for both through one centre.

One application

You can apply for university and TAFE SA courses through SATAC. Undergraduate applicants list up to six preferences, and TAFE SA applicants up to three.

TAFE SA selection

TAFE SA courses have their own entry requirements, which may include a selection score rather than an ATAR. The flexible option in the SATAC system also contributes to a TAFE SA Selection Score for some courses.

TAFE as a pathway

TAFE SA can be a pathway to university. Completing a TAFE qualification can provide a basis for admission to a degree and sometimes credit towards it. If you are interested in both, ask about pathway arrangements.

Applying

Apply through the SATAC website, list your TAFE SA preferences and follow the same offer-round process. SATAC’s guides explain TAFE SA entry in detail.

For parents and carers

  • SATAC is the admissions centre. It scales results, calculates the ATAR and makes offers for SA and NT universities and TAFE SA.
  • The SACE Board runs the courses. It sets and assesses subjects and awards the SACE and NTCET.
  • The aggregate is out of 90. Your child’s best 90 credits, built from three 20-credit subjects plus a flexible option, form the aggregate behind the ATAR.
  • The ATAR is a rank. An ATAR of 80 means your child finished ahead of about 80 per cent of the age group, not that they scored 80 per cent.
  • Adjustments affect the selection rank. Bonus points are added to the aggregate for particular courses; the TES does not show the adjusted ATAR.
  • There are many pathways. Foundation programs, the STAT, VET and tertiary transfer mean a place is available even if the ATAR is not what your child hoped for.

The best help is practical: encourage steady work, help check course prerequisites, support a sensible preference list and keep perspective.

Adult and non-school-leaver entry

Many university students in South Australia and the Northern Territory are not coming straight from Year 12. SATAC and the universities provide routes for adults and other non-school leavers.

What can be considered

  • Previous Year 12 results. An ATAR you received in the past does not expire.
  • Previous tertiary study. University or other higher education study is usually assessed on its results.
  • VET qualifications. Some qualifications provide a basis for admission.
  • Work and life experience. Some courses consider relevant experience.
  • The STAT. Many courses accept the STAT for eligible applicants.
  • Foundation and enabling programs. Completing one can provide a basis for admission.

Adult entry

Universities publish adult entry routes for applicants who have been out of school for a period. These often consider a combination of experience, previous study and tests such as the STAT.

Applying

Non-school leavers apply through SATAC in the same way as school leavers, listing their preferences. SATAC and the universities can advise on which route suits your background.

What does SATAC do?

SATAC has two main jobs. First, it takes your SACE results, scales them, and calculates your ATAR. Second, it manages the university and TAFE application and offer process for South Australia and the Northern Territory.

On the ATAR side, SATAC scales your Stage 2 subject results, adds your best subjects into a university aggregate, and ranks that aggregate into an ATAR. On the applications side, it collects your preferences and makes offers on behalf of the institutions.

So SATAC is both the calculator of your rank and the gateway to your offers, across two territories.

A rule of thumb

If your question is about a subject, a grade, an exam or the SACE itself, it is a SACE Board question. If it is about scaling, your aggregate, your ATAR, an application or an offer, it is a SATAC question. Your results come from the SACE Board; your rank and your offers come from SATAC.

Adjustment factors and the ATAR

SATAC’s member universities offer adjustment factors, sometimes called bonus points, which are added to your university aggregate to boost your rank for particular courses.

How they work

Adjustments are made to your university aggregate, which boosts your ATAR for selection purposes. Importantly, your Tertiary Entrance Statement does not show your adjusted ATAR; you contact the relevant university or SATAC for that information.

Kinds of adjustment

Universities set their own schemes, which can include adjustments for particular subjects, for location or circumstances, and for other criteria. Because each university’s scheme differs, the adjustments available to you can vary by institution and course.

Selection ranks

Your selection rank for a course is your ATAR plus any adjustments you are eligible for at that institution. Course cut-offs are usually expressed as selection ranks, so compare your likely selection rank, not just your ATAR, with a course’s requirements.

Where to check

SATAC publishes adjustment factor information, and each university explains its own schemes. Our guides to ATAR bonus points and the selection rank calculator can help.

What the SACE Board does

The SACE Board of South Australia is responsible for the senior secondary certificate and its assessment. Its work produces the results that SATAC scales.

Setting subjects

The SACE Board sets the Stage 1 and Stage 2 subjects that make up the SACE and NTCET, including the Tertiary Admissions Subjects approved for tertiary entrance.

Overseeing assessment

Stage 2 subjects combine school assessment, worth 70 per cent, with external assessment, worth 30 per cent. The SACE Board moderates school assessment so that grading is consistent with each subject’s performance standards across schools.

Awarding the certificate

The SACE Board awards the SACE in South Australia and the NTCET in the Northern Territory. To complete the SACE, students meet requirements including the Personal Learning Plan, literacy and numeracy, the Research Project and a set number of Stage 2 credits at the required grades.

Issuing results

The SACE Board issues subject grades and results, which students can access through SACE Students Online. SATAC uses these results to calculate scaled scores, aggregates and ATARs.

Working with SATAC

The SACE Board and SATAC work together each year, with the SACE Board providing results and SATAC calculating and reporting the aggregate and ATAR. The two bodies also publish complementary information about tertiary entrance.

Common misunderstandings about SATAC

“The SACE Board gives me my ATAR”

The SACE Board sets and assesses your subjects and awards the certificate. SATAC scales your results, calculates your aggregate and ATAR, and makes offers.

“My aggregate is my ATAR”

Your aggregate is a score out of 90. Your ATAR is a rank out of 99.95, derived by ranking your aggregate against other students. They are different numbers.

“All my subjects count equally”

Your aggregate uses your best 90 credits, built from three 20-credit subjects plus a flexible option. A weaker subject outside your best results may count for little or nothing.

“My TES shows my adjusted ATAR”

Your Tertiary Entrance Statement shows your ATAR but not any adjustments. Contact the university or SATAC for your adjusted selection rank.

“A SA ATAR is worth less interstate”

The ATAR is national. A SA or NT ATAR is used at the same value across Australia.

“If I miss the ATAR I need, I cannot go to university”

Many undergraduate courses offer pathways other than the ATAR, including foundation programs, the STAT, VET and tertiary transfer.

Precluded combinations and counting restrictions

Two sets of SATAC rules shape which results can count: precluded combinations and counting restrictions.

Precluded combinations

Some pairs of subjects are so closely related that SATAC does not allow both to count in full towards your aggregate. These are precluded combinations. If you take two such subjects, only one may count fully, or they may be treated in a particular way under the rules.

Counting restrictions

Counting restrictions limit how certain subjects or kinds of study contribute to your aggregate. For example, Recognised Studies are limited to 20 credits, and some subjects may have specific rules about how they can be counted.

Why these rules exist

The rules keep the aggregate fair, so that students cannot gain an advantage by taking several very similar subjects, and so that the aggregate reflects a genuine breadth of study.

How to check

SATAC publishes its precluded-combination and counting rules in its Tertiary Entrance booklet. If you take several related subjects, check the rules or ask your school or SATAC how your combination will be treated.

Why it matters when choosing subjects

Knowing the rules in advance helps you avoid a situation where two subjects you worked hard in cannot both count. It is worth checking before you finalise your Stage 2 choices.

SATAC vs the SACE Board

Students often confuse SATAC with the SACE Board, but they do different things. The SACE Board of South Australia sets the SACE subjects, oversees the assessments, and awards the SACE certificate.

SATAC comes after. It takes the results the SACE Board produces, scales them, and turns them into an ATAR, then handles applications. In short: the SACE Board runs the courses and assessments; SATAC turns the results into an ATAR and an offer.

So the two bodies are a relay. The SACE Board hands over your assessed results, and SATAC converts them into the rank universities use. They are separate organisations with separate roles.

The SACE and the NTCET

South Australian students complete the South Australian Certificate of Education (SACE). Northern Territory students complete the Northern Territory Certificate of Education and Training (NTCET), which uses the SACE framework. Because the two certificates share the same structure and are overseen by the SACE Board, it is efficient for one admissions centre to calculate the ATAR and process applications for both.

Consistency across the two territories

Because SA and NT students study the same curriculum and are scaled and ranked by SATAC through the same process, an ATAR from South Australia and an ATAR from the Northern Territory are produced identically. Neither is harder to earn than the other, and both sit on the same national scale.

What this means for students

If you move between South Australia and the Northern Territory during senior school, your SACE or NTCET study carries over within the same system. And wherever you complete it, your ATAR is calculated by SATAC and accepted across Australia.

Your Tertiary Entrance Statement explained

The Tertiary Entrance Statement (TES) is the document SATAC issues to eligible students. Understanding it helps you see exactly how your ATAR was built.

What it shows

The TES shows your university aggregate and ATAR, calculated using scaled scores. It lists all Stage 2 SACE subjects that can contribute to a university aggregate, with their credit values and scaled scores, and shows any approved Recognised Studies under a separate heading.

How to read the aggregate calculation

The TES sets out the calculation in two parts: the first 60 credits from three 20-credit Tertiary Admissions Subjects, and the flexible option of the best 30 credits. It shows the subtotal for each part and the total aggregate out of 90.

What it does not show

Your TES shows your ATAR but not any adjustment factors. If you are eligible for adjustments, your adjusted selection rank is not on the statement; you contact the university or SATAC for that.

Where to find it

SATAC sends the TES with your Year 12 results, and students can also check their TES and ATAR via SACE Students Online.

Completing the SACE or NTCET

Qualifying for the SACE or NTCET is a requirement for an ATAR, so it is worth understanding what completion involves.

The requirements

To complete the SACE, students complete a set number of credits of subjects and meet grade requirements in the compulsory elements: the Personal Learning Plan, literacy, numeracy and the Research Project, along with a set number of Stage 2 credits at the required grades. The NTCET uses the same framework.

Stage 1 and Stage 2

The SACE is usually completed over two years. Stage 1, generally in Year 11, includes compulsory elements such as the Personal Learning Plan. Stage 2, generally in Year 12, includes the subjects and the Research Project that contribute to the ATAR.

Why completion matters for the ATAR

You must qualify for the SACE or NTCET to receive a university aggregate and ATAR. Completing the certificate, and the right pattern of Tertiary Admissions Subjects, is therefore the foundation of ATAR eligibility.

Where to check the rules

The SACE Board publishes the full completion requirements, and SATAC publishes the tertiary entrance rules. Check both, or ask your school, when planning your subjects.

Why SA and NT share SATAC

The Northern Territory does not run its own separate senior certificate or admissions centre. NT students study the SACE, the same certificate as South Australia, so it makes sense for the same body to handle both.

Because both territories use the SACE and SATAC, their students are scaled and ranked the same way. This is why a SA ATAR and an NT ATAR are produced by an identical process.

So sharing SATAC is not unusual; it reflects that SA and the NT share the same curriculum. One system serves both, which keeps their results consistent.

What scaling does in South Australia

The SACE Board describes scaling as the mathematical process that provides a fair basis for comparing results across different subjects. SATAC applies it to all Stage 2 SACE subjects that can contribute to a university aggregate, and to any approved Recognised Studies.

Why only scaled scores count

Only the scaled scores from Tertiary Admissions Subjects and Recognised Studies can be used in the calculation of a university aggregate. Your raw grades are converted into scaled scores first, so that a result in one subject can be compared fairly with a result in another.

How a scaled score looks

Each 20-credit Stage 2 subject produces a scaled score out of 20, and each 10-credit subject, such as the Research Project, a scaled score out of 10. On your Tertiary Entrance Statement, SATAC lists each subject with its credit value and scaled score. A student’s statement might show, for example, Biology (20 credits) at 17.10 and a 10-credit Research Project at 9.50.

What scaling means for you

  • The same grade in two subjects can produce different scaled scores.
  • Scaling does not change your position within a subject.
  • Scaling is recalculated each year, so past patterns are a guide, not a guarantee.
  • Even a scaled score of 0.0 can be used in the aggregate.

Scaling in South Australia, in more depth

What scaling compares

Scaling compares how students in each subject perform across all their subjects. If a subject’s students tend to be strong across the board, the subject’s results are placed relatively high on the common scale; if the cohort is broad, they are placed lower. The aim is that a scaled score represents a similar level of achievement whichever subject it comes from.

Why scaling is needed

Without scaling, students could gain or lose simply by their choice of subjects. Scaling removes that incentive, so you can choose Stage 2 subjects based on your interests and strengths rather than trying to game the system.

Scaling varies along the range

Scaling is applied along the whole range of results in a subject, so its effect can differ at the top, middle and bottom. That is why it is better to look at scaling near your own likely result than at a single headline figure.

Why SATAC does not publish per-subject scaled scores in advance

SATAC assigns scaled scores each year based on that year’s students, and it advises against choosing subjects on the basis of historical scaling. Your own scaled scores appear on your Tertiary Entrance Statement after results. This is one reason ATAR estimates for SA and the NT are approximate.

What scaling means for your choices

Choose Stage 2 subjects you are good at and interested in. A strong result in a subject you enjoy usually contributes more to your aggregate than a weak result in a subject chosen only for its scaling.

Why your ATAR differs from your grades

Students are often surprised that their ATAR does not match their raw grades. Three features of the SATAC system explain the difference.

Scaling

Your raw grades are converted into scaled scores that place results from different subjects on a common scale. A high grade in one subject can produce a different scaled score from the same grade in another.

The best-90-credits rule

Your aggregate uses only your best 90 credits under SATAC’s rules, so not every result counts, and the mix of full and half contributions in the flexible option shapes your total.

The conversion to a rank

Your aggregate is ranked against other students to produce your ATAR. Because the ATAR is a position in the group, it depends on how everyone performed, not just on your own scores.

What this means

Read your ATAR as a rank, not a mark. Your Tertiary Entrance Statement shows your scaled scores and aggregate, which explain how your results combined into your ATAR.

How SATAC scales your results

SATAC scales each Stage 2 subject so that a score in one subject is comparable to a score in another. Scaling reflects how strong the students taking a subject are across all their subjects, not how hard the subject feels.

A subject scales up when its students are strong across the board, and down when the cohort is broad. This keeps the ATAR fair, so your rank does not depend on which subjects you happened to choose. See how scaling works.

The result is a scaled score for each of your subjects. Those scaled scores, not your raw marks, are what SATAC uses to build your aggregate and ATAR.

How the 90-credit aggregate is built

SATAC explains that you need 90 credits of study to calculate a university aggregate, and that the aggregate is the best possible outcome from your eligible results. It is built in two parts.

The first 60 credits

The first 60 credits are the scaled scores from three 20-credit Tertiary Admissions Subjects. Normally 10-credit subjects do not count towards the first 60 credits, but some 10-credit subjects, when studied in valid pairs, can substitute for a 20-credit subject.

The flexible option: the final 30 credits

The flexible option is the best 30 credits of scaled scores from a combination of:

  • the scaled score of a 20-credit Tertiary Admissions Subject;
  • half the scaled score of one or more 20-credit Tertiary Admissions Subjects;
  • 10-credit Tertiary Admissions Subjects, such as the Research Project; and
  • approved Recognised Studies, up to a maximum of 20 credits.

The best possible outcome

SATAC calculates your aggregate as the best possible outcome from these options, within its counting restrictions and precluded-combination rules. That means you are not penalised for taking more than the minimum; SATAC uses the combination that gives you the highest aggregate.

In short
Three 20-credit subjects for the first 60 credits, plus the best 30 credits from your remaining results for the flexible option, give a university aggregate out of 90.00, which is converted to an ATAR.

VET and Recognised Studies

One distinctive feature of the SATAC system is the flexible option, which can include study beyond standard Stage 2 subjects.

What Recognised Studies are

Recognised Studies are studies other than standard Tertiary Admissions Subjects that SATAC can include in your flexible option, up to a maximum of 20 credits. They include completed VET qualifications and some university subjects.

How VET is scaled

SATAC assigns scaled score equivalents to Recognised Studies to keep the ATAR calculation fair. For a completed VET qualification, the scaled score is based on the average of the first 70 credits of Tertiary Admissions Subjects used in the calculation of your university aggregate. A Certificate III or higher can contribute towards entry.

University subjects in the flexible option

Some students include university subjects in their flexible option. These are not pre-approved and must be submitted to SATAC for approval. SATAC generally approves degree-level subjects on application, provided they are not introductory subjects, and assigns them scaled score equivalents.

The 20-credit limit

Recognised Studies can contribute a maximum of 20 credits to your aggregate, all within the 30-credit flexible option. More than one type of Recognised Studies can be used, within that limit.

Tip
If you are completing VET or university study alongside your SACE, check with SATAC how it can count towards your flexible option, and whether it needs to be submitted for approval.

Choosing Stage 2 subjects with the ATAR in mind

Take enough Tertiary Admissions Subjects

Your aggregate needs at least 60 credits from 20-credit Tertiary Admissions Subjects, so plan to take at least three, and usually four or five, full TAS at Stage 2. Taking more than the minimum gives SATAC more results to choose your best from.

Choose subjects you can do well in

Because your aggregate uses your best scaled results, the subjects in which you are likely to achieve your highest grades usually contribute most. Interest and strength matter more than scaling.

Remember the Research Project

The compulsory 10-credit Research Project can contribute through the flexible option, so treat it seriously.

Check prerequisites

Some university courses require particular Stage 2 subjects. Check the requirements for courses you are interested in before finalising your Stage 2 choices.

Watch precluded combinations

Some closely related subjects cannot both count in full. Check SATAC’s precluded-combination and counting rules if you take several related subjects.

Consider Recognised Studies

If you are completing VET or university study, check whether it can count as Recognised Studies in your flexible option, within the 20-credit limit.

More worked university aggregates

Seeing a few more examples helps show how the flexible option works. These are illustrative, with invented scaled scores.

Example 1: five 20-credit subjects plus the Research Project

Ava completes five 20-credit Tertiary Admissions Subjects and the 10-credit Research Project. Her three strongest 20-credit subjects form the first 60 credits. For the flexible option, SATAC takes the best 30 credits from her two remaining 20-credit subjects (or half-contributions of them) and her Research Project, choosing the combination that gives the highest total. Because she has more than the minimum, SATAC has several combinations to choose from.

Example 2: four 20-credit subjects plus the Research Project

Ben completes four 20-credit subjects and the Research Project. His three best 20-credit subjects form the first 60 credits. His flexible option draws on his fourth 20-credit subject and his Research Project, up to 30 credits. This is a common pattern.

Example 3: including a VET qualification

Chloe completes three 20-credit subjects, the Research Project and a Certificate III. Her three 20-credit subjects form the first 60 credits. Her flexible option includes her Research Project and her VET qualification as Recognised Studies, scaled using the average of the first 70 credits of her Tertiary Admissions Subjects, up to the 20-credit Recognised Studies limit.

Example 4: using half-contributions

Daniel has several strong 20-credit subjects. For his flexible option, SATAC may use half the scaled score of one or more 20-credit subjects to make up the best 30 credits. This flexibility is why the option is called “flexible”.

What the examples show

In every case, SATAC builds the aggregate from the combination that gives the highest total, within its rules. Taking a full, varied program gives you more combinations and protects you if one result is lower than expected.

The university aggregate

Once your subjects are scaled, SATAC combines your best scaled results into a university aggregate. The aggregate is drawn from 90 credits of your strongest Stage 2 study: three 20-credit Tertiary Admissions Subjects for the first 60 credits, plus a flexible option of the best 30 credits from your remaining results, so your best subjects carry your result.

Your compulsory Research Project can contribute to this aggregate, alongside your Tertiary Admission Subjects. Because only your best results count, attempting a broad program does not penalise you; SATAC uses the combination that works most in your favour.

So the aggregate captures your strongest scaled subjects. The higher your aggregate, the higher your ATAR, since the ATAR is your aggregate expressed as a rank.

How the aggregate becomes a rank

SATAC reports your university aggregate on a scale of 0 to 90.00, in intervals of 0.1. It then ranks every student’s aggregate and derives a percentile rank, comparing your position with other students who achieved a similar aggregate or higher. That percentile position is expressed as your ATAR, on the national scale from 0 to 99.95.

What an ATAR of 80 means

An ATAR of 80 means you finished ahead of about 80 per cent of the relevant age group. Because the ATAR is a rank, it can differ from your aggregate and from your raw subject grades. Two students with the same aggregate receive the same ATAR, but the same aggregate can map to a slightly different ATAR in different years, because the ranking depends on that year’s students.

The conversion table

SATAC publishes a SACE and NTCET university aggregate to ATAR conversion table each year, showing how aggregates related to ATARs for that year. Because it is published after results, calculators use past tables to estimate your ATAR.

Your Tertiary Entrance Statement

SATAC sends eligible students a Tertiary Entrance Statement showing their university aggregate and ATAR, along with the scaled scores used. It is the document that records your tertiary entrance results.

ATAR eligibility in SA and the NT

Not every SACE or NTCET student receives an ATAR. To obtain a university aggregate and an ATAR, you must meet SATAC’s requirements.

The requirements

To obtain a university aggregate and ATAR, you must qualify for the SACE or NTCET, comply with the rules on precluded combinations and counting restrictions, and complete at least 90 credits of study at Stage 2, of which at least 60 credits must be from 20-credit Tertiary Admissions Subjects. A maximum of 20 credits can be Recognised Studies. The subjects used can come from a maximum of three attempts, which need not be in consecutive years.

What a Tertiary Admissions Subject is

SATAC describes Tertiary Admissions Subjects as SACE Stage 2 subjects approved by SATAC’s member institutions as suitable preparation for tertiary study. You must complete a minimum number of TAS credits to be eligible for an ATAR.

The Research Project

The Research Project is a compulsory 10-credit Stage 2 subject for the SACE. Its scaled score can contribute to your aggregate through the flexible option, alongside your Tertiary Admissions Subjects.

If you are not eligible

Students who do not receive an ATAR can still reach university. SATAC reminds students who may not have received the ATAR they hoped for that many undergraduate courses offer admission pathways other than the ATAR.

From aggregate to ATAR, in more detail

Ranking the aggregates

Once every eligible student’s aggregate is calculated, SATAC ranks the aggregates and works out each student’s percentile position. SATAC describes this as deriving a percentile rank based on your aggregate and comparing your rank with other students who achieved a similar aggregate or higher.

Relating it to the age group

The ATAR reflects your position relative to your age group, not just among students who received an ATAR. That is why the typical ATAR among recipients sits above 50: students who receive an ATAR tend to be stronger academically than the whole age group.

The conversion table

SATAC publishes a SACE and NTCET university aggregate to ATAR conversion table each year. It shows, for that year, which aggregates corresponded to which ATARs. For example, a published example shows how an aggregate of 65.6 out of 90 converts to an ATAR. Because the table is published after results, calculators use past tables to estimate your ATAR.

Why the same aggregate can give different ATARs

Because the conversion depends on the current year’s students, the same aggregate can correspond to a slightly different ATAR in different years. In a large cohort the differences are usually small, but they are one reason no one can know an exact ATAR before results.

What this means for you

Read your ATAR as a rank. To understand how it was built, look at your scaled scores and aggregate on your Tertiary Entrance Statement, and use the year’s conversion table to see how your aggregate mapped to your ATAR.

From aggregate to ATAR

The final step is turning your aggregate into an ATAR. SATAC ranks every student’s aggregate and expresses your position as an ATAR, a number up to 99.95 that shows the percentage of students you finished above.

So an ATAR of 80 means you finished ahead of about 80 percent of the relevant age group. The ATAR is a rank, not a mark, which is why it can differ from your raw scores.

This national rank is what universities across Australia use, so a SA or NT ATAR is directly comparable to an ATAR from any other state.

Precluded combinations in South Australia

SATAC treats certain pairs of subjects as overlapping so heavily that it will not count both at full value toward your aggregate. These are called precluded combinations. The idea is that two subjects covering much the same material should not both earn credit as if they were separate achievements. If you take a precluded pair, SATAC counts one of them and sets the other aside, or counts it only as Recognised Studies, depending on the rule for that pair.

Precluded combinations most often involve closely related mathematics or language subjects, or a subject and its background-speakers or continuers variant. The practical risk is that a student plans a load expecting five countable subjects, only to find two of them are a precluded pair, leaving effectively four. Before you lock in your Stage 2 subjects, check the current precluded-combinations list so your load gives SATAC the full set of countable results you expect.

How the counting rules fit together

Put the rules in order and the aggregate becomes easy to picture. First, SATAC identifies your Tertiary Admissions Subjects and takes your best three 20-credit results for the first 60 credits. Then it builds the flexible final 30 credits from your best remaining results, which can include a fourth and part of a fifth subject, the Research Project, and a capped amount of Recognised Studies. Precluded combinations are applied so overlapping subjects do not both count at full value. The result is a 90-credit aggregate drawn from your strongest eligible study.

Because the aggregate rewards your best results rather than every result equally, a single weaker subject rarely sinks a strong overall profile, provided you have enough countable study for SATAC to set it aside. That is why planning for a little more than 90 credits of Tertiary Admissions Subjects gives you a safety margin.

What a credit means in the SACE

SACE subjects are measured in credits rather than in “units” or “subjects”, and the number of credits tells you how much a subject counts. A full-year Stage 2 subject is worth 20 credits; a semester-length subject is worth 10 credits. To complete the SACE you need 200 credits in total across Stage 1 and Stage 2, and you must meet the literacy, numeracy, Personal Learning Plan and Research Project requirements along the way. When SATAC builds your university aggregate, it works in credits too, which is why the aggregate is described as being drawn from 90 credits rather than from a fixed number of subjects.

Thinking in credits helps when you plan a load. Four 20-credit Tertiary Admissions Subjects plus a 10-credit subject give you 90 credits of Stage 2 study, which is the amount SATAC draws on for the university aggregate. Carrying a fifth full subject gives SATAC more to choose from, because the flexible 30-credit portion of the aggregate is taken from your best remaining results after the first three 20-credit subjects are counted.

Plan in credits. If you aim for at least 90 credits of Stage 2 Tertiary Admissions Subjects, with a little more in reserve, SATAC has a full aggregate to build and some room to pick your strongest results.

Tertiary Admissions Subjects and the rest

Not every Stage 2 subject counts the same way toward the aggregate. SATAC distinguishes Tertiary Admissions Subjects (often shortened to TAS) from other recognised study. The first 60 credits of your aggregate must come from three 20-credit Tertiary Admissions Subjects. The flexible final 30 credits can include a fourth and part of a fifth Tertiary Admissions Subject, your Research Project, and a capped amount of Recognised Studies, including some VET and some interstate or international study, scaled onto the SACE scale.

Because the first 60 credits are locked to Tertiary Admissions Subjects, it pays to make sure at least three of your strongest full-year subjects are Tertiary Admissions Subjects. A student who loads up on subjects that are recognised but not classified as Tertiary Admissions Subjects can find that those results only ever reach the flexible 30-credit portion, and only if they are among the best remaining results.

How the Research Project fits the aggregate

The Research Project is a compulsory part of the SACE, and students sometimes assume it cannot help their ATAR. In South Australia the Research Project can contribute to the university aggregate through the flexible 30-credit portion, provided it is among your best remaining results after the first three 20-credit Tertiary Admissions Subjects are counted. A 10-credit Research Project that scales well can lift an aggregate where a weaker fourth subject would not.

This is one reason not to treat the Research Project as a box to tick. A strong result there is a result that can count, and because it is compulsory, every student has it available. Students who put genuine effort into the Research Project give SATAC one more strong result to draw on when it builds the flexible portion of the aggregate.

Recognised Studies, VET and the cap

Recognised Studies covers learning that sits outside the standard Stage 2 subjects but is still recognised for the SACE, including some vocational education and training and some interstate or overseas study. A capped amount of Recognised Studies, commonly up to 20 credits, can count toward the flexible portion of the aggregate, scaled onto the SACE scale so it can be compared fairly with ordinary subjects.

The cap matters. Because only a limited amount of Recognised Studies can count, a student cannot build an entire aggregate out of VET credits. The system is designed so that the core of the aggregate is Tertiary Admissions Subjects, with Recognised Studies adding to, rather than replacing, that core. If you are combining a VET qualification with your SACE and hoping it lifts your ATAR, check in advance how much of it can count and how it will be scaled.

Check before you count on it. How a specific VET qualification is recognised and scaled can change. Confirm the current treatment with SATAC rather than assuming a certificate will count toward your aggregate.

A worked example: a balanced load

Imagine a student who takes five Stage 2 Tertiary Admissions Subjects plus the Research Project. SATAC takes the best three 20-credit subjects for the first 60 credits of the aggregate. For the flexible final 30 credits it looks at the remaining results: the fourth subject, part of the fifth, and the Research Project, and it counts the best of those up to 30 credits, after scaling. A weaker fifth subject that would have dragged the total down is simply not among the best remaining results, so it sits outside the aggregate.

The lesson is that a broad, balanced load protects a student. With more than 90 credits of countable study, SATAC can build the aggregate from the strongest results and quietly leave a weak one aside, rather than being forced to count it because there is nothing better to use.

A worked example: relying on scaling

Now imagine two students with similar raw results, one in subjects that historically scale up and one in subjects that historically scale down. After SATAC applies scaling, the first student’s aggregate can sit higher, because strong results in competitively studied subjects are worth more on the common scale. This is not a trick; it is the scaling step doing its job of making results from different subjects comparable.

The temptation is to chase “high-scaling” subjects regardless of ability. That usually backfires, because scaling rewards a strong result, and a strong result is easier to earn in a subject that suits you. The better strategy is to choose subjects you can do well in, keep an eye on how they tend to scale, and aim for genuine strength rather than gaming the scale.

Scaling is not published in advance. SATAC does not release each subject’s scaling before results, so any estimate of how a subject will scale is informed guesswork. Treat a calculator’s figure as a guide, not a guarantee.

Common mistakes to avoid

A few errors come up every year, and all of them are avoidable. The first is treating an estimate as a result. A calculator gives a range built on assumptions about scaling that SATAC has not published, so a student who banks on a single number can be caught out when the official ATAR differs. Read an estimate as a band, and plan preferences across that band.

The second is listing too few preferences. A student who lists only a dream course, with nothing below it, has no safety net if the cut-off rises or their ATAR lands lower than hoped. Listing a spread, first choice first, costs nothing and protects you, because SATAC always makes the best offer it can from your ordered list.

The third is missing a prerequisite. A high ATAR does not rescue an application to a course that requires a specific subject you did not take. Check prerequisites early, while you can still choose the subjects that satisfy them, rather than discovering the gap after results.

The fourth is ignoring the pathways. Students who fixate on the ATAR as the only door sometimes give up when it falls short, not realising that foundation programs, the STAT, VET and tertiary transfer lead to the same degrees. Knowing the pathways exist changes how a disappointing ATAR feels.

The deadline mistake. Missing a SATAC application or offer-response deadline is the one error that can cost a place outright. Note the key dates early and set your own reminders ahead of them.

How an SA ATAR calculator helps

An SA-specific ATAR calculator is useful precisely because it builds in the aggregate rules: best three 20-credit Tertiary Admissions Subjects for the first 60 credits, then the best remaining results up to 30 more, with scaling applied to put subjects on a common footing. A generic calculator that ignores the 90-credit structure or the SATAC scaling will give a figure that looks precise but rests on the wrong model.

Use a calculator to test choices, not to lock in a number. Try different subject combinations and different levels of results, and watch how your estimate moves. That tells you which subjects carry your estimate and where extra effort would help most. The estimate’s absolute value matters less than what it reveals about your choices.

Every state and territory has a tertiary admissions centre, and they work in much the same way: collect applications, apply the aggregate and scaling rules for their system, calculate the ATAR, and run offer rounds. SATAC covers South Australia and the Northern Territory. Victoria has VTAC, New South Wales and the ACT have UAC, Queensland has QTAC, Western Australia has TISC, and Tasmania’s university admits largely through its own processes.

The ATAR itself is national, so a rank calculated by SATAC means the same as the same rank from any other centre. What differs is the machinery behind it: the certificate, the subjects, the exact aggregate rules and the scaling. That is why an SA student uses SATAC’s rules and an interstate student uses their own centre’s, even though both end up on the one ATAR scale.

The STAT in detail

The Special Tertiary Admissions Test, usually called the STAT, is a test of general reasoning and comprehension rather than of specific subject knowledge. It gives applicants who do not have a competitive ATAR, or no ATAR at all, a way to demonstrate the kind of thinking university study demands. Many South Australian courses accept a STAT result as a basis for admission or as a supplement to other credentials, which makes it a central part of the pathway landscape.

Because the STAT measures skills rather than content, you cannot cram facts for it the way you might for a subject exam, but you can prepare. Practising the question types, working on timed reading and reasoning, and sitting sample tests all help you perform closer to your real ability on the day. The test rewards clear, careful thinking under time pressure.

The STAT is especially useful for mature applicants, for students whose ATAR fell short of a course, and for anyone returning to study after time away. It is worth checking early whether your preferred course accepts the STAT, and how it weighs the result, so you can decide whether sitting it is worthwhile for your situation.

Check acceptance first. Not every course uses the STAT, and those that do may weigh it differently. Confirm with SATAC and the university before you register to sit it.

Foundation and enabling programs in depth

Foundation studies and enabling programs are structured courses that build the academic skills a degree requires and, on successful completion, provide a basis for admission. In South Australia, providers such as the University of Adelaide College and UniSA College run programs of this kind, each leading toward degrees at the associated university. They suit students who want a supported, structured route rather than a single test.

These programs do more than tick an entry box. They teach academic writing, study skills and subject foundations, so students who come through them often arrive at their degree better prepared than they would have been straight from a borderline ATAR. For a student who struggled in Year 12 but is capable of university work, an enabling program can be a stronger start than squeezing into a course on a bare rank.

Entry requirements, length and the degrees they lead into vary by program, so the sensible first step is to contact the university offering your preferred course and ask which foundation or enabling program feeds into it and what that program requires.

TAFE SA as a route to a degree

TAFE SA sits alongside the universities in the SATAC process, and for many students it is a route into a degree rather than a dead end. A completed TAFE SA qualification, such as a diploma, can provide a basis for admission to a related degree and sometimes credit that shortens the degree. Through SATAC you can list TAFE SA preferences alongside university ones, so you do not have to choose one world or the other at application time.

This matters for students whose ATAR falls short of a direct university offer. Starting at TAFE SA, performing well, and then transferring into a degree with credit is a well-worn path, and the university judges the transfer on your TAFE SA results rather than on your Year 12 ATAR. For some students that is a more forgiving and better-prepared route than direct entry.

List both. Because SATAC lets you list university and TAFE SA preferences together, you can keep a direct-entry hope and a TAFE SA pathway alive in the one application.

Tertiary transfer in practice

Tertiary transfer lets a student move into a course on the basis of university study already completed, rather than on their ATAR. A student who enrols in a related course, does well in the first year or semester, and then applies to transfer is assessed on that university performance. For courses where the ATAR cut-off is the main barrier, this turns a high-stakes Year 12 result into one input among several.

Universities often set aside a portion of places for transfer applicants or admit them through a quota, so the route is a planned part of the system rather than an exception. If your ATAR falls short of your target course but you can get into a related one, performing strongly there and transferring is a realistic plan worth discussing with the university early.

The key is to choose a starting course that genuinely connects to your target, so that your study and any credit carry across. A careers adviser or the university’s admissions staff can help you map a starting course to the degree you ultimately want.

Common myths about the SA system

Several beliefs circulate about the South Australian system that are worth correcting. The first is that the ATAR is a mark out of 100. It is not; it is a rank. An ATAR of 85 means you are ranked near the top 15% of your age group, not that you earned 85%. Mistaking the rank for a percentage leads students to misread both their own result and a course’s cut-off.

The second myth is that you should pick subjects purely for their scaling. Scaling rewards a strong result, and strong results are easier to earn in subjects that suit you, so chasing a high-scaling subject you dislike usually lowers rather than raises your aggregate. Choose for genuine strength, then keep scaling in mind at the margins.

The third myth is that the ATAR is the only way into university. In South Australia, as elsewhere, a large share of students enter through pathways: foundation programs, the STAT, VET and tertiary transfer. Treating the ATAR as the single door creates needless pressure and hides the routes that help when a rank falls short.

The fourth myth is that a weaker subject automatically drags down your ATAR. Because SATAC builds the aggregate from your best 90 credits, a weak result can be set aside if you have enough stronger study to replace it. The system is designed to reward your strengths, not to punish a single slip.

Rank, not mark. If you remember one thing about the ATAR, make it this: it reports where you sit relative to your age group, which is why it can look different from your subject marks.

Putting it all together

SATAC’s job is to turn your Stage 2 results into a fair, nationally comparable rank and to run the shared application and offer process for South Australia and the Northern Territory. It scales your results so subjects can be compared, builds a 90-credit aggregate from your strongest study, converts that into your ATAR, adds any adjustment factors to form your selection rank, and matches your ranked preferences to course cut-offs. Understanding each step turns a confusing process into a sequence you can plan for.

The practical takeaways are simple. Choose subjects you can do well in, keep at least three strong Tertiary Admissions Subjects, watch for precluded combinations, and give SATAC a little more than 90 credits to work with. Lodge your SATAC application on time with a realistic spread of preferences, first choice first. Check prerequisites and key dates early. And remember the pathways, so a disappointing ATAR is a detour rather than a dead end.

Choosing Stage 2 subjects strategically

Good subject selection balances three things: what you are strong in, what your target courses require, and how subjects tend to scale. Strength comes first, because scaling rewards a strong result and because you will sustain effort more easily in subjects you can do well in. Prerequisites come next, since a missing one can rule you out regardless of rank. Scaling is a tie-breaker at the margins, not the starting point.

A sensible plan is to lock in the subjects your target courses require, add the subjects you are genuinely strongest in, and then, among roughly equal options, lean toward those that tend to scale a little better. Keep enough Tertiary Admissions Subjects that at least three strong 20-credit results anchor your aggregate, and carry a little extra so SATAC can set a weak result aside. Avoid precluded combinations that quietly cost you a countable subject.

Review the plan with your careers adviser, who can flag prerequisite gaps and precluded pairs you might miss. Subject choice is the single decision over which you have the most control, and getting it right in Year 11 shapes everything SATAC can do with your results in Year 12.

Strength first, scaling last. Choose subjects you can do well in and that meet your prerequisites; use scaling only to choose between options you would be roughly equally strong in.

What to do on results day

When results and your ATAR are released, read them calmly before reacting. Compare your ATAR, plus any adjustment factors that give your selection rank, against the recent cut-offs for your preferences. If your top preference is within reach, you are well placed for a first-round offer. If it is not, this is the moment to use the preference changes SATAC allows, swapping estimates for choices based on your real rank.

If your ATAR falls short of every preference, do not treat results day as the end. Note which later rounds apply, adjust your preferences to realistic and guaranteed-entry options, and look up the pathways into your target course: foundation programs, the STAT, VET and tertiary transfer. A plan made calmly on results day beats a scramble, and the pathways mean a lower-than-hoped ATAR rarely closes a door for good.

How to read an SA ATAR estimate well

An estimate is only as good as the assumptions behind it, and the biggest assumption in any South Australian estimate is scaling. Because SATAC does not publish each subject’s scaling before results, every calculator infers it from past patterns. That means two things. First, the estimate is a range, not a point: a sensible calculator might tell you your ATAR is likely to land within a band of a few points either side. Second, the estimate is most trustworthy when your entered results are realistic, so feeding in hopeful marks produces a hopeful, unreliable figure.

The right way to use an estimate is to run it several times. Enter conservative results for a floor, likely results for a middle, and strong-but-plausible results for a ceiling. The band between the floor and the ceiling is what you plan around. If your target course sits inside that band, it is a realistic reach; if it sits well above the ceiling, you should lean on pathways and safety preferences; if it sits below the floor, you can list it with confidence and look higher as well.

Running the estimate this way also shows you the shape of your result, not just its size. You will see which subjects pull the estimate up and which hold it back, and whether a change in one subject moves the whole figure much or little. That insight is more useful than the headline number, because it tells you where effort pays off in the months before results.

Plan around the band. Treat your estimate as a floor-to-ceiling range and build your preference list across it, rather than betting everything on a single predicted number.

Where extra effort moves your ATAR most

Not every hour of study moves your ATAR equally. Because the aggregate is built from your best 90 credits and because scaling rewards strong results, effort pays off most in two places: lifting a subject from good to strong, where scaling turns the improvement into more aggregate, and shoring up the subjects that are currently among your best 90 credits, since those are the ones that actually count. Pouring time into a sixth subject that will not make your best results, or into a subject you have already maxed out, moves the figure far less.

A calculator helps you find these leverage points. Raise one subject’s result slightly and watch the estimate; if it barely moves, that subject is probably not among your counted credits or is already strong. If it jumps, that subject is both counted and scaling-sensitive, which makes it a good target for the next block of study. Repeating this across your subjects turns a vague “study harder” into a specific plan.

None of this replaces broad competence, since you cannot know for certain in advance exactly which results will count. But at the margins, when you are deciding where a limited number of study hours should go, aiming them at counted, scaling-sensitive subjects is how you get the most ATAR for your effort.

Keeping the ATAR in proportion

For all the detail, it helps to end where the system itself points: the ATAR is one rank, from one year, used mainly to allocate university places. It is central to admission, and worth planning for carefully, but it is not a verdict on your ability or a ceiling on your future. South Australia’s pathways exist precisely because a single rank cannot capture everyone’s potential, and they carry large numbers of students into the same degrees each year by routes other than a high Year 12 result.

So plan well, use a good estimate, choose subjects wisely, lodge a sensible preference list on time, and then let the process run. If your ATAR meets your goals, the path is straightforward. If it does not, the pathways are there, and they lead to the same places. Either way, understanding how SATAC builds your rank turns results day from a mystery into a step you have prepared for.

What is a university aggregate?

The university aggregate is a score reported in the range 0 to 90.00, calculated from the scaled results of your performance in Tertiary Admissions Subjects and any approved Recognised Studies. It is the basis for your ATAR.

How many credits are in a university aggregate?

You need 90 credits of study to calculate a university aggregate. The first 60 credits come from three 20-credit Tertiary Admissions Subjects, and the final 30 credits form the flexible option.

What is the flexible option?

The flexible option is the final 30 credits contributing to your university aggregate. It is the best 30 credits from a combination of 20-credit and 10-credit Tertiary Admissions Subjects, half the scaled score of 20-credit subjects, and up to 20 credits of Recognised Studies.

What is a Tertiary Admissions Subject?

A Tertiary Admissions Subject, or TAS, is a SACE Stage 2 subject approved by SATAC’s member institutions as suitable preparation for tertiary study. You must complete a minimum number of TAS credits to be eligible for an ATAR.

Does the Research Project count towards my ATAR?

Yes, it can. The Research Project is a compulsory 10-credit Stage 2 subject, and its scaled score can contribute to your university aggregate through the flexible option, alongside your Tertiary Admissions Subjects.

How is a 20-credit subject scaled?

Each 20-credit Stage 2 Tertiary Admissions Subject produces a scaled score out of 20. SATAC lists each subject with its credit value and scaled score on your Tertiary Entrance Statement.

How is a 10-credit subject scaled?

Each 10-credit subject, such as the Research Project, produces a scaled score out of 10. It can contribute to your aggregate through the flexible option.

Can 10-credit subjects count in the first 60 credits?

Normally no. The first 60 credits come from three 20-credit Tertiary Admissions Subjects. However, some 10-credit subjects, when studied in valid pairs, can substitute for a 20-credit subject.

What is the maximum university aggregate?

The university aggregate is reported on a scale of 0 to 90.00, in intervals of 0.1. It is calculated from your best 90 credits of eligible study.

How is my aggregate turned into an ATAR?

SATAC ranks every student’s aggregate and derives a percentile rank, comparing your position with other students. That position is expressed as your ATAR, on the national scale from 0 to 99.95.

What does an ATAR of 80 mean in SA?

An ATAR of 80 means you finished ahead of about 80 per cent of the relevant age group. Because the ATAR is a rank, it can differ from your aggregate and your raw subject grades.

Can the same aggregate give a different ATAR in different years?

Yes. Two students with the same aggregate in the same year receive the same ATAR, but the same aggregate can map to a slightly different ATAR in different years, because the ranking depends on that year’s students.

What is a Tertiary Entrance Statement?

The Tertiary Entrance Statement, or TES, is the document SATAC issues to eligible students. It shows your university aggregate and ATAR and lists the scaled scores used to calculate them.

Does my TES show my adjusted ATAR?

No. Your Tertiary Entrance Statement shows your ATAR but not any adjustment factors. If you are eligible for adjustments, you contact the relevant university or SATAC for your adjusted selection rank.

Where can I see my ATAR?

SATAC sends your Tertiary Entrance Statement with your Year 12 results, and you can also check your TES and ATAR via SACE Students Online.

What do I need to get an ATAR in SA or the NT?

You must qualify for the SACE or NTCET, comply with the precluded-combination and counting rules, and complete at least 90 credits of Stage 2 study, of which at least 60 credits are from 20-credit Tertiary Admissions Subjects, with at most 20 credits of Recognised Studies, in a maximum of three attempts.

Do my results have to come from one year?

No. The subjects used in your university aggregate can come from a maximum of three attempts, which need not be in consecutive years.

Can a scaled score of 0.0 count?

Yes. SATAC notes that subjects with scaled scores of 0.0 can be used in the calculation of the university aggregate.

What are precluded combinations?

Precluded combinations are pairs of closely related subjects that SATAC’s rules prevent from both counting in full towards your aggregate. SATAC’s counting restrictions and precluded-combination rules are applied when your aggregate is calculated.

Can VET count towards my SA ATAR?

Can university subjects count towards my ATAR?

Some students include university subjects in their flexible option. These are not pre-approved and must be submitted to SATAC for approval. SATAC generally approves degree-level subjects that are not introductory.

What is the maximum credit for Recognised Studies?

Recognised Studies can contribute a maximum of 20 credits to your university aggregate, all within the 30-credit flexible option. More than one type of Recognised Studies can be used, within that limit.

How does SATAC scale VET qualifications?

SATAC assigns a scaled score to a completed VET qualification based on the average of the first 70 credits of Tertiary Admissions Subjects used in the calculation of your university aggregate.

Does scaling change each year in SA?

Yes. SATAC assigns scaled scores each year based on that year’s students, and it advises against choosing subjects on the basis of historical scaling. Your own scaled scores appear on your TES after results.

Why can’t I see subject scaling in advance?

SATAC does not publish per-subject scaled scores in advance, because they depend on each year’s students. This is one reason ATAR estimates for SA and the NT are approximate.

Should I choose subjects based on scaling?

No. SATAC advises against choosing subjects on the basis of historical scaling. A strong result in a subject you are good at usually contributes more to your aggregate than a weak result in a subject chosen only for its scaling.

How many preferences can I list with SATAC?

Undergraduate applicants can list up to six preferences in a SATAC application, and TAFE SA applicants can list up to three. List your courses in the order you want them.

How does SATAC decide my offer?

SATAC matches you to the highest preference you are eligible for in each round, based on your selection rank and the course’s requirements. You may receive more than one offer across rounds.

Can I change my SATAC preferences?

Yes. You can change your preferences up to each round’s change-of-preference deadline. It is worth reviewing your list once you have your ATAR.

What is a selection rank?

Your selection rank for a course is your ATAR plus any adjustment factors you are eligible for at that institution. Course cut-offs are usually expressed as selection ranks.

Do SA universities offer bonus points?

Yes. SATAC’s member universities offer adjustment factors that are added to your university aggregate to boost your rank for particular courses. Each university sets its own scheme.

SATAC is the admissions centre owned by South Australia’s universities to run a shared application and admission process. It works closely with the SACE Board and the universities.

No. SATAC calculates the ATAR from the results the SACE Board reports. Queries about a subject result go to the SACE Board’s processes, not SATAC.

SATAC builds your university aggregate from your scaled Stage 2 results and converts it into your ATAR. It then matches your ranked preferences to course selection ranks to make offers.

Building the aggregate from your strongest results means a single weaker subject does not sink an otherwise strong profile, provided you have enough countable study for SATAC to set the weak one aside.

If you take a precluded pair, SATAC counts one at full value and sets the other aside or counts it only as Recognised Studies, so you effectively have one fewer countable subject than you might expect.

Can my ATAR go down after release?

Your ATAR is set from your results at release. It would only change if an underlying subject result changed through the SACE Board’s processes.

No. You can decline, or accept and still wait for a higher preference in a later round in most cases. Read the offer’s conditions.

Later rounds fill remaining places and can bring a higher preference if a spot opens. Keeping your preferences updated lets you benefit from later rounds.

A precluded combination is a pair of closely related subjects that SATAC’s rules prevent from both counting in full towards your aggregate. Check SATAC’s rules if you take several related subjects.

Can 10-credit subjects count towards my aggregate?

Yes, through the flexible option, and in valid pairs they can substitute for a 20-credit subject in the first 60 credits. On their own, 10-credit subjects normally do not count towards the first 60 credits.

How is the flexible option calculated?

The flexible option is the best 30 credits of scaled scores from a combination of 20-credit and 10-credit Tertiary Admissions Subjects, half the scaled score of 20-credit subjects, and up to 20 credits of Recognised Studies, chosen to give the best outcome.

What does ‘best possible outcome’ mean?

SATAC calculates your aggregate as the best possible outcome from your eligible results, within its counting and precluded-combination rules. That means it uses the combination that gives you the highest aggregate.

Do I need the Research Project for an ATAR?

The Research Project is a compulsory element of the SACE, which you must complete to qualify for the certificate and therefore to be eligible for an ATAR. Its scaled score can also contribute to your aggregate.

Can I count university subjects towards my ATAR?

Some students include university subjects in their flexible option as Recognised Studies. These must be submitted to SATAC for approval, and SATAC generally approves degree-level subjects that are not introductory.

How many credits of Recognised Studies can I use?

A maximum of 20 credits of Recognised Studies can contribute to your aggregate, all within the 30-credit flexible option. More than one type can be used, within that limit.

Is the Northern Territory ATAR calculated the same way?

Yes. NT students complete the NTCET, which uses the SACE framework, and SATAC calculates their ATAR using the same process as for SACE students.

How should I order my SATAC preferences?

Should I put a safe course first?

No. You are offered the highest preference you are eligible for, so put your genuine first choice first and safe options lower down. A list with aspirational, realistic and safe options works best.

How many preferences should I use?

Use as many as you need to cover your range. Undergraduate applicants can list up to six preferences and TAFE SA applicants three. Avoid listing several near-identical courses at the same level.

What happens if I am eligible for two preferences?

SATAC offers you the higher of the two in your list. That is why the order of your preferences matters: you receive the highest preference you qualify for.

Each state builds its aggregate differently. SATAC uses a 90-credit structure producing an aggregate out of 90.00, while NSW uses scaled marks in 10 units out of 500. Only the ATAR is comparable between states.

SATAC builds your aggregate as the best possible outcome from your eligible results. It takes your three strongest 20-credit subjects for the first 60 credits, then the best 30 credits from your remaining results for the flexible option, within its counting and precluded-combination rules.

They keep the aggregate fair, so students cannot gain an advantage by taking several very similar subjects, and so the aggregate reflects genuine breadth of study.

How does SATAC rank my aggregate?

SATAC ranks every eligible student’s aggregate and derives a percentile position, comparing your rank with other students who achieved a similar aggregate or higher. That position is expressed as your ATAR.

The ATAR reflects your position relative to your age group, not just among ATAR recipients. That is why the typical ATAR among recipients sits above 50.

No. Each university and TAFE SA sets its own selection ranks for its courses, based on demand and places. SATAC publishes them and runs the offer process, but the universities decide them.

Selection ranks move with demand and the number of places. A more popular course, or fewer places, pushes the rank up; the reverse pushes it down. Last year’s figure is a guide, not a guarantee.

No. The selection rank is your ATAR plus any adjustment factors. A course compares your selection rank, not your bare ATAR, against its cut-off.

Can my selection rank be higher than my ATAR?

Yes. Adjustment factors are added to your ATAR to form your selection rank, so the rank can be higher. It cannot exceed the maximum the scheme allows.

Is there a cap on adjustment factors?

Yes. Schemes cap how many points can be added, so adjustments lift your rank within a limit rather than without bound. Check the current cap.

Do adjustment factors guarantee a place?

No. They lift your selection rank, which improves your chance against the cut-off, but a place still depends on meeting the rank and any prerequisites, and on places being available.

What are subject adjustments?

Some universities add points for studying particular subjects relevant to a course, such as a language or a mathematics subject. These are a type of adjustment factor and vary by university.

Can I see my selection rank before offers?

How does SATAC scale my results?

SATAC adjusts each subject’s results so achievement in different subjects can be compared on a common scale. Results in subjects where the group performed strongly tend to scale up, and the scaled results feed your aggregate.

Why is scaling needed at all?

Without it, the same raw mark in two subjects could reflect very different levels of achievement. Scaling puts all subjects on a comparable footing so the aggregate and ATAR are fair across subject choices.

Does scaling punish certain subjects?

Scaling is not a punishment. It reflects how a subject’s cohort performed across all their subjects. A subject studied by strong students tends to scale up; that is the mechanism, not a penalty on anyone.

Can I predict my scaling?

Only roughly. SATAC does not publish each subject’s scaling in advance, so a calculator’s estimate is informed guesswork based on past patterns. Treat it as a guide.

Does a hard subject always scale up?

Not automatically. Scaling follows how the cohort performed, which often correlates with a subject’s difficulty but is not the same thing. A strong result matters more than a subject’s reputation.

Should I pick subjects just for scaling?

Generally no. Scaling rewards a strong result, and a strong result is easier in a subject that suits you. Chasing a high-scaling subject you struggle in usually backfires.

Does scaling change every year?

Scaling reflects each year’s cohort, so it can shift year to year. That is another reason not to rely on a fixed figure for a subject.

How does scaling reach my aggregate?

Your scaled subject results are combined, best results first, into a 90-credit aggregate. Scaling happens before the results are added, so the aggregate is built from comparable scaled results.

Is the Research Project scaled?

Yes. Like other results, the Research Project is scaled so it can be compared fairly, and it can count toward the flexible part of the aggregate if it is among your best remaining results.

Are VET results scaled?

Recognised VET study that counts toward the aggregate is scaled onto the SACE scale so it can be compared with ordinary subjects, within the cap that applies to Recognised Studies.

What is the maximum aggregate?

The aggregate has a set maximum built from 90 credits of scaled study. SATAC converts the full range of aggregates into ATARs, with the top aggregates mapping to the highest ATARs.

How does the aggregate become an ATAR?

SATAC ranks all students by aggregate and converts that ranking into the ATAR scale, so your ATAR reports your position in the age group rather than your aggregate directly.

Is the ATAR a percentage?

No. It is a rank. An ATAR of 80 means you are ranked around the top 20% of your age group, not that you scored 80%.

What is the highest ATAR?

The highest ATAR is 99.95. ATARs are reported in steps of 0.05, so the scale runs 99.95, 99.90, 99.85 and so on down.

Why are ATARs reported in 0.05 steps?

The 0.05 steps give a fine-grained rank without implying more precision than the data supports. Each step separates students into a narrow band of the age group.

Does the ATAR include students who left school?

The ATAR is a rank against the relevant age group, which is why a given ATAR reflects your standing among your peers, not only among those who finished Year 12. This is set by the national methodology.

Can two students have the same ATAR?

Yes. Many students share each ATAR value, because the 0.05 steps group students into bands. Sharing an ATAR is normal.

Is the SA ATAR comparable to other states?

Yes. The ATAR is a national scale, so an ATAR from SATAC means the same rank as the same ATAR from another state’s admissions centre, even though each centre calculates it from its own results.

Subject results can be subject to the SACE Board’s review processes, and SATAC calculates the ATAR from those results. Check the SACE Board’s process for querying a subject result.

How long is my ATAR valid?

An ATAR does not expire. You can use it in later years, though universities may also weigh more recent study for mature applicants.

Can I improve my ATAR by repeating a subject?

Repeating or adding Stage 2 study in a later year can change the results SATAC draws on. Whether it helps depends on your results and the aggregate rules; seek advice first.

Is a bare pass in the SACE enough for an ATAR?

Completing the SACE and having enough eligible scaled study are what let SATAC calculate an ATAR. Simply passing does not guarantee a high ATAR, which depends on your scaled results.

Do I need to finish the SACE to get an ATAR?

You need to complete the SACE requirements and have the eligible Stage 2 study SATAC uses to build an aggregate. Without completing the certificate, an ATAR generally cannot be calculated.

What is the Tertiary Entrance Statement?

It is the statement SATAC issues showing your ATAR and the results used to calculate it. It is the official record of your rank for university and TAFE SA applications.

Who can see my ATAR?

Your ATAR is private to you unless you choose to share it. SATAC provides it to institutions you apply to as part of the application process.

Can my parents access my SATAC account?

The account is yours. You can share information with your family, but SATAC deals with the applicant. Keep your login secure.

What if I lose my SATAC login?

Use SATAC’s account recovery process to regain access. Keep your contact email current so recovery is straightforward.

Is there a SATAC guide for Year 12s?

SATAC publishes a tertiary entrance booklet and online guides each year with the rules, key dates and course information. Read the current edition rather than an older one.

Does SATAC advise on course choice?

SATAC publishes course information and can explain the process, but choosing a course is yours to make, with help from the university and your careers adviser.

Can I talk to a person at SATAC?

Yes. SATAC has a customer enquiries team you can contact for help with applications, offers and the ATAR. Check the website for current contact details.

What is the difference between SATAC and the university?

SATAC runs the shared application and offer process and calculates the ATAR; the university sets its courses, selection ranks and prerequisites and enrols you once you accept. You apply through SATAC and enrol with the university.

Does SATAC handle enrolment?

No. SATAC handles applications and offers. Once you accept an offer, you enrol directly with the institution.

Do I pay fees to SATAC or the university?

You pay a SATAC application fee for the application process, and tuition and other fees to the university once enrolled. They are separate.

What if I am a mature-age applicant?

SATAC can assess mature applicants on previous study, work experience, the STAT and foundation programs, not only on a school ATAR. Contact SATAC about your background.

Do I need an ATAR as a mature applicant?

Not always. Many courses admit mature applicants on other bases, such as the STAT, previous tertiary study or a qualification. An ATAR is one option, not the only one.

How does SATAC treat overseas qualifications?

SATAC can assess some overseas qualifications to produce a rank or a basis for admission. The treatment depends on the qualification; check with SATAC.

Can I use a diploma to enter a degree?

A completed diploma can provide a basis for admission and sometimes credit toward a degree. Check how a specific diploma is recognised for your course.

What support exists for regional students?

Adjustment schemes often recognise rural and remote location, and universities offer support and sometimes accommodation. Check each university’s regional support.

Does SATAC help with scholarships?

Scholarships are offered by the universities and other bodies, not administered through the core SATAC application. Check each university’s scholarship information.

Where is the most reliable information?

SATAC’s own website and current booklet, and the universities’ course pages, are the most reliable sources. They are updated each year, unlike older guides or forum posts.
