What's a good ATAR?

It is the question every Year 12 student asks. The honest answer is that a good ATAR depends on your goal, but there are clear benchmarks. Here is what the numbers actually mean.

Key takeaways

  • The median ATAR is around 70, so 70 is a solid, middle-of-the-pack result.
  • An ATAR of 80+ opens most university courses.
  • An ATAR of 90+ is competitive for in-demand degrees.
  • 95+ is usually needed for medicine, law and other high-demand courses at leading universities.
  • A “good” ATAR is really the one that gets you into your course — nothing more.
  • Cut-offs move each year with demand, so treat published numbers as a guide.
  • Adjustment factors and pathways can lower the ATAR you actually need.

Four questions that define a good ATAR for you

  1. Which courses do I want? Start with the courses that genuinely interest you, including at least one aspirational choice and one safe option.
  2. What selection rank do they need? Find each course’s most recent lowest selection rank and any guaranteed entry rank.
  3. What adjustments am I eligible for? Add subject, regional, equity or other adjustments that apply to you at each institution.
  4. What else do they require? Check prerequisites, assumed knowledge and additional criteria such as interviews or portfolios.

Answering these four questions turns “Is my ATAR good?” into a practical comparison. A good ATAR is simply one that, after adjustments, clears the selection rank needed for the courses you want, provided you meet their other requirements.

Benchmarks still help

General benchmarks help you get your bearings before you have specific courses in mind. An ATAR around 70 is close to the typical result among recipients in the largest states, 80 places you in roughly the top 20 per cent of your age group, and 90 in roughly the top 10 per cent. Use these benchmarks to understand your position, then turn to your specific courses.

Why a "good" ATAR depends on your goal

The ATAR is a rank, so “good” is relative. An ATAR that is perfect for one course may fall short for another. The right way to think about it is not “what is a good ATAR” in the abstract, but “what ATAR do I need for the course I want”.

That said, there are clear benchmarks that hold across the board. They come from where each ATAR sits in the age group, and from the cut-offs universities have published for years.

Three ways to judge whether an ATAR is good

Because “good” is relative, it helps to look at any ATAR from three angles.

  • Against the age group. Your ATAR tells you the approximate percentage of your age group in your state you performed better than. An ATAR of 75 means better than about 75 per cent.
  • Against other ATAR recipients. Because not everyone receives an ATAR, the typical ATAR among recipients is around 70. An ATAR above 70 is above the middle of that group in the largest states.
  • Against your goals. The most useful comparison is with the selection ranks needed for the courses you want, after adding any adjustment factors you are eligible for.

An ATAR can look modest from one angle and excellent from another. A 72 is close to the typical result among recipients, but it may be comfortably above the threshold of the course a student wants most, which makes it a very good result for that student.

Benchmarks that hold broadly

While “good” depends on the goal, some benchmarks are useful everywhere. An ATAR of 80 places you in roughly the top 20 per cent of your age group, 90 in roughly the top 10 per cent, and 99 in roughly the top 1 per cent. Among students who receive an ATAR, a result around 70 is typical.

Why the typical result is a better benchmark than 50

Many people assume 50 must be average because it sits in the middle of the scale. For the whole age group, that is true: an ATAR of 50 marks the middle of everyone of a similar age in the state. But most of the people reading about ATARs are students who will actually receive one, and for that group the middle is much higher. UAC reported a median of 70.75 in NSW in 2025, and VTAC an average of 69.52 in Victoria in 2024.

For most students, then, the more useful benchmark is the typical result among recipients. A result above around 70 places you above the middle of students receiving an ATAR in the largest states, while a result in the 60s sits a little below it. Both, however, place you ahead of most of your age group, and both open a range of courses.

Why benchmarks can mislead

Benchmarks are useful for orientation, but they are not decisions. A student with an ATAR of 66 who meets the threshold of their dream course has a better result for their purposes than a student with 88 who narrowly misses theirs. Always come back to your own goals.

What is the average ATAR?

Here is a subtle but important point. The ATAR is a rank of the whole Year 12 age group, but not everyone receives an ATAR — some students take vocational paths that do not produce one. Among the students who do receive an ATAR, the median sits around 70.

So an ATAR of 70 is genuinely average for university-bound students, not a weak result. Anything above it puts you in the stronger half of that group. This surprises people who assume the average must be 50, but a rank of the whole age group works differently once vocational students are set aside.

What the official figures show

Published data from admissions centres confirms that the typical ATAR among recipients is around 70. UAC reported a median ATAR of 70.75 in NSW in 2025, with a median of 71.60 for female students and 69.80 for male students, and a median of 71.55 in 2024. VTAC reported an average ATAR of 69.52 in Victoria in 2024, up from 69.41 in 2023. In the ACT, where a high share of students receive an ATAR, the median among recipients in 2025 was 78.35.

Why the median is above 50

The ATAR compares you with your whole age group in your state, including young people who left school early or did not receive an ATAR. Students who do receive an ATAR tend to be stronger academically than the age group as a whole, so the middle of the recipient group sits well above the middle of the age group. That is why 50 is not the typical ATAR.

Reading the table

The table shows why both statements can be true at once: an ATAR of 80 is in the top 20 per cent of the age group, yet about a third of NSW ATAR recipients achieved 80 or above. The difference comes from who is being counted.

Who receives an ATAR

Understanding who receives an ATAR explains why the typical result is around 70. In NSW in 2025, 77,693 students completed at least one ATAR course, 68,896 were awarded the HSC and 60,432 received an ATAR. Nationally, a December 2025 Mitchell Institute report estimated that around 64 per cent of Year 12 students receive an ATAR. Students who do not receive one often follow vocational or other pathways, and their absence from the recipient group is part of why the recipient median sits well above 50.

The top of the scale

At the top, the numbers are small. In NSW in 2025, each of the highest few ATAR bands held around 50 students, and 53 students received 99.95. In Victoria, 42 students received 99.95, and in Queensland, 37. These figures show how fine the distinctions are at the top of the scale.

Each ATAR band in more detail

Below 60

Below 60, direct entry is available for some courses at some institutions, often with adjustments, and pathway programs become especially valuable. University diplomas, college programs and enabling courses are designed for exactly this situation, and many lead into the second year of a related degree once completed successfully. Students in this range who are interested in university should talk directly to admissions teams, who can explain the options that suit them. Remember too that a result in the 50s often reflects solid marks: in NSW, UAC explains that HSC marks around 70 typically lead to ATARs in the 50s.

60 to 70

Between 60 and 70, many courses become available at a range of institutions, particularly in arts, science, business, nursing, teaching and other general fields. Adjustments can lift selection ranks into the low 70s and beyond for some courses. This band sits just below the typical result among ATAR recipients in the largest states, so it is a solid foundation for many plans.

70 to 80

Between 70 and 80, most general degrees are within reach at many institutions. This band contains the typical result among ATAR recipients in NSW and Victoria. With adjustments, students in this band can also reach some courses with thresholds in the low to mid 80s.

80 to 90

Between 80 and 90, students are in roughly the top 20 to top 10 per cent of their age group. Most courses are open, and many popular degrees are competitive options. In NSW in 2025, about 34.6 per cent of ATAR recipients achieved 80 or above.

90 to 95

Between 90 and 95, students are in roughly the top 10 to top 5 per cent of their age group. In-demand degrees in fields such as engineering, commerce and psychology at many institutions are within reach.

95 and above

Above 95, students are in the top 5 per cent of their age group. This is the range for the most competitive courses, many of which also use additional criteria. At this level, preparing for interviews, admissions tests and portfolios can matter as much as the final decimal places of the ATAR.

What each ATAR band opens up

As a broad guide, here is what different ATAR levels tend to make possible:

  • 60–70: A range of university courses, especially at universities with lower cut-offs and through pathway programs.
  • 70–80: Most general degrees, including many business, arts and science courses.
  • 80–90: Competitive for popular degrees, and comfortable for the majority of courses.
  • 90–95: In range for in-demand degrees like engineering, commerce and psychology at strong universities.
  • 95–99.95: The level for the most competitive courses, such as medicine, dentistry, law and veterinary science.

How to read these bands

The bands above are a broad guide, not a rule. The same type of course can have very different thresholds at different universities, at different campuses of the same university and in different years. Thresholds are also usually expressed as lowest selection ranks, which include adjustment factors, so a student with an ATAR below a band may still be offered a course within it. Use the bands to get your bearings, then check the specific courses you are interested in.

Is my ATAR good? A score-by-score guide

Students often ask whether a particular ATAR is good. Here is how to think about common results. In each case, “good” ultimately depends on the courses you want and any adjustment factors you are eligible for.

Is 50 a good ATAR?

An ATAR of 50 means you performed better than about half of your age group in your state. Among students who receive an ATAR, it is below the typical result of around 70. It still opens a range of options, particularly pathway programs, diplomas and some courses with lower thresholds. Many students who start with an ATAR around 50 reach the degree they want through a pathway.

Is 55 a good ATAR?

An ATAR of 55 places you ahead of about 55 per cent of your age group. In NSW, UAC explains that students with HSC marks around 70 typically receive ATARs in the 50s, so a result in this range often reflects solid marks rather than poor ones. Look at courses whose thresholds sit near your likely selection rank and at pathway programs for courses you want more.

Is 60 a good ATAR?

An ATAR of 60 places you ahead of about 60 per cent of your age group. It opens a range of university courses, especially at institutions with lower thresholds and through adjustment factors and pathway programs. It is a reasonable foundation for many study plans.

Is 65 a good ATAR?

An ATAR of 65 places you ahead of about 65 per cent of your age group and close to the typical result among ATAR recipients. Many general degrees have thresholds in this region at some institutions. In the ACT in 2025, 78.8 per cent of ATAR recipients had an ATAR of 65 or above, which shows how the meaning of a result among recipients can differ between systems.

Is 70 a good ATAR?

An ATAR of 70 is close to the typical result among ATAR recipients in the largest states and places you ahead of about 70 per cent of your age group. It opens many general degrees and is a solid, middle-of-the-pack result.

Is 75 a good ATAR?

An ATAR of 75 is above the typical result among ATAR recipients and places you in roughly the top quarter of your age group. Many courses in business, arts, science, nursing and teaching are within reach at a range of institutions.

Is 80 a good ATAR?

An ATAR of 80 places you in roughly the top 20 per cent of your age group. In NSW in 2025, about a third of ATAR recipients achieved 80 or above. It opens most university courses and is competitive for many popular degrees.

Is 85 a good ATAR?

An ATAR of 85 places you in roughly the top 15 per cent of your age group. It is a strong result that is competitive for many in-demand courses, and with adjustment factors it can bring additional courses within reach.

Is 90 a good ATAR?

An ATAR of 90 places you in roughly the top 10 per cent of your age group. In NSW in 2025, about 17.3 per cent of ATAR recipients achieved 90 or above. It is competitive for most in-demand degrees, though a few of the most competitive courses look for higher ranks and additional criteria.

Is 95 a good ATAR?

An ATAR of 95 places you in roughly the top 5 per cent of your age group, a very high result. It is in range for many of the most competitive courses, although some, such as medicine, also use admissions tests and interviews.

Is 99 a good ATAR?

An ATAR of 99 places you in roughly the top 1 per cent of your age group, an exceptional result. At this level, the other criteria used by the most competitive courses, such as interviews and admissions tests, often matter as much as the ATAR.

Is 99.95 a good ATAR?

It is the highest ATAR possible, placing you in the top 0.05 per cent of your age group. In 2025, 53 students received 99.95 in NSW, 42 in Victoria and 37 in Queensland.

Why “good” is often misjudged: rank versus percentage

Many students and parents judge an ATAR as if it were a percentage mark, and that leads to misjudging whether it is good.

The percentage trap

At school, 70 per cent on a test is often seen as a solid but unremarkable result, and 50 per cent as a bare pass. Applying the same intuition to the ATAR leads people to see 70 as ordinary and 50 as a fail. But the ATAR is a rank: 70 means you performed better than about 70 per cent of your age group, and among students who receive an ATAR it is close to the typical result.

Marks and ATARs are on different scales

UAC explains that most HSC courses have a median mark above 75, while the median ATAR is usually around 70, and that students with marks around 70 typically receive ATARs in the 50s while marks around 80 typically lead to an ATAR around 80. A student who averages 72 in their HSC courses has not “got a 72”; they are in a crowded part of the distribution where many students have similar marks.

Judging fairly

To judge an ATAR fairly, read it as a position, compare it with the typical result among recipients and, most importantly, compare your selection rank with the thresholds of the courses you want.

How results compare from year to year

Because the ATAR represents a position in the age group, its meaning is designed to be stable from year to year. Published figures confirm that typical results move only a little.

  • NSW: UAC reported a median ATAR of 71.55 in 2024 and 70.75 in 2025. The number of students at 99.95 was 51 in 2024 and 53 in 2025.
  • Victoria: the average ATAR was 69.41 in 2023 and 69.52 in 2024. VTAC reported 41 students at 99.95 in 2024 and 42 in 2025, and 47,461 ATARs in 2024 compared with 49,310 in 2025.

These small movements reflect changes in the number of students receiving an ATAR and in how results are spread, not a change in what an ATAR means. An ATAR of 80 represents roughly the same position in the age group each year.

What does change: course thresholds

While the meaning of an ATAR is stable, course thresholds can move noticeably from year to year as demand and the number of places change. That is why a buffer above last year’s threshold is sensible.

Differences in results between groups

Published statistics sometimes break results down by group. UAC’s 2025 figures, for example, show a median ATAR of 71.60 for female students and 69.80 for male students in NSW. Of the 53 NSW students who received 99.95, 19 were female and 34 male.

These figures describe groups, not individuals. The ATAR calculation applies the same rules to every student, and your own ATAR depends on your own results. Group statistics are useful for understanding the system, but they say nothing about what any individual can achieve.

In Queensland, they are reported as “30.00 or less”, and in South Australia ATARs below 30.00 are not shown on the Tertiary Entrance Statement. A result in this range still means you completed an ATAR pattern of study. Pathway programs, enabling courses and diplomas are the usual next steps for students who want to go to university.

40 and 45

An ATAR of 40 or 45 places you ahead of about 40 or 45 per cent of your age group. Some courses and many pathway programs are open at these levels, especially with adjustment factors.

68 and 72

Results in the late 60s and low 70s sit around the typical result among ATAR recipients in the largest states. They open many general degrees at a range of institutions.

77 and 82

Results in the late 70s and low 80s place you in roughly the top quarter to top fifth of your age group. Many popular courses are within reach, and adjustments can extend your options further.

87 and 92

Results in the high 80s and low 90s are strong, placing you in roughly the top 13 to 8 per cent of your age group. Many in-demand degrees are within reach at a range of institutions.

97 and 98

99.5

An ATAR of 99.5 places you in roughly the top half a per cent of your age group. Near the top, the decimal places matter for the most competitive courses, but additional criteria such as interviews and admissions tests often matter as much as the rank.

Engineering

Engineering thresholds vary widely between institutions. Many programs have prerequisites or assumed knowledge in mathematics and sometimes physics. See the ATAR required for engineering.

Commerce and business degrees span a wide range of thresholds, from very competitive programs at some institutions to more accessible ones elsewhere. Some have mathematics prerequisites or assumed knowledge. See the ATAR required for commerce.

Always check the specific course
Field-level guidance can only take you so far. Two courses with similar names at different institutions can have very different thresholds and requirements. Check each course’s current information before you finalise your preferences.

Worked selection rank examples

Because universities use selection ranks, the ATAR you need for a course depends on the adjustments you are eligible for. These illustrative examples show how that works.

A regional student

Emma’s ATAR is 81.40. She lives in a regional area, and one university offers her a regional adjustment of a few points for the course she wants. Her selection rank for that course is several points above her ATAR, which brings it above the course’s recent threshold of 84. At another university that does not offer her an adjustment, her selection rank stays at 81.40.

A student with a subject adjustment

Kai’s ATAR is 87.20, and he achieved a strong result in a language that one university rewards with a subject adjustment for its commerce course. His selection rank for that course rises, while his selection rank for an engineering course at the same university, which does not use that adjustment, stays at 87.20.

A student applying through an equity scheme

Mia’s ATAR is 74.65. Her schooling was disrupted by a long illness, and she applied to her admissions centre’s equity scheme with supporting documents. Several institutions grant her adjustments, raising her selection rank for courses on her list and bringing a course with a threshold of 78 within reach.

A student without adjustments

Sam’s ATAR is 90.05, and he is not eligible for adjustments at the institutions he is applying to. His selection rank equals his ATAR for every course. He compares 90.05 with each course’s threshold and builds his preference list accordingly.

All names and figures are illustrative. Adjustment schemes, sizes and caps differ between institutions.

Choosing subjects with a good ATAR in mind

A good ATAR starts with subject choices made in Year 10. Here is how to choose with your goals in mind.

Choose subjects you can do well in

Your ATAR depends on where you finish in each subject relative to other students, after scaling. Subjects you are good at and enjoy are usually the ones where you will finish highest. That matters more than how a subject tends to scale.

Meet the prerequisites for your goals

Check the prerequisites and assumed knowledge for courses you might want. If you are interested in engineering, science, health or commerce, mathematics choices can be particularly important.

Meet your state’s ATAR rules

Make sure your subjects meet the eligibility requirements, such as compulsory English and minimum numbers of eligible subjects, units or credits.

Consider a buffer

In states where only your best results count, an extra subject can protect you if one goes badly, provided it does not take too much time from your other subjects.

Use scaling only as a tie-breaker

If you are equally strong in and interested in two subjects, scaling at your expected level can help you decide. Our guides to the highest scaling subjects and whether hard subjects help explain why it should not drive your choices.

Match the ATAR to the course, not the other way round

Rather than chasing a round number, work backwards from the course you want. Look up its cut-off, then aim a little above it to be safe.

Medicine usually needs 95 and above plus an admissions test. Law at leading universities is similar. Nursing, teaching and many science degrees sit comfortably in the 70s. Our course entry guides cover the main degrees with indicative ATARs.

A step-by-step way to set your target

  1. List the courses you are interested in. Include your dream course, several realistic options and at least one safe option.
  2. Find each course’s most recent threshold. Look for the lowest selection rank and, where available, the course’s ATAR profile.
  3. Check every other requirement. Note prerequisites, assumed knowledge, interviews, tests, portfolios and deadlines.
  4. Estimate your adjustments. Work out which adjustment factors you are likely to be eligible for at each institution.
  5. Set a target with a buffer. Aim a few points above the threshold of your preferred course, after allowing for adjustments.
  6. Track your progress. Use a calculator built for your state to compare your current results with your target, and re-run it as your results come in.

Our target ATAR calculator and guide to target ATAR strategy can help with each step.

How big should your buffer be?

A buffer protects you against two uncertainties: movement in the course’s threshold and the difference between your estimate and your final ATAR. There is no fixed rule, but a few principles help.

  • Larger for volatile courses. If a course’s threshold has moved noticeably in recent years, a larger buffer makes sense.
  • Larger for early estimates. Early in Year 12, your estimate is less certain, so aim higher above the threshold.
  • Smaller with guaranteed entry. If a course has a guaranteed entry rank, aiming at or slightly above it is often enough.
  • Account for adjustments. Calculate your buffer on your expected selection rank, not just your ATAR.

Whatever buffer you choose, keep realistic and safe options on your preference list as well.

Why cut-offs move every year

A published cut-off is not a fixed pass mark. It is usually the lowest selection rank, including any adjustment factors, that received an offer in a previous round. If more students apply for a course this year, the cut-off rises. If fewer apply, it falls.

This is why you should treat any cut-off as a guide, and aim above it. A course listed at 85 one year might land at 87 the next. Building in a buffer protects you from a bad-luck year.

Reading course thresholds properly

Universities publish several different figures for each course. The lowest selection rank is the lowest rank, including adjustment factors, that received an offer in a particular round. Many universities also publish ATAR profiles showing the lowest, median and highest ranks of students offered places. The median is the middle of that group, so half the students offered a place had a lower rank. Some institutions also publish guaranteed entry ranks.

When you compare your likely selection rank with a course, use the lowest selection rank as your main guide and the profile to understand where students typically sat.

Why thresholds rise and fall

A threshold reflects the number of places a course offers and the number and strength of applicants who list it highly. If a university reduces places or a course becomes more popular, the threshold can rise. If places increase or demand falls, it can fall. Thresholds can also differ between offer rounds within the same year.

A buffer protects you

Because thresholds move, aiming exactly at last year’s figure is risky. A buffer of a few points above the threshold, after adjustments, gives you room for movement and for any difference between your estimate and your final ATAR.

Cut-offs, profiles and guaranteed entry: what each tells you

Three kinds of figure are commonly published for courses, and each tells you something different about whether your ATAR is good enough.

The lowest selection rank

This is the lowest rank, including adjustments, that received an offer in a particular round. It describes what happened in the past and is the most useful single figure for judging your chances, but it can move in your year.

The ATAR profile

Many universities publish the lowest, median and highest ranks of students offered places. The profile shows the spread of successful applicants. The median tells you where a typical successful applicant sat, but half of those offered places were below it.

The guaranteed entry rank

Some institutions publish a guaranteed entry rank for particular courses, promising an offer to applicants at or above it who meet all other requirements. It is a forward-looking promise rather than a description of the past. Some institutions also make offers below their guaranteed entry rank when demand allows.

Using all three

If a course has a guaranteed entry rank and your selection rank is above it, the course is likely to be safe, provided you meet every requirement. If not, use the lowest selection rank as your main guide and the profile to understand where you sit among likely applicants.

Adjustment factors can lower what you need

The ATAR you need is not always the raw cut-off. Many universities add adjustment factors to your rank, based on things like where you live, subjects you took, or difficulty you faced during Year 12. These lift your selection rank above your ATAR for that course.

A student with an ATAR of 88 and five adjustment points is considered at 93 for that course. If your ATAR is close to a cut-off, adjustment factors can close the gap. See how selection rank works.

The main kinds of adjustment

  • Subject adjustments for strong results in particular subjects, such as languages or mathematics, often when they are relevant to the course. See subject bonus points.
  • Regional and location adjustments for students who live or study in regional or remote areas. See regional bonus points.
  • Equity adjustments for students whose education has been affected by circumstances such as hardship, illness, disability or disrupted schooling, through schemes such as UAC’s Educational Access Schemes, VTAC’s Special Entry Access Scheme and QTAC’s Educational Access Scheme. See educational access schemes.
  • Elite athlete and performer adjustments for students competing or performing at a high level. See elite athlete bonus points.
  • School recommendation schemes, which consider school recommendations and other criteria, sometimes leading to offers before ATARs are released.

Caps, combinations and applications

Most institutions cap the total adjustment a student can receive for a course, and some courses do not accept certain adjustments. Some institutions allow adjustments from different schemes to be combined, while others limit combinations; our guide to combining regional and equity adjustments explains common patterns. Some adjustments are applied automatically; others require an application with its own deadline.

How adjustments change what counts as good

If you are eligible for adjustments, the ATAR you need for a course can be lower than its published threshold. A student with an ATAR of 84 and an adjustment of 4 points has a selection rank of 88 for that course. How offers work once you have your ATAR

Offer rounds

Admissions centres release offers in several rounds, from early offers in the year before study through the main ATAR-based rounds in December and January to later rounds in February. In 2026, the first ATAR-based offers arrive on 23 December in NSW, the ACT, Victoria and Queensland, on 24 December in Western Australia and on 14 January 2027 in South Australia and the NT.

How offers are decided

For most courses, applicants are ranked by selection rank and offers are made from the top down until places are filled, subject to prerequisites and other requirements. You receive an offer for the highest preference you are eligible for.

Accepting and changing

Respond to each offer before its acceptance deadline. In most systems, accepting an offer does not stop you receiving an offer for a higher preference in a later round, but check your admissions centre’s rules. You can usually change your preferences between rounds.

If you miss out in one round

Many students receive offers in later rounds, and some courses still have places after the main rounds. Keep your preferences up to date and watch for new offers until the final rounds.

Myths about what makes a good ATAR

“Anything below 90 is disappointing”

In NSW in 2025, about 17.3 per cent of ATAR recipients achieved 90 or above. The great majority of students who receive an ATAR, including many who go on to excellent careers, have ATARs below 90.

“A good ATAR is one your friends are impressed by”

A good ATAR is one that opens the course you want. Friends’ reactions are not a useful measure.

“You should use your ATAR on the most prestigious course you can get”

Choosing a course because of its threshold rather than your interests is a common source of regret. Choose the course you want.

“The average ATAR is 50”

Among students who receive an ATAR, the typical result is around 70 in the largest states.

“A low ATAR means you cannot go to university”

Pathways, adjustment factors, alternative entry schemes and later applications mean many students with lower ATARs reach university.

“A high ATAR guarantees success at university”

University success depends far more on engagement, habits and interest in your course than on the ATAR that got you in.

A step-by-step plan if your ATAR is lower than you hoped

  1. Pause before deciding. Give yourself a little time to absorb the result. Most change of preference deadlines are days away, not hours.
  2. Check your results. Make sure every subject is listed correctly. If something seems wrong, contact your school or curriculum authority promptly.
  3. Work out your selection ranks. Add every adjustment you are eligible for at each institution. Your selection rank may be higher than your ATAR for some courses.
  4. Look again at thresholds. Compare your selection ranks with the most recent lowest selection ranks, and check whether any course has a guaranteed entry rank you meet.
  5. Widen the search. Look at the same course at other institutions and campuses, and at related courses that could lead to a transfer.
  6. Research pathways. Find diplomas, college programs and enabling courses that lead into your preferred degree, and check the results needed to progress.
  7. Check remaining schemes. Some adjustment and equity schemes have deadlines after results day.
  8. Rebuild your preferences. Keep your genuine first choice at the top if you still want it, then add realistic options and at least one safe option.
  9. Get advice. Talk to your careers adviser, admissions centre or the universities you are interested in.
  10. Keep perspective. A different route can lead to the same destination.

If your ATAR is lower than you hoped

A lower ATAR is not the end of the road. Universities run many pathways designed exactly for this. A diploma or TAFE qualification can lead into a degree after a year. Enabling and foundation programs offer another way in. Some students start a related course and transfer once they have strong university marks.

These routes are common and respected, and they often end in the same degree. If your ATAR falls short of your first choice, it is worth looking at pathways early rather than assuming the door is closed.

Pathways in more detail

  • University diplomas and college programs. Many lead into the second year of a related degree once completed successfully.
  • TAFE qualifications. A diploma or advanced diploma can give a basis for admission and sometimes credit towards a degree.
  • Enabling and foundation programs. These build academic skills and can provide a basis for admission. Many are available without tuition fees for eligible domestic students.
  • Related courses and internal transfers. Starting in a related course with a lower threshold and transferring based on university results is a common route.
  • Alternative entry schemes. Portfolio, audition, interview, early offer and equity schemes all consider more than the ATAR.
  • Mature-age entry. Adults can often apply on the basis of work experience, prior study or a test such as the Special Tertiary Admissions Test.

Making the most of a pathway

Before you choose a pathway, check how much credit it gives towards the degree you want, what results you need in it to transfer and how long the whole route will take. Talk to the university’s admissions team about your options.

Reviewing your preferences

If your ATAR is lower than you hoped, review your preferences before the next change of preference deadline. Add courses whose thresholds sit near your selection rank, consider pathways into your preferred course and keep at least one safe option.

When a modest ATAR is a great result

A number that looks modest in the abstract can be an excellent result in context. Here are some situations where that is true.

When it clears your threshold

If your selection rank is comfortably above the threshold of the course you want most, your ATAR has done exactly what it needed to do, whatever the number.

When it reflects real progress

A student who started Year 11 with weak results and finished with an ATAR in the 60s or 70s has made genuine progress. That progress, and the habits behind it, will serve them well at university.

When it was earned in difficult circumstances

Students who completed Year 12 while managing illness, caring responsibilities, work, disrupted schooling or other challenges have achieved something significant. Equity schemes exist partly to recognise this, and such students may be eligible for adjustments that raise their selection rank.

When it opens a pathway

For many students, an ATAR that opens a diploma or enabling program is the first step to the degree they want. Pathway students graduate with the same qualification as students who entered directly.

If your ATAR is high: making the most of it

A high ATAR gives you more choices, but it also brings its own pressures. Here is how to make the most of it.

Choose by interest, not by rank

Some students feel they should choose the course with the highest threshold they can reach, so as not to “waste” their ATAR. That is a poor reason to choose a course. A course you are genuinely interested in, at an institution that suits you, is a far better choice than a prestigious course you do not want.

Look at scholarships

Some scholarships use the ATAR as one of their criteria, often alongside other factors. Many have their own applications and deadlines, some before results are released. Check each university’s scholarship information early.

Consider advanced programs

Some universities offer advanced, honours-track or combined degrees for high-achieving students. They can be rewarding, but they also involve more work, so choose them for interest rather than status.

Remember the other criteria

For the most competitive courses, a high ATAR is often necessary but not sufficient. Interviews, admissions tests and portfolios may matter as much as the rank.

Scholarships and the ATAR

Scholarships are one of the few places where the ATAR can matter beyond admission. Here is what to know.

  • Academic scholarships may use the ATAR as a main criterion, often with a minimum rank.
  • Equity scholarships often consider financial need or other circumstances, sometimes alongside academic results.
  • Regional and access scholarships may support students from particular areas or backgrounds.
  • Course-specific scholarships may be tied to particular fields or programs.

Many scholarships require a separate application, and some close before ATARs are released. Check deadlines early, and do not assume you are ineligible without reading the criteria.

Using a related course and a transfer

One of the most common ways to reach a course with a higher threshold is to start in a related course and transfer later. Here is how it usually works.

Choose the starting course carefully

Pick a course with a lower threshold whose first-year units overlap with your target course, so that your studies count towards it if you transfer. Ask the university which courses are the usual starting points.

Know the transfer requirements

Internal transfers are usually based on university results, such as a grade point average or weighted average mark, sometimes with other criteria. Some courses have limited transfer places. Find out the requirements before you start.

Focus on your first-year results

Because transfers depend on university results, your first-year performance matters. Attend classes, use feedback and support services, and treat first year as seriously as Year 12.

Have a backup plan

If you do not transfer, you will still be in a course you chose. Make sure it is one you would be willing to complete.

A simple results-day decision guide

  1. Check your results. Make sure all your subjects are listed and correct.
  2. Note your ATAR. Read it as a position in your age group.
  3. Work out your selection ranks. Add any adjustments you are eligible for, course by course.
  4. Compare with thresholds. Use the most recent lowest selection rank for each course.
  5. Sort your options. Mark each course as safe, realistic or aspirational.
  6. Review your preferences. Put your genuine first choice first and keep at least one safe option.
  7. Look at pathways. For any course above your range, note the pathway options.
  8. Mind the deadline. Make changes before the next change of preference deadline.
  9. Talk it through. Discuss your plan with someone you trust before you finalise it.

What a good start at university looks like

Whatever your ATAR, a good first year at university sets you up for everything that follows. Your university results will soon matter far more than your ATAR for transfers, scholarships, honours and further study, so it is worth starting well.

Get organised early

Read your unit outlines, note assessment dates and plan your weeks. University gives you more independence than school, and a simple weekly plan helps you stay on top of it.

Go to classes and engage

Attending lectures, tutorials and labs, and taking part in discussions, makes a real difference to your results and your enjoyment of the course.

Use feedback and support

Use the feedback on your first assessments to improve the next ones. Most universities offer academic skills support, peer mentoring, library help and wellbeing services. Students who use them early often do better.

Build connections

Get to know classmates and teaching staff. Study groups, clubs and societies can make university more enjoyable and help you through demanding periods.

Know the key dates

Census dates matter: withdrawing from a unit before its census date generally means you will not be charged for it and it will not appear as a fail. If you are unsure about a unit or course, get advice before the census date.

Does your ATAR matter after you graduate?

Barely. Your ATAR gets you into your first course, and after that it stops mattering. Employers do not ask for it. Once you are at university, your university marks are what count, including if you want to transfer courses.

So while the ATAR feels like everything in Year 12, its job is narrow and short-lived: it opens the door to your next step, and then it is done.

When your ATAR still matters later

There are a few situations where your ATAR remains relevant after you start university. Some scholarships use it, particularly for first-year students. If you transfer early in your degree, it may be considered alongside your university results. And if you apply for university again later, your ATAR does not expire. In almost every case, though, your university results quickly become more important.

What employers look at instead

Employers usually focus on more recent and relevant evidence: university results, qualifications, work experience, skills and how you present yourself in applications and interviews. Our guide on whether your GPA matters after graduation looks at what employers consider.

What employers and others see later

Once you have completed further study, the people who assess you will usually look at very different evidence from your ATAR.

  • Graduate employers often consider university results, work experience, skills, interviews and assessments.
  • Postgraduate programs usually look at your undergraduate results and sometimes admissions tests, interviews or experience.
  • Professional bodies focus on accredited qualifications and supervised experience.
  • Most other employers look at your experience, skills and references.

That is why, while a good ATAR helps you start, what you do from there matters far more over time.

How the meaning of a good ATAR stays stable

Students sometimes worry that a good ATAR in one year will not be a good ATAR in the next. Because the ATAR represents a position in the age group, its meaning is designed to stay stable from year to year: an ATAR of 80 places you in roughly the top 20 per cent of your age group whatever the year.

What does change is the demand for particular courses and the number of places universities offer. When a course becomes more popular or reduces places, its threshold can rise; when demand eases or places increase, it can fall. That is why the same ATAR can open a course one year and narrowly miss it the next, even though the ATAR itself means the same thing.

The practical lesson is to judge your ATAR against the most recent thresholds, build in a buffer and keep options across your range. Our 2026 release dates guide explains when each state’s results and offers arrive.

Is the ATAR the only thing that matters?

No, and this is worth remembering when you judge whether yours is “good enough”. For many courses the ATAR is one part of the picture. Some degrees require specific subjects as prerequisites, so a strong ATAR without the right subjects still will not get you in.

Competitive courses often add their own hurdles. Medicine asks for an admissions test and an interview. Creative degrees may want a portfolio or audition. Some universities weigh a personal statement or prior experience. So a “good” ATAR gets you into the running, and the other requirements decide the offer. Check every requirement for your course early, not just the ATAR cut-off.

Prerequisites and assumed knowledge

A prerequisite is a subject, or a level of achievement in a subject, that you must have to be eligible for a course. Assumed knowledge is what a course expects you to know; if you have not studied it, you may still be eligible but may be advised to complete a bridging course. Check both well before you choose your senior subjects.

Additional criteria

Many courses use criteria beyond the ATAR, such as admissions tests, interviews, portfolios, auditions, personal statements or questionnaires. For these courses, a good ATAR is necessary but not sufficient. Register for any tests or interviews on time and prepare for them as seriously as for your exams.

Early offers

Some universities make offers before ATARs are released, through early offer or school recommendation schemes that use Year 11 results or other criteria. Some of these offers are conditional on achieving a minimum ATAR or meeting a prerequisite.

Each student starts from a specific course, uses the most recent data, accounts for adjustments, adds a sensible buffer and tracks progress with estimates. That is the practical meaning of a good ATAR: one tied to a goal and planned with a margin.

Setting a realistic target

The healthiest way to use all of this is to set a target you can act on. Pick the course you want, find its most recent cut-off, and add a few points as a buffer against a busy year. That single number becomes your goal for the rest of Year 12.

Then work backwards. An estimate from your current marks tells you how far you have to go, and which subjects will move your rank most. A clear target beats worrying about whether some abstract ATAR is “good”.

Example targets

  • A student aiming for a course with a recent lowest selection rank of 78, who expects a 3-point regional adjustment, might set an ATAR target of around 78, giving a selection rank buffer of a few points.
  • A student aiming for a course with a recent lowest selection rank of 92 and no expected adjustments might set a target of 94 or 95.
  • A student aiming for a course that also uses an interview and admissions test might set an ATAR target with a buffer and plan preparation for the other criteria.

These examples are illustrative; your own target depends on your course, your adjustments and the most recent data.

Using your ATAR to build a strong preference list

A good ATAR is most useful when it is matched with a well-built preference list. Here is how to build one.

Put your genuine first choice first

You are offered the highest preference you are eligible for, so listing a course you want less above one you want more can cost you the better option. Put your dream course first, even if it is a stretch.

Cover your whole range

Include aspirational courses near or above the top of your likely range, realistic courses within it and at least one safe course below it. That way, wherever your ATAR lands, you have an option you are happy with.

Use all your preferences wisely

Each admissions centre allows a different number of preferences: UAC allows five, QTAC and TISC allow six, and VTAC allows eight. Use them to cover your range rather than listing several near-identical courses at the same level.

Review after results

Most admissions centres allow you to change your preferences after results are released, up to each round’s deadline. Once you know your ATAR, review your list and adjust it if your result is higher or lower than expected.

From a good ATAR to a good start at university

Once you start university, your ATAR quickly gives way to your university results. A good start there matters more for your future than the ATAR that got you in.

University results work differently

University grades are usually based on how well you meet the standards of each unit, not on your rank against other students. Universities combine your grades into measures such as a grade point average or weighted average mark, which are used for transfers, scholarships and further study. A good first year

Attend classes, use feedback, get to know your teaching staff and classmates, and use support services early. Students with modest ATARs often do very well at university when they engage fully with their course.

Thinking about a good ATAR in Year 10, 11 and 12

Year 10

It is too early to know your ATAR, but not too early to think about the courses that interest you, their prerequisites and your state’s eligibility rules. Choose subjects you are good at and enjoy.

Year 11

Build strong habits and test your subject choices. In Tasmania, eligible courses this year may count towards your TE Score. A first estimate can give you a rough sense of your range.

Year 12

Set a target based on the course you want, with a buffer, and track your progress with estimates as your results come in. Prepare for any additional criteria and build a preference list across your range.

  • Which adjustment factors apply to this course, and how are they capped?
  • Are there prerequisites, assumed knowledge or additional criteria?
  • What pathways lead into this course if I do not reach the threshold?

A term-by-term plan towards your target

Term 1: set your target

Choose the courses you are interested in, find their most recent thresholds and requirements, and set a target with a buffer. Run a first estimate from your current results to see the gap.

Term 2: build momentum

Work steadily in every subject, use feedback from each assessment and re-run your estimate. Identify the subjects that move your estimate most and give them extra attention.

Term 3: test yourself

Use trial or practice exams to test your preparation under exam conditions. Update your estimate and draft your preference list across your likely range. Check adjustment scheme and course deadlines.

Term 4: finish strongly

Prepare thoroughly for your final exams, look after your sleep and wellbeing, and review your preference list before the deadlines that matter.

December: compare and decide

When your ATAR arrives, work out your selection ranks, compare them with thresholds and adjust your preferences before the next deadline.

A step-by-step plan if your ATAR is higher than you expected

  1. Check your results. Make sure everything is listed correctly.
  2. Recalculate your selection ranks. Add any adjustments you are eligible for.
  3. Look for new options. Check whether courses you left off your list are now within reach.
  4. Check requirements. Confirm prerequisites, assumed knowledge and any additional criteria before adding a course.
  5. Choose by interest. Only move a course higher if you genuinely want it more; do not choose a course just because its threshold is higher.
  6. Look at scholarships. Some scholarships use the ATAR, and some are still open after results.
  7. Update your preferences before the deadline. Make sure your list is in the order you genuinely want.

A good ATAR is relative to you

There is no single number that counts as a good ATAR for everyone. A good ATAR is the one that gets you into the course and life you want. For one student that might be 95, for another 70.

So the useful question is not 'what is a good ATAR?' but 'what ATAR do I need for my goal?'. Measured against your own plans, a good ATAR is simply one that opens the door you are aiming at.

Different students, different good ATARs

  • For a student who wants to study nursing at their local university, an ATAR comfortably above that course’s threshold is an excellent result, whatever the number.
  • For a student aiming for a highly competitive course, a strong ATAR may need to be paired with a strong interview and admissions test.
  • For a student planning to start with a diploma and transfer, the ATAR matters less than the pathway’s entry requirements.
  • For a student heading into an apprenticeship or work, the ATAR may not matter at all.

In each case, the right question is not whether the number is high but whether it opens the door the student wants.

Mistakes people make when judging an ATAR

Treating it as a percentage

An ATAR of 70 is not “70 per cent”. It means you performed better than about 70 per cent of your age group, and it is close to the typical result among ATAR recipients.

Comparing with the wrong group

Comparing your ATAR with friends who share their results on social media gives a distorted picture, because the highest results are shared most. Compare with the typical result among recipients, or better, with your course thresholds.

Ignoring adjustments

Judging an ATAR only against published thresholds ignores adjustment factors. Your selection rank may be several points higher for some courses.

Treating thresholds as fixed

Thresholds move from year to year and between rounds. A result just below last year’s threshold may still lead to an offer, and one just above it is not guaranteed.

Forgetting other requirements

A high ATAR does not overcome a missing prerequisite or a missed interview registration. Check every requirement.

Choosing a course to match the ATAR

Choosing the highest-threshold course you can reach, rather than the one you want, often leads to dissatisfaction. Choose by interest and fit.

Assuming a lower ATAR closes doors permanently

Pathways, transfers and later study keep many doors open. A lower ATAR changes the route, not necessarily the destination.

A checklist for deciding whether your ATAR is good for you

  1. I have read my ATAR as a position in my age group, not a percentage.
  2. I know the typical ATAR among recipients in my state.
  3. I have worked out my selection rank for each course, including adjustments.
  4. I have compared my selection ranks with each course’s most recent lowest selection rank.
  5. I have checked every other requirement, such as prerequisites and interviews.
  6. I have at least one realistic and one safe option on my preference list.
  7. I know the pathways into my preferred course if I fall short.
  8. I know the next change of preference deadline.
  9. I have talked my options through with someone I trust.

What school results mean for you

Schools often publish figures such as their median ATAR or the share of students above 90. These describe a group of students in a particular year. They say little about any individual student’s ATAR, which depends on their own results under the same rules as every other student in the state. A strong student at any school can achieve a high ATAR, and a school’s median says nothing about whether your ATAR is good for your goals.

Reflecting on your result

Once the immediate decisions are made, it can help to reflect on your result in a broader way.

What it says, and what it does not

Your ATAR says something about your Year 12 results in your chosen subjects, relative to your age group, at one point in time. It does not say anything about your character, creativity, kindness, practical skills or potential.

What you learned

Year 12 builds habits that last: managing time, working through difficult material, responding to feedback and coping with pressure. Those habits will matter at university and beyond, whatever your ATAR.

What comes next

Your next step, whether a degree, a pathway, training or work, is where your future is built. Many people find that the path they took after Year 12 matters far more than the number that started it.

Final thoughts on what makes an ATAR good

A good ATAR is not a single number. It is a result that, with any adjustments you are eligible for, opens the course you want, at an institution that suits you, while you meet the course’s other requirements. For one student that might be 65, for another 95.

The general benchmarks help you understand where you stand: around 70 is typical among ATAR recipients in the largest states, 80 places you in roughly the top 20 per cent of your age group, and 90 in roughly the top 10 per cent. But the decisions that matter are made course by course, using selection ranks, thresholds and requirements.

Whatever your result, remember that pathways, transfers and later study keep many doors open, and that your ATAR is a tool for one step. What you do next will shape your future far more than the number that starts it.

Your ATAR is not your worth

It is worth saying plainly: your ATAR is a rank for university entry, not a measure of your intelligence, your character or your future. Many successful people did not get the ATAR they hoped for.

So take it seriously without letting it define you. It matters for a specific purpose, and there are many pathways beyond it. Keeping that perspective helps you make calm, sensible decisions, whatever your result.

Keeping perspective

Results day can bring strong feelings, whether your ATAR is higher or lower than you hoped. It helps to remember that the ATAR is a tool for one step: first entry to university. Many people build successful and satisfying lives through every kind of route, including pathways, vocational training, work and later study. If disappointment or stress is weighing on you, talk to someone you trust, such as a parent, teacher, careers adviser or school counsellor.

What to do with your ATAR: common scenarios

Your ATAR is above your target

Congratulations. Review your preferences before the next deadline in case courses you left off are now within reach, and check every requirement before adding them.

Your ATAR is close to your target

Work out your selection rank for each course, including adjustments. If you are just below a threshold, keep the course on your list if you genuinely want it, because thresholds can move between rounds, and make sure you have realistic and safe options as well.

Your ATAR is below your target

Look at related courses at other institutions, pathway programs into your preferred course and adjustment schemes you may be eligible for. Many students reach their goal by a different route.

You are unsure what you want to study

Use your ATAR to see which courses are realistic, then choose one that interests you and keeps options open. Many degrees allow you to explore before specialising, and transfers are common.

You are considering a gap year

Your ATAR remains valid. You can often defer an offer or apply again the following year. Check deferral rules before you accept an offer.

Key terms

ATAR

Australian Tertiary Admission Rank: your position relative to your age group in your state, from 0.00 to 99.95.

Median ATAR

The middle ATAR among students who receive one. UAC reported 70.75 in NSW in 2025.

Selection rank

Your ATAR plus any adjustment factors for a particular course at a particular institution.

Lowest selection rank

The lowest selection rank to which an offer was made for a course in a particular round, often called the cut-off.

ATAR profile

A university’s summary of the lowest, median and highest ranks of students offered a place in a course.

Guaranteed entry rank

A selection rank at or above which some institutions guarantee an offer for a course, provided all other requirements are met.

Adjustment factors

Points added to your ATAR for particular courses, often called bonus points.

Prerequisite

A subject or level of achievement you must have to be eligible for a course.

Pathway

An alternative route into a degree, such as a diploma, enabling program or internal transfer.

Looking after yourself around results

The lead-up to results and results day itself can be stressful. A few habits help.

  • Limit comparisons. Mute group chats if they make you anxious. The results shared most loudly are rarely typical.
  • Plan before the day. Know your options across your range in advance, so results day is about choosing, not panicking.
  • Take your time. You usually have days, not minutes, before the next change of preference deadline.
  • Talk to someone. A parent, teacher, careers adviser or school counsellor can help you think through your options.
  • Keep perspective. Your ATAR is a tool for one step, not a measure of your worth.

If stress, worry or low mood are getting in the way of everyday life, reach out to someone you trust or your GP. Support is available, and asking for it early makes a difference.

What is a cut-off?

A cut-off usually refers to the lowest selection rank, including adjustment factors, that received an offer for a course in a particular round. It is a guide, not a fixed minimum or a guarantee.

Why do cut-offs change each year?

Cut-offs reflect the number of places and the number and strength of applicants who list a course highly. If demand rises or places fall, the cut-off can rise, and vice versa.

What is a good ATAR in WA?

In Western Australia, the ATAR is calculated from the TEA, which includes bonuses for a language other than English and advanced mathematics. A good ATAR is one that, with any adjustments, meets the threshold of the course you want at a WA or interstate institution.

What is a good ATAR in South Australia?

SATAC calculates ATARs from the university aggregate. A good ATAR in South Australia is one that meets the selection rank needed for your preferred course, after any adjustments. Note that SATAC’s first ATAR-based offers in 2026 come on 14 January 2027.

The University of Tasmania explains that an ATAR of 80 means a TE Score at or above that of 80 per cent of Tasmanian Year 12 school leavers. A good ATAR is one that opens the course you want, at UTAS or elsewhere.

Because a larger share of ACT students receive an ATAR, and the ATAR compares students with the whole age group, the distribution among ACT recipients sits higher. The median among ACT recipients was 78.35 in 2025, compared with 70.75 in NSW.

Do Victorian students need a higher ATAR?

No. The ATAR represents the same position in the age group in every state. Thresholds for particular courses at particular institutions reflect local demand, not a different ATAR scale.

When do I find out if my ATAR is good enough?

When you receive your ATAR and compare your selection ranks with course thresholds, and finally when offers are released. In 2026, ATARs are released between 10 and 20 December, and the first ATAR-based offers from 23 December in most states.

Is my ATAR good enough for an offer in round one?

Compare your selection rank for each course with the most recent threshold for the corresponding round. If your selection rank is comfortably above it and you meet all requirements, an offer is likely, but not guaranteed, because thresholds can move.

What are offer rounds?

Offer rounds are scheduled dates on which admissions centres release offers. They run from early offers in the year before study through the main ATAR-based rounds in December and January to later rounds in February.

Do thresholds change between rounds?

They can. Thresholds for later rounds may differ from earlier ones as places are filled and applicants accept or decline offers. Some courses still have places in later rounds.

What is a change of preference deadline?

It is the date by which you must finalise your preferences for a particular offer round. Most admissions centres allow changes after results are released, up to each round’s deadline.

Can I change my preferences after seeing my ATAR?

Yes. Most admissions centres allow you to change your preferences after results, up to each round’s deadline. Many students adjust their lists once they can compare their selection rank with thresholds.

What is a good ATAR for a university diploma?

University diplomas and college programs often have their own entry requirements, which may include a minimum ATAR lower than the associated degree, or other criteria. Check each program’s requirements.

What is a good ATAR for an enabling program?

Enabling programs often do not require an ATAR and are designed for students who need to build skills before degree study. Check each program’s eligibility criteria.

What is a good ATAR for a regional campus?

Thresholds at regional campuses vary by course and are often different from metropolitan campuses. Regional students may also be eligible for adjustments. Check each course and campus.

Can a good ATAR get me into a course at a university I have never heard of?

Yes, and it may be an excellent choice. Many institutions offer strong courses with lower thresholds because of their location, size or profile. Judge each course on its content and fit.

How do I decide between a dream course and a safe course?

List your dream course first and the safe course lower down. You will be offered the highest preference you are eligible for, so you do not have to choose between them in advance.

What if my dream course has a cut-off above 99?

Courses with very high thresholds usually also use additional criteria such as interviews and admissions tests. Prepare for those as well, list the course first if you genuinely want it, and plan alternatives and pathways in case you do not receive an offer.

Is it possible to get into medicine without a 99?

Some medical programs consider applicants with ATARs below 99, especially when combined with strong admissions test and interview results, or through rural, equity or graduate-entry pathways. Requirements differ between programs, so check each one.

Is it possible to study law without a 99?

Yes. Many law programs have thresholds below 99, especially outside the most competitive institutions, and some universities offer law as a graduate degree after a first degree.

What is a good ATAR for a double degree?

Double or combined degrees often have higher thresholds than either component. Check the specific combined program’s threshold and requirements.

Can I study what I want with an average ATAR?

Often, yes, either directly at some institutions or through pathways and transfers. An average ATAR among recipients, around 70 in the largest states, opens many general degrees.

What is a realistic ATAR for me?

A realistic ATAR is the range your current results suggest, based on a calculator built for your state and your teachers’ views. Run cautious, realistic and optimistic scenarios to see your range.

Should my target be my estimate or higher?

Your target should be based on the course you want, with a buffer above its threshold. If that target is above your realistic estimate, use it to guide your effort and plan pathways and alternatives as well.

How much can I improve my ATAR in Year 12?

It depends on your starting point, your subjects and your effort. Steady work, good use of feedback and thorough exam preparation can lift results in the subjects that count. In the crowded middle of the range, small improvements can move the ATAR noticeably.

Does improving one subject raise my ATAR much?

It depends whether the subject counts and where your aggregate sits. Improving a counted subject raises your aggregate directly, and in the middle of the range that can move your ATAR more than you might expect.

Which matters more, my best subject or my weakest?

Your weakest counted subject often matters more than students expect, because improving it raises your aggregate directly. Your best subject already contributes strongly. Subjects outside your counted results matter mainly as buffers.

Is a good ATAR worth the stress?

A good ATAR is useful, but no ATAR is worth damaging your health. Steady, sustainable work usually produces better results than extreme pressure. Keep perspective and ask for support if stress becomes overwhelming.

How do I talk to my parents about my ATAR?

Share how you read your ATAR, which courses it opens and what your plan is. Parents often worry less when they see a clear plan with options across your range.

What should I do if I feel my ATAR does not reflect my ability?

That is a common feeling, and the ATAR is a narrow measure of results at one point in time. Focus on what it opens, consider pathways and remember that your university results and later achievements will soon matter far more.

Can I appeal my ATAR?

Your ATAR is the output of a fixed calculation. If you believe a subject result is wrong, contact your school or curriculum authority about that result promptly, as review processes have deadlines. If the result changes, your ATAR is recalculated.

Does my ATAR appear on my academic transcript at university?

Your university transcript records your university results. Your ATAR is part of your Year 12 record and admission information rather than your university transcript.

Do universities publish ATAR profiles?

Many do. ATAR profiles typically show the lowest, median and highest ranks of students offered places in each course, sometimes separately for ATAR-based and other admissions.

What are the main things that decide an offer?

Your selection rank for the course, the order of your preferences, the number of places, the course’s threshold for the round and whether you meet prerequisites and any additional criteria.

Is a good ATAR the most important part of Year 12?

It matters for university entry, but Year 12 also builds skills, habits and relationships that last. Many students find that keeping a balanced perspective helps both their wellbeing and their results.

Can a diploma lead into the second year of a degree?

Many university diplomas and college programs are designed to lead into the second year of a related degree once completed with the required results. Arrangements differ between institutions, so check how much credit a diploma gives and what results you need.

Is a pathway student treated differently at university?

No. Students who enter through pathways study the same degree and graduate with the same qualification as students who enter directly. A degree testamur does not say how its holder was first admitted.

Do pathways take longer?

Some add time, but many are designed to minimise it, for example diplomas that lead into the second year of a degree. Check the total time and credit involved before choosing.

How do I choose a starting course for a transfer?

Choose a course whose first-year units overlap with your target course, so your studies count if you transfer. Ask the university which courses are usual starting points and what results you need to transfer.

What results do I need to transfer?

Transfer requirements differ by course and institution. They are usually based on your university results, such as a grade point average or weighted average mark, and some courses have limited transfer places.

Can TAFE give me credit towards a degree?

Some TAFE diplomas and advanced diplomas give credit towards related degrees, and they can provide a basis for admission. Check the credit arrangements between the TAFE and the university.

What is an equity scheme?

An equity scheme considers students whose education has been affected by circumstances such as hardship, illness, disability or disrupted schooling. Examples include UAC’s Educational Access Schemes, VTAC’s Special Entry Access Scheme and QTAC’s Educational Access Scheme. Eligible students may receive adjustments.

Do I need documents for an equity scheme?

Usually, yes. Equity schemes typically require an application with supporting documents, such as statements from schools, doctors or other professionals. Check each scheme’s requirements and deadlines.

Is it too late to apply for adjustments after results?

Some schemes have deadlines after results day, while others close earlier. Check each scheme’s dates as soon as possible.

What does a good ATAR mean for a mature-age student?

Mature-age students may be assessed on their ATAR, if they have one, or on more recent study, work experience, enabling programs or tests such as the Special Tertiary Admissions Test. A good basis for admission is one that meets the course’s requirements.

Does my ATAR expire if I wait years to apply?

No. Your ATAR does not expire. Universities may also consider more recent study or experience and use whichever basis gives you the strongest rank.

Can I combine my ATAR with later study?

Universities usually consider your strongest basis for admission. If you complete further study, it may be assessed alongside or instead of your ATAR, depending on the institution’s rules.

How do I know what my result means compared with last year?

Because the ATAR represents roughly the same position in the age group each year, an ATAR of 80 means much the same thing in different years. What changes is course thresholds, so compare your selection rank with the most recent thresholds.

No. Your ATAR reflects Year 12 results in your chosen subjects at one point in time. It says nothing about your character, creativity, kindness or potential.

Managing time, working through difficult material, responding to feedback, communicating clearly and coping with pressure. These skills matter at university, at work and in life, whatever your ATAR.

Is it normal to change plans after results?

Yes. Many students adjust their plans once they see their ATAR and options. Changing course, choosing a pathway or taking a gap year are all common and sensible choices.

How do I make a decision I will not regret?

Choose a course you are genuinely interested in, at an institution that suits you, and make sure you understand the options for changing direction later. Talk it through with people you trust before deciding.

What if I cannot decide between two offers?

Compare the courses’ content, structure, location, support and career outcomes, and talk to students or staff at each institution. Remember that internal transfers and later study allow you to change direction if needed.

What is the difference between ATAR and selection rank on an offer?

Offers are based on selection ranks, which include adjustments. Your ATAR is the same for every course; your selection rank can differ from course to course.

Will a good ATAR help me get a job?

Not directly. Employers generally focus on university results, qualifications, experience and skills. Your ATAR helps you start the study that leads to those.

As a tool for one step: getting into the course you want. Use it to plan, compare options and make decisions, and then let your next step become the focus.

Which subjects help me get a good ATAR?

Subjects you are good at and enjoy usually help most, because your ATAR depends on where you finish in each subject relative to other students. Meet your state’s ATAR rules and the prerequisites for courses you want, and use scaling only as a tie-breaker.

Do I need maths for a good ATAR?

Mathematics is not compulsory for the ATAR in most states, but many university courses require or assume it. If you are interested in engineering, science, health or commerce, mathematics choices can be important.

In states where only your best results count, an extra subject can protect you if one goes badly. It only helps if you can do reasonably well in it without harming your other results.

Engineering courses often require or assume higher-level mathematics, and sometimes physics or chemistry. Requirements differ between institutions, so check each course.

What prerequisites do health courses have?

Do arts courses have prerequisites?

Many arts, humanities and social science courses have few or no prerequisites beyond English. Check each course’s requirements.

Prepare thoroughly for your final exams, look after your wellbeing and review your preference list before the relevant deadlines.

Do thresholds differ between first and later rounds?

They can. As offers are accepted or declined, later rounds may have different thresholds, and some courses still have places after the main rounds.

How do I find out a course’s guaranteed entry rank?

Universities that offer guaranteed entry publish the rank on their course pages or admissions information. Not every course or institution offers one.

What is the difference between a cut-off and a guaranteed entry rank?

A cut-off is the lowest selection rank that received an offer in a past round, so it describes what happened. A guaranteed entry rank is a promise for the coming intake: if your selection rank is at or above it and you meet all other requirements, you will receive an offer.

Are guaranteed entry ranks higher than cut-offs?

They can be higher, lower or similar, depending on the course and the institution’s approach. Some institutions make offers below their guaranteed entry rank when demand allows. Check each course.

How do I compare ATAR profiles?

Can I get an offer below the median of an ATAR profile?

Should I worry if my ATAR is below my friends’?

Can I predict a good ATAR from Year 11 results?

Only roughly. Year 11 results give an indication, but Year 12 results, exams, moderation and scaling all affect the final ATAR. Use early estimates for exploration, not prediction.

How do teachers know what ATAR I might get?

Teachers see your work and know how past students with similar results performed. That helps them estimate where you are likely to finish in their subject, which is a key input for any ATAR estimate.

Do selective schools produce better ATARs?

Averages differ between schools for many reasons, including which students they enrol. But no bonus is applied for attending any school, and your ATAR depends on your own results under the same rules as every other student.

Is a good ATAR harder to get at a competitive school?

Does tutoring guarantee a good ATAR?

No. Tutoring can help with specific needs, but results depend on many factors, including consistent study, use of feedback and exam preparation. Many students achieve good ATARs without tutoring.

How many hours should I study for a good ATAR?

Is it ever too late to improve my ATAR?

Does a good ATAR require top marks in every subject?

No. High ATARs come from strong, consistent results across the subjects that count. No state requires top marks in every subject.

Can one bad exam ruin a good ATAR?

Rarely. Your ATAR combines several subjects, and in most states each subject result combines school assessment with the exam. One disappointing exam usually lowers one subject result rather than ruining your ATAR.
