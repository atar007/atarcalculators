How accurate are ATAR calculators?

An ATAR calculator can be genuinely useful, if you understand what it can and cannot do. This honest guide explains how accurate they are, why they estimate, and how to use one well.

ATAR calculators give a close estimate, not your exact ATAR. Your real ATAR depends on how every student performs and on the exact scaling for that year, which is only known once all results are in. A good calculator, using the latest official scaling data, gets close, but it can never be exact until results are finalised.

Key takeaways

  • ATAR calculators give an estimate, not your exact result.
  • Your real ATAR depends on how everyone else performs that year.
  • Accuracy depends on the scaling data the calculator uses.
  • A good calculator uses the latest official scaling from your state.
  • Estimates are most accurate near the top and middle of the range.
  • Use an estimate to set targets and plan, not as a guarantee.

Why calculators can only estimate

Your ATAR is a rank, so it depends on how every other student performs, not just on you. That information does not exist until all results are finalised. No calculator can know it in advance.

On top of that, the exact scaling changes a little each year with the cohort. A calculator has to use last year’s scaling as its best guide. Both facts mean a calculator gives a close estimate, never the exact final number.

What a calculator knows, and what it cannot know

It helps to separate the information an ATAR calculator has from the information it would need to produce your exact ATAR. A calculator knows the rules your state uses to build an aggregate, because those rules are published. It knows how subjects scaled in past years, because admissions centres publish scaling reports. And it knows whatever marks you type in. That is a solid foundation for an estimate.

What a calculator cannot know is how this year’s students will perform. Your ATAR is a rank, so it depends on the results of tens of thousands of other students in your state. Their results do not exist until the end of the year. Scaling for this year is calculated from those results, and the conversion from aggregate to ATAR is calculated from them too. Until every result is in and processed, those pieces are simply missing.

The calculator also cannot know your own final results until they are released. Most students enter school assessment marks, trial exam marks or marks they expect to achieve. Each of those is an approximation of the final, moderated or combined result that will actually be used in your ATAR.

Why the rank depends on everyone else

Think of the ATAR as your place in a very long queue made up of your whole age group in your state. Your position depends on how many people are ahead of you. If you improve, you move forward. But if many other students improve too, you may not move forward as far as you expected. A calculator can estimate where you would have stood in last year’s queue, but this year’s queue has different people in it.

In practice, the age group in a large state is big enough that it behaves in a fairly stable way from year to year. That is why calculators based on recent data can give useful estimates. But stable does not mean identical, and small shifts in the distribution of results can move a given aggregate up or down by a fraction of a point or more.

Why scaling has to be recalculated each year

Scaling is the process that places results in different subjects on a common scale. It depends on how strong each subject’s students are across all their subjects. Because the students taking each subject change every year, scaling has to be recalculated every year. A calculator built before results are released has to use the most recent scaling available, which describes last year’s students, not yours.

For most subjects, scaling moves only a little from one year to the next. For some subjects, especially those with small or changing cohorts, it can move more. A calculator that uses last year’s scaling will be close for stable subjects and less precise for volatile ones. Our guide to how scaling changes from year to year explains why some subjects move more than others.

Why moderation adds another layer

In most states, school assessment marks are adjusted before they are used, so that marks from different schools can be compared fairly. This process is called moderation. In NSW, your HSC mark in each course combines your moderated school assessment mark and your exam mark in equal parts. In Western Australia, your combined score for an ATAR course is the average of your moderated school mark and your exam mark. In South Australia and the Northern Territory, Stage 2 subjects combine school assessment, worth 70 per cent, with external assessment, worth 30 per cent, and school assessment is moderated. In Queensland, external assessment contributes 25 per cent of the result in most General subjects and 50 per cent in mathematics and science subjects.

Moderation usually depends on how your school group performs in the external exam. If your school’s cohort does better or worse in the exam than its school marks suggested, school marks can move up or down. A calculator cannot know this in advance. If you enter a raw school mark, your estimate will be based on a number that may change before it reaches your ATAR.

The five places uncertainty enters an estimate

Every ATAR estimate follows a chain of steps, from the marks you type in to the number on the screen. Uncertainty can enter at each step. Understanding where it comes from helps you judge how much to trust a particular estimate and what you can do to improve it.

Step 1: The marks you enter

This is almost always the biggest source of error. If you enter the marks you expect rather than marks you have actually achieved, your estimate inherits all the uncertainty in those expectations. Students often overestimate their exam performance, especially in subjects they enjoy. Others underestimate themselves out of caution. Either way, the estimate can only be as good as the inputs.

Even real marks are not final. A school assessment mark in Term 2 is not the same as your final assessment mark, which may include later tasks. A trial exam mark is a useful guide to your final exam mark, but the final exam is a different paper, sat under different conditions, marked by different markers, and compared with a different group of students.

Step 2: Moderation

Moderation rarely changes the order of students within a school group, but it can change the size of the marks. If you enter a raw school mark of 85 and your school’s group performs less strongly in the exam than its marks suggested, your moderated mark could be lower. If the group performs more strongly, it could be higher. Some calculators let you enter exam-style marks to reduce this effect. Most simply use what you enter.

Step 3: Scaling

A calculator uses published scaling from a past year. For a large, stable subject, the scaling that applies to your year is likely to be close to the past year’s. For a small or changing subject, it may differ more. The effect is usually modest for any one subject, but it can add up across several subjects, particularly if they all move in the same direction.

Step 4: The aggregate

Each state builds its aggregate differently. A calculator that models those rules correctly will build the same aggregate the admissions centre would, given the same scaled results. A calculator that simplifies the rules, for example by ignoring the NSW requirement to include 2 units of English or the Victorian increments for fifth and sixth studies, can produce a different aggregate even from the same inputs.

Step 5: The rank

Finally, the aggregate is converted to an ATAR. Officially, that conversion is calculated from the current year’s age group. A calculator uses a conversion from a past year. In large states, this conversion is fairly stable, but it shifts a little each year, and the shift can be larger at some points in the range than others.

The practical lesson
You can control step 1 by entering the most realistic marks you can, and step 4 by choosing a calculator that models your state’s rules properly. Steps 2, 3 and 5 are outside anyone’s control until results are released, which is why every estimate should be treated as a range.

A short guide to scaling for calculator users

Scaling is the step that most ATAR calculators work hardest to model, and it is the step most students understand least. You do not need to know the mathematics, but a clear picture of what scaling does will help you read any estimate more sensibly.

What scaling is for

Students take different combinations of subjects, and a mark of 80 in one subject does not necessarily represent the same level of achievement as a mark of 80 in another. Scaling places results from different subjects on a common scale, so that they can be added together fairly to form an aggregate. Without scaling, students would be rewarded or penalised simply for their choice of subjects.

How scaling decides a subject’s position

Scaling looks at how the students in each subject perform across all their subjects. If a subject’s students tend to perform strongly in their other subjects, the subject’s results are placed relatively high on the common scale. If they tend to perform less strongly, the results are placed lower. Scaling does not reward a subject for being difficult in itself; it reflects the strength of the group taking it.

Why scaling varies along the range

Scaling is not a single number added to every result. It is applied along the whole range of results in a subject, and its effect can differ at the top, in the middle and at the bottom. VTAC, for example, notes that the adjustment for a study is different at each study score and changes as the score moves away from the average. That is why a good calculator applies scaling at your level of result, not a headline figure.

How the states differ

Every state uses a scaling process, but the details differ. In NSW, scaled marks are reported per unit, out of 50. In Victoria, study scores are scaled, and VTAC applies a policy adjustment to languages that affects their scaling. In Queensland, QTAC uses an iterative inter-subject scaling process. In Western Australia, TISC scales each ATAR course score. In South Australia and the NT, SATAC produces scaled scores but does not publish them for each subject. In Tasmania and the ACT, local processes apply.

What this means for estimates

Because scaling is recalculated every year from the current students, a calculator can only apply last year’s version. For large, stable subjects, this works well. For small or changing subjects, it adds uncertainty. Our guides to ATAR scaling and common scaling myths go into more detail.

Moderation explained, state by state

Moderation is the process that makes school assessment marks comparable across schools. It matters for estimates because it can change your school marks after you have entered them into a calculator. The approach differs between states.

NSW

In NSW, each school’s assessment marks for a course are moderated using the exam marks of the same group of students. The order of students within the school group is kept, but the size of the marks and the gaps between them can change to match how the group performed in the exam. Your HSC mark then combines your moderated assessment mark and your exam mark in equal parts.

Victoria

In Victoria, school-assessed coursework is statistically moderated using students’ performance in the external examinations. As in NSW, the order of students within the school group is generally preserved, but the scores can be adjusted to align with exam performance.

Queensland

In Queensland, internal assessment in General subjects is quality assured through processes called endorsement and confirmation, in which the QCAA reviews assessment instruments and samples of student work. The QCAA has stated that external assessment results do not scale internal assessment results. That makes internal results more predictable for calculator users, although confirmation can still change provisional marks.

Western Australia

In Western Australia, school marks for each ATAR course are statistically moderated using the course’s examination marks. Your combined score is then the average of your moderated school mark and your exam mark.

South Australia and the NT

In South Australia and the NT, school assessment for Stage 2 subjects is moderated to make sure grading is consistent with each subject’s performance standards across schools. School assessment makes up 70 per cent of each subject, and external assessment the other 30 per cent.

What moderation means for your estimate

If you enter raw school marks into a calculator, remember that they may change through moderation before they contribute to your ATAR. Your rank within your school group is often a better guide than the raw mark. If you can, base your inputs on exam-style marks, such as trial exams marked against official guidelines, as well as your school results.

What makes an ATAR calculator accurate

The single biggest factor is the scaling data. A calculator that uses the latest official scaling report from your state will be far closer than one using old or made-up numbers.

The method matters too. A good calculator scales each subject, combines your best results the way your state does, and then maps that to a rank. A calculator that just averages your marks is not really estimating an ATAR at all. See how ATAR is calculated.

The features of an accurate ATAR calculator

Not every tool that calls itself an ATAR calculator does the same job. Some are carefully built models of a state’s official process; others are rough averaging tools. When you are deciding how much to trust a calculator, look for these features.

  • It is built for your state. Each state has its own senior certificate, its own scaling process and its own rules for building an aggregate. A single national calculator that treats every state the same way cannot be accurate for all of them.
  • It names its data source and year. A trustworthy calculator tells you which official report it uses, such as UAC’s scaling report for NSW or VTAC’s scaling report for Victoria, and which year that report covers.
  • It scales each subject separately. Scaling differs between subjects and at different points within each subject. A calculator should apply the right scaling to each subject at your level of result, not a single average adjustment.
  • It applies your state’s counting rules. It should include English where your state requires it, count the right number of subjects or units, apply any increments or bonuses, and choose your best results correctly.
  • It converts the aggregate to a rank. The final step should map your aggregate to an ATAR using published conversion data, not simply relabel your average mark.
  • It shows a range. Because an estimate is uncertain, the most honest calculators show a likely range around the central figure.
  • It explains its limitations. A calculator that is open about what it cannot do is more trustworthy than one that promises precision.

How each state’s rules shape a calculator

The rules a calculator must model are different in every state, and some are more complex than others. Here is what an accurate calculator has to get right in each system.

  • NSW: scale each course’s marks, then add your best 2 units of English and your best 8 other units, applying UAC’s rules on how many units can come from particular groups of courses.
  • Victoria: scale each study score, then add your English study and your next three best scaled studies, plus 10 per cent of your fifth and sixth, or an eligible VET increment, and apply VTAC’s rules for combinations such as multiple mathematics studies.
  • Queensland: scale each subject result, then add your best five scaled results, which can include one Applied subject or one eligible VET qualification, and check the English requirement.
  • Western Australia: scale each ATAR course score, then add your best four scaled scores, plus 10 per cent of your best language other than English, 10 per cent of Mathematics Methods and 10 per cent of Mathematics Specialist.
  • South Australia and the NT: use scaled scores for your best 90 credits, made up of three 20-credit subjects and a flexible option of 30 credits, with limits on Recognised Studies.
  • Tasmania: add your best course scores from eligible Level 3 and Level 4 courses completed over up to two years to form a TE Score, using the rules that apply from 2025.
  • ACT: add your best three major scaled scores and 60 per cent of your next major or minor, using scaled scores produced through the ACT’s own scaling process.

Our guide to how the ATAR is calculated explains each of these rules in more detail, and each state has its own guide, such as the NSW calculation and the Victorian calculation.

Why averaging marks is not an ATAR estimate

Some simple tools ask for your marks and return their average as an “ATAR”. This is not an estimate of your ATAR at all. Your ATAR is a rank against your age group, built from scaled results under your state’s rules. An average raw mark ignores scaling, ignores the rules for which results count, and ignores the conversion from aggregate to rank.

The gap can be large. UAC explains that in NSW, most HSC courses have a median mark above 75, while the median ATAR is usually around 70, and that students with marks around 70 typically receive ATARs in the 50s. A student who averages 70 in the HSC and uses an averaging tool might expect an ATAR of 70, when their actual ATAR could be well below that. In the other direction, a student with strong marks in courses that scale well might receive an ATAR above their average mark.

How accurate estimates are in each state

The accuracy of an ATAR estimate depends partly on how much information each state publishes and how complex its process is. In some states, the official reports give calculators almost everything they need. In others, important pieces are not published, and calculators have to approximate them. Here is how the states compare.

NSW

UAC publishes a detailed annual report on the scaling of the HSC, including information on how marks in each course scale and how aggregates relate to ATARs. That makes it possible to build a careful NSW calculator. The main sources of uncertainty are the marks students enter, the moderation of school assessment marks, and year-to-year changes in scaling. Because each HSC mark combines a moderated assessment mark and an exam mark in equal parts, a student who enters only school marks has left half of each final mark unknown.

Victoria

In Victoria, the main input to the ATAR is the study score, which is already a measure of your position within a study. VTAC publishes a scaling report and, on results day, a table that converts aggregates to ATARs. That gives calculators good material to work with. The main uncertainty is the study score itself: before results, students usually have to estimate it from their school assessment and practice exams, and a study score depends on how you compare with every other student in the study across the state.

Queensland

QTAC publishes information on scaling and on the ATAR each year, and it uses an iterative inter-subject scaling process. Queensland subject results combine internal assessment, confirmed by the QCAA, with external assessment, which contributes 25 per cent in most General subjects and 50 per cent in mathematics and science subjects. Students often know much of their internal assessment result before the external assessment, which makes part of their final result more predictable. The external assessment, and the year’s scaling, remain the main sources of uncertainty.

Western Australia

TISC publishes tertiary entrance statistics, including information on scaling and the relationship between the TEA and the ATAR. In Western Australia, each ATAR course combines a moderated school mark and an exam mark in equal parts, so the exam is a major source of uncertainty. Calculators also need to model the bonuses for a language other than English and for advanced mathematics correctly, because those bonuses can make a noticeable difference to the TEA.

South Australia and the Northern Territory

In South Australia and the NT, Stage 2 subjects combine school assessment, worth 70 per cent, with external assessment, worth 30 per cent. SATAC converts subject results into scaled scores, but it does not publish scaled scores for each subject, and it advises students not to choose subjects based on historical scaling. That means calculators for South Australia and the NT have to rely on more general information, and their estimates should be treated with extra caution.

Tasmania

Tasmania’s TE Score rules changed for students from 2025, so calculators that rely on data from earlier years may not reflect the current rules. The TE Score can include courses completed over up to two years, which helps students estimate more of their result in advance, because some courses may already be finished. As data under the new rules accumulates, estimates should become more reliable.

ACT

The ACT uses its own process to scale results across colleges and courses before the aggregate is formed and converted to an ATAR. That process is harder for an outside calculator to replicate than in states where scaling is based on a single statewide exam. ACT estimates are useful for planning, but they are generally more approximate than estimates for the largest states.

A rule of thumb
The more your state publishes, and the more of your final result you already know, the more accurate your estimate can be. Students who have completed more of their assessment, or whose state publishes detailed scaling, can generally rely on estimates a little more.

Key numbers behind each state’s estimates

Published figures from admissions centres help explain why estimates behave differently in different states. The numbers below come from recent official reports and statements. They change every year, so treat them as context rather than as inputs for your own estimate.

NSW: marks, medians and the gap between them

UAC’s figures for 2025 show that 60,432 students received an ATAR, out of 77,693 who completed at least one ATAR course and 68,896 who were awarded the HSC. The median ATAR was 70.75. About 17.3 per cent of ATAR recipients had an ATAR of 90 or above, 34.6 per cent had 80 or above and 51.3 per cent had 70 or above. At the very top, 53 students received 99.95.

UAC also explains that most HSC courses have a median mark above 75, while the median ATAR is usually around 70, and that students with marks around 70 typically receive ATARs in the 50s, while marks around 80 typically lead to an ATAR around 80. These relationships are a reminder that HSC marks and ATARs are on different scales, and that a calculator which simply relabels your average mark as an ATAR will be well off in the middle of the range.

Victoria: many combinations, one aggregate

VTAC reported 49,310 ATARs for 2025, up from 47,461 in 2024, with 42 students receiving 99.95. In 2024, the average ATAR was 69.52. One striking figure is the variety of combinations: VTAC has reported more than 24,000 unique study combinations in a single year. That variety is why a Victorian calculator must apply the rules correctly to any combination, not just the most common ones.

The Victorian aggregate is built from your English study and your next three best scaled studies, plus 10 per cent of your fifth and sixth, and can range from zero to over 210. VTAC publishes the table that converts aggregates to ATARs on results day, which is why estimates before then must use the previous year’s conversion.

Queensland: the best five and fine bands

In Queensland, about 30,167 students received an ATAR in 2025, and 37 received 99.95. The TEA is the sum of the best five scaled results, with a maximum of 500. The ATAR scale contains 2,000 bands, and ATARs of 30 or below are reported as “30.00 or less”. QTAC’s scaling uses an iterative process involving all eligible students’ results. QTAC has also noted that, in 2025, no subject was scaled up at every point in its range, a reminder that scaling effects vary along each subject’s results.

Western Australia: bonuses in the TEA

In Western Australia, the TEA is the sum of the best four scaled scores plus 10 per cent of the best scaled score in a language other than English, 10 per cent of the scaled score in Mathematics Methods and 10 per cent of the scaled score in Mathematics Specialist. With four scaled scores out of 100 each and three possible bonuses of up to 10 each, the maximum TEA is 430. Because the bonuses can add several points even when the related courses are not in the best four, a WA calculator that ignores them can understate the TEA.

South Australia and the NT: 90 credits

In South Australia and the NT, the university aggregate is calculated from the best 90 credits of eligible study, out of a maximum of 90. Scaled scores are out of 20 for 20-credit subjects and out of 10 for 10-credit subjects, and up to 20 credits of Recognised Studies can be included. For VET, SATAC uses a scaled score based on the average of the student’s first 70 credits of tertiary admission subjects. SATAC does not publish scaled scores for each subject, which is the main reason SA and NT estimates are approximate.

Tasmania: a new TE Score

From 2025, the Tasmanian TE Score is built from a student’s best Level 3 and Level 4 course scores over up to two years, with each course worth up to 15 points and a total of 60 to 75 points from four or five courses. To be eligible, students need their TCE and a Satisfactory Achievement or better in at least four Level 3 or 4 courses. The University of Tasmania explains that an ATAR of 80 means a TE Score at or above that of 80 per cent of Tasmanian Year 12 school leavers.

ACT: a small system with high participation

In the ACT, 5,235 students received a Senior Secondary Certificate in 2025, and about 61 per cent received a Tertiary Entrance Statement. The median ATAR was 78.35, and 78.8 per cent of ATAR recipients had an ATAR of 65 or above. The ATAR is based on the best three major scaled scores plus 60 per cent of the next major or minor.

Why these numbers matter for estimates
They show that each state’s ATAR is built differently, that the middle of the range is crowded, and that published data differs in detail between states. A calculator can only be as good as the data its state makes available.

Why two calculators give different results

If two calculators disagree, it is almost always because they use different scaling data or different methods. One might use this year’s official report; another might use figures from years ago.

Some also take shortcuts, such as ignoring how subjects are combined, or applying one state’s rules everywhere. This is why it is worth using a calculator that is transparent about its data and built for your state.

The main types of ATAR calculator

When students compare estimates, they are often comparing very different kinds of tool. Knowing which kind you are using explains a lot about why its number differs from another.

  • Data-driven state calculators. These use published scaling data and the official rules for one state, and convert an aggregate to an ATAR using published conversion data. When they are kept up to date, they are usually the most reliable estimates available before results.
  • Simplified calculators. These use a shortcut, such as a single average scaling adjustment per subject, or a rough conversion from average mark to ATAR. They can give a ballpark figure, but they miss important details.
  • Averaging tools. These average your raw marks and present the result as an ATAR. They do not estimate an ATAR at all.
  • School-based predictions. Some schools estimate students’ ATARs using their own historical data, such as how past students with similar school results performed. These can be very useful because they reflect the school’s own assessment patterns, but they depend on how many years of data the school has and how similar this year’s group is.
  • Crowd-sourced estimates. Some tools are built from results that past students have shared. They can capture real outcomes, but their data may not be representative, and it is often unclear how up to date it is.

A worked example: three calculators, one student

Imagine a NSW student, Amira, who enters the same marks into three different tools. The figures below are illustrative, not predictions, but they show how the differences arise.

The first tool is a data-driven NSW calculator that uses the most recent UAC scaling report. It scales each of her courses at her level of result, includes her 2 units of English, selects her best 8 other units, and converts her aggregate using recent conversion data. It returns an estimate of 86, with a likely range of a point or two either side.

The second tool uses the same method but relies on scaling data that is several years old. Two of Amira’s courses have scaled a little differently since then, and the conversion from aggregate to ATAR has shifted slightly. It returns 88.

The third tool simply averages her raw marks, which come to 82, and labels that as her ATAR. It ignores scaling, ignores the rules for which units count, and ignores the conversion to a rank.

None of these tools is lying, but only the first is giving Amira the best estimate it can from current information. The second is close but less current. The third is answering a different question. If Amira understood these differences, she would plan around the first estimate’s range and treat the others with caution.

Questions to ask when two estimates disagree

  1. Are both tools built for my state?
  2. Which year’s scaling does each tool use?
  3. Does each tool scale every subject separately at my level of result?
  4. Does each tool apply my state’s counting rules, including English requirements and limits?
  5. Does each tool convert the aggregate to a rank using published data?
  6. Did I enter exactly the same marks and subjects in both?

Very often, the last question explains the difference. Students sometimes enter a slightly different subject, unit count or mark in each tool without noticing. Check your inputs before you conclude that one tool is wrong.

When different estimates are actually helpful

If two well-built calculators give slightly different estimates, that difference is useful information. It shows how sensitive your estimate is to changes in data or method. A student whose estimates range from 84 to 87 has a realistic sense of the uncertainty around their position, which is more helpful than a single number from one tool.

How close is an ATAR estimate to the real result?

With good data, an estimate is usually close, often within a few points of the final ATAR. It will not be exact, but it is a reliable guide for planning.

The estimate tends to be most accurate through the middle and upper parts of the range, where most students sit. At the very top, tiny mark differences move the rank in ways no calculator can perfectly predict, so treat those estimates as approximate.

What “within a few points” really means

When people say an estimate is usually within a few points, they mean that, for students who enter realistic marks into a well-built calculator, the official ATAR typically lands near the estimate rather than far away from it. It does not mean every estimate lands within a fixed margin. Some students will be very close; a smaller number will be further away, usually because their final results were different from the marks they entered.

It is also worth remembering that the error is not always symmetrical. Students who enter optimistic marks are more likely to see an official ATAR below their estimate, and students who enter cautious marks are more likely to see one above it. The calculator is not biased in either direction; the inputs are.

Why the size of the range depends on your inputs

An estimate built from final-year school assessment marks that are close to complete is more reliable than one built from Year 11 results or from marks you hope to achieve. As the year goes on and more of your results are known, the range of plausible ATARs narrows. That is why it makes sense to re-run an estimate after each major assessment period, and to expect the estimate to settle as you approach results day.

Why a few marks matter more in some parts of the range

A change of a few marks does not have the same effect on your ATAR everywhere on the scale. This is one of the least understood features of the ATAR, and it explains why estimates can feel more or less stable for different students.

The middle of the range is crowded

Results across a whole age group tend to bunch up in the middle and thin out at the extremes. In the crowded middle, many students have similar aggregates. If your aggregate rises by a small amount, you can move past a large number of students, so your ATAR can rise by a noticeable amount. If it falls by a small amount, the reverse happens.

This means that in the middle of the range, an estimate is sensitive to small errors in the marks you enter. A few marks across your subjects can move your estimated ATAR by a point or more. That is not a flaw in the calculator; it is a feature of the ATAR itself.

The top of the range is spread out

Near the top, results are more spread out. Each step of the ATAR represents a smaller share of the age group, and there are fewer students at each level of aggregate. A small change in aggregate moves you past fewer students, so it changes your ATAR by a smaller amount.

That might suggest that estimates at the top are more precise, and in terms of ATAR points they often are. But the decisions that depend on top ATARs are made on very fine margins, such as the difference between 99.0 and 99.5 for some of the most competitive courses. At that level, the tiny differences that a calculator cannot predict, such as a single exam going slightly better or worse, can decide which side of a threshold you land on. That is why, as noted above, very high estimates are best treated as approximate.

The lower part of the range

Below the middle, the picture depends on your state and on how many students in that part of the age group receive an ATAR. Estimates can still be useful, but students in this part of the range should pay particular attention to course thresholds, adjustment factors and pathways, because small differences in selection rank can open or close specific options.

What this means for you

  • If your estimate is in the middle of the range, focus on the accuracy of your inputs. Small improvements in your marks can make a real difference to your ATAR.
  • If your estimate is near the top, treat it as approximate when you compare it with very high thresholds, and keep realistic alternatives on your preference list.
  • Wherever you are, plan around a range rather than a single figure.

Reading an estimate as a range

A single number such as 87.35 looks precise, but that precision is misleading. Before results are released, the honest answer to “what will my ATAR be?” is a range. Many calculators, including ours, show a likely range alongside the central estimate for exactly this reason.

Build three scenarios

One of the simplest ways to turn an estimate into a range is to run three versions of your results.

  • Cautious: marks a little below your current results in each subject, as if your exams went slightly less well than expected.
  • Realistic: marks in line with your current results and teacher feedback.
  • Optimistic: marks a little above your current results, as if your exams went slightly better than expected.

The three estimates give you a practical range. Plan your preferences so that you have good options across the whole of it, not just at the realistic point.

Use the range for decisions

When you compare your estimate with course thresholds, compare the range rather than the central figure. If a course’s recent lowest selection rank sits below the cautious end of your range, it is likely to be a realistic option. If it sits within your range, it is a reasonable aim but not a certainty. If it sits above your optimistic end, it is a stretch, though still worth listing if you genuinely want it. Remember to add any adjustment factors you are eligible for, because universities use your selection rank, not your ATAR. Our selection rank calculator can help.

An example
A student whose cautious, realistic and optimistic estimates are 78, 82 and 85 might put a course with a recent threshold of 88 first, two courses with thresholds around 80 to 84 next, and at least one course with a threshold below 78 further down. That list works whichever end of the range the official ATAR lands at.

Accuracy versus precision

Two words that are often used interchangeably mean quite different things when it comes to ATAR estimates. Understanding the difference protects you from being misled by a confident-looking number.

Precision is about detail

A precise figure has lots of detail. An estimate of 87.35 is more precise than an estimate of “around 87”, because it specifies the result to two decimal places. But precision says nothing about whether the figure is right.

Accuracy is about closeness to the truth

An accurate estimate is close to the official result. An estimate of “between 85 and 89” is less precise than 87.35, but if your official ATAR turns out to be 86.10, the range was accurate, while the precise figure was not.

Why calculators can look more precise than they are

Because the ATAR itself is reported to two decimal places, it is natural for calculators to show estimates in the same format. But before results are released, the second decimal place, and often the first, carry little real information. A calculator that shows a range alongside its central figure is giving you a more accurate picture of what it actually knows.

What this means for you

When you read an estimate, mentally round it and attach a range. Instead of “my ATAR will be 87.35”, think “my ATAR is likely to be somewhere in the mid-to-high 80s”. That small shift in thinking leads to much better decisions about preferences and targets.

What affects your estimate’s accuracy

A few things change how close your estimate lands. The accuracy of the marks you enter matters most: garbage in, garbage out. Predicted marks give a rougher estimate than real ones.

Year-to-year shifts in scaling and in the strength of the cohort also nudge the result. And unusual subject combinations, or subjects with small cohorts, are harder to estimate precisely. None of this makes a calculator useless — it just means the number is a guide.

Your marks: garbage in, garbage out

The phrase “garbage in, garbage out” is blunt, but it captures the most important point about ATAR calculators. A calculator does exactly what it is told with the marks you give it. If those marks are realistic, the estimate can be useful. If they are wishful, the estimate will be too.

Realistic marks usually come from three places: your actual school assessment results so far, your performance in trial or practice exams, and your teachers’ feedback on where you are likely to finish. Combining all three is more reliable than relying on any one of them.

Exams carry real weight

In most states, final exams contribute a large share of each subject’s result. In NSW, the exam mark makes up half of each HSC mark. In Western Australia, the exam mark makes up half of each combined score. In South Australia and the NT, external assessment is 30 per cent of each Stage 2 subject. In Queensland, external assessment is 25 per cent of most General subjects and 50 per cent of mathematics and science subjects. In Victoria, exams are a major part of each study score. Because exam results are not known until results day, every estimate before then carries exam uncertainty.

Small cohorts and unusual subjects

Subjects taken by only a small number of students can scale less predictably from year to year, because a few students with unusually strong or weak results can shift the group’s overall pattern. Some subjects are also offered only in particular schools or regions. If several of your subjects have small cohorts, allow a wider range around your estimate.

Input errors

It sounds obvious, but many surprising estimates come from simple input mistakes: choosing the wrong course from a list, entering a 1-unit course as 2 units, typing a mark in the wrong field, or forgetting to include a subject. Before you rely on an estimate, read your entries back carefully.

From trial marks to final ATAR: why estimates move

Many students run their most important estimate after their trial exams. It is a natural moment: trial results are the closest thing to final exam results that students have before the real thing. But trial results and final results are not the same, and it is worth understanding why estimates often move between the two.

Trials are school-based

Trial exams are usually set and marked by your school. They may be harder or easier than the final exam, and they are marked against the standards your teachers apply. A trial mark of 80 in one school may not mean the same as a trial mark of 80 in another. Your rank in the trial within your school is often more informative than the mark itself, because moderation in most states preserves the order of students within a school group.

The final exam compares you with the whole state

Your final exam is sat by every student in the subject across the state. Your result depends not only on how well you do but also on how the paper is marked and how your performance compares with everyone else’s. Some students perform better in the final exam than in the trial, often because the extra weeks of revision pay off. Others perform less well, sometimes because of nerves or because the final paper suits them less.

School marks are moderated

If you include school assessment marks in your estimate, remember that they will usually be moderated. Your position within your school group is likely to be preserved, but the size of your mark may change depending on how your group performs in the exam.

Scaling is recalculated

Even if your final results matched your trial results exactly, your ATAR could still differ from your estimate because scaling and the conversion from aggregate to ATAR are recalculated for your year.

Our guide to estimating your ATAR from trial results and our mock exam ATAR guide explain how to use trial marks as sensibly as possible.

A useful habit
When you run an estimate from trial marks, note the date and the marks you used. When final results arrive, compare them subject by subject with your trial inputs. That comparison will tell you far more about why your official ATAR differs from your estimate than looking at the ATARs alone.

How much scaling moves from year to year

A calculator built before results are released must use scaling from a past year. How much that matters depends on how much scaling changes between years.

Most large subjects are fairly stable

For subjects taken by large numbers of students, the kinds of students who choose them tend to be similar from year to year, so their scaling usually changes only modestly. This is the main reason calculators based on recent data can give useful estimates.

Some subjects move more

Subjects with small cohorts, subjects whose cohort changes significantly, and new or revised courses can move more. A change in syllabus, a change in which students are encouraged to take a subject, or a change in a related subject can all shift its scaling. When a subject’s scaling moves noticeably, estimates for students taking it will be less precise.

System changes can reset the pattern

Occasionally, a state changes its rules or its curriculum in a way that affects scaling more broadly. Queensland’s move from the OP to the ATAR for students completing Year 12 from 2020 is one example; Tasmania’s change to the TE Score rules from 2025 is another. After a change like this, older data is less useful, and calculators need a few years of new data before their estimates settle.

Our guide to ATAR scaling changes explains the patterns in more detail, and our guide to reading a scaling report shows you how to check the data for your own subjects.

How different kinds of subject affect estimates

Some kinds of subject are easier for a calculator to handle than others. If your combination includes any of the following, it is worth understanding how they affect your estimate.

Extension and advanced courses

Extension and advanced courses often attract strong cohorts, and their results can contribute well to an aggregate. In NSW, 1-unit extension courses are reported out of 50 and count as single units in your best 10. Calculators must handle them correctly, including UAC’s rules on how related courses, such as the different levels of mathematics, can be counted together. Check that your calculator treats your extension courses as the rules require.

Languages

Languages often have small cohorts, which can make their scaling less predictable. Some states also treat languages in particular ways: VTAC applies a policy adjustment to the scaling of languages in Victoria, and in Western Australia 10 per cent of your best scaled score in a language other than English is added to your TEA as a bonus. A good calculator must model these features to give a sensible estimate.

Subjects with practical components

Subjects that include performances, folios, practical exams or major works, such as music, drama, visual arts and design subjects, can be harder to estimate, because practical results are often less predictable than written exam results. If you take one or more of these subjects, use your teachers’ feedback on your practical work and allow a slightly wider range.

Applied and vocational subjects

In Queensland, Applied subjects have no external assessment, so their results are based entirely on internal assessment, which can make them more predictable. One Applied subject can contribute to an ATAR when combined with four General subjects. VET qualifications and other recognised studies can contribute in several states within limits. Not every calculator models these, so check before relying on an estimate that includes them.

Subjects completed in an earlier year

If you completed a subject a year early, check whether your calculator lets you include it and whether your state’s rules allow it to count. In Tasmania, courses completed over two years can contribute to the TE Score as a matter of course.

How to use an ATAR estimate well

Use it to plan, not to panic or celebrate. An estimate tells you roughly where you stand, which helps you set a realistic target and see which subjects move your rank most.

Re-run it as your real marks come in, and it gets more accurate. Compare it with the cut-off for your course, aim a little above, and let it guide your effort for the rest of the year.

A simple routine for using estimates

An estimate is most useful when you use it regularly and consistently, rather than once in a moment of anxiety. Here is a routine that many students find helpful.

  1. Set up your subjects once. Enter your subjects carefully in a calculator built for your state, and check that the unit counts, course names and any special rules are correct.
  2. Run three scenarios. Enter cautious, realistic and optimistic marks for each subject. Write down all three estimates.
  3. Compare with your goals. Look at the recent thresholds of the courses you are interested in, adding any adjustment factors you are likely to be eligible for. Note where each course sits relative to your range.
  4. Identify your levers. Change one subject’s mark at a time to see which subjects move your estimate most. Those are the subjects where extra effort is likely to pay off.
  5. Repeat after each assessment period. Update your marks after major assessments and trial exams, and record how your range changes.
  6. Use the range for preferences. When you build your preference list, make sure it has good options across your whole range.

Finding the subjects that move your ATAR most

Not every subject has the same effect on your ATAR. A subject that is already among your best results, and that scales well at your level, may have a larger effect than a subject that only just makes it into your best results. A subject that is not counted at all may have no direct effect.

A calculator lets you test this. Raise one subject’s mark by a few points and see how much your estimate changes. Then do the same for each of your other subjects. The results can be surprising. Some students discover that a subject they have been neglecting is one of their biggest levers, while a subject they have been pouring time into makes less difference than expected.

Be careful not to take this too far. Every subject you take may end up counting, depending on how your results turn out, and a strong effort in all your subjects protects you if one goes less well than expected. Use the exercise to set priorities, not to abandon subjects.

Setting a target with a buffer

Once you know the threshold for a course you want, it is sensible to aim a little above it rather than exactly at it. Thresholds can move from year to year, and your estimate has its own uncertainty. A buffer of a few points gives you room for both. Our target ATAR calculator and our guide to target ATAR strategy can help you set a realistic goal.

What not to do with an estimate

  • Do not treat it as a promise and remove safe options from your preference list.
  • Do not compare your estimate with friends’ estimates as though they were official results.
  • Do not let a disappointing estimate convince you that your effort no longer matters. Estimates change as results come in.
  • Do not let an encouraging estimate make you complacent. The final exams still carry substantial weight.

When to run an estimate during Year 11 and 12

The value of an estimate changes through the senior years. Early on, it is a rough guide to the kind of ATAR your current results might lead to. Later, it becomes a practical tool for planning preferences. Here is how to use estimates at each stage.

Year 10 and early Year 11: exploring

Before you have senior results, an ATAR estimate is very rough. It can still be useful for exploring how different subject combinations might work, but the inputs are largely guesses. Use it to understand your state’s rules and the role of scaling, not to predict your result. Our guide to predicting your ATAR in Year 11 explains what an early estimate can and cannot tell you.

Late Year 11: first real data

By the end of Year 11, you have real results in senior subjects. In Tasmania, some of these results may count directly towards your TE Score. In other states, Year 11 results are a guide to how you are likely to perform in Year 12. An estimate at this stage gives a first sense of your likely range, though it is still wide.

Year 12, Term 1 and Term 2: building the picture

As final-year assessments come in, your estimate becomes more grounded. Update it after each major assessment period. This is a good time to identify the subjects that move your estimate most and to adjust your study priorities.

Year 12, Term 3: after trials

After trial or practice exams, many students have their most useful estimate before results day. Use it to finalise a draft preference list, with options across your range. Remember that final results can differ from trial results.

Term 4: before and after exams

Just before exams, an estimate is mainly useful for planning preferences. After exams, some students run an estimate based on how they feel the exams went. That can help with planning, but it is subject to the same limits as any estimate, and it is often worth waiting for official results before making major decisions.

Results day: switch to official numbers

Once your official ATAR arrives, the estimate has done its job. Use your official ATAR, together with any adjustment factors, to finalise your preferences. If you want to understand any difference between your estimate and your official result, compare your inputs with your final subject results.

Getting the most accurate estimate in your state

The general advice to enter realistic marks applies everywhere, but each state reports results differently, and the best inputs for a calculator differ accordingly. Here are practical tips for each system.

NSW: think in HSC marks and units

In NSW, each HSC course result is reported as an HSC mark, which combines your moderated assessment mark and your exam mark in equal parts. Most courses are 2 units and reported out of 100, while 1-unit extension courses are reported out of 50. When you enter marks into a NSW calculator, enter your best estimate of each HSC mark, not just your raw school assessment marks.

A practical approach is to estimate your assessment mark and your exam mark separately, then average them. For your assessment mark, use your current school assessment results, remembering that moderation may move them. For your exam mark, use your trial results and teacher feedback, adjusted for the fact that the HSC exam compares you with the whole state. Make sure your calculator includes your 2 units of English and handles extension courses correctly. Our HSC ATAR calculator and our guide to converting HSC marks to an ATAR explain the details.

Victoria: think in study scores

In Victoria, the input that matters is your study score in each study, which ranges from 0 to 50. The VCAA sets the mean study score in each study at 30, with a standard deviation of about 7, so roughly two-thirds of students in a study receive a score between about 23 and 37. A study score reflects your position in the study across the state, combining your school-assessed coursework and your exams.

To estimate a study score, think about where you are likely to rank in each study across the state, not just in your class. Your rank within your school, your SAC results and your practice exam results all help. If you are near the top of a strong school group, you may be heading for a high study score; if you are in the middle of a group, you may be closer to 30. Our VCE ATAR calculator and our guide to converting VCE results to an ATAR explain how to enter study scores.

Queensland: internal and external assessment

In Queensland, each General subject result is out of 100. Internal assessment contributes 75 marks in most subjects and 50 marks in mathematics and science subjects, with external assessment making up the rest. Because you often know much of your internal assessment result before the external assessment, you can enter that part with some confidence and focus your uncertainty on the external assessment.

A practical approach is to add your known or expected internal marks to a cautious, realistic and optimistic estimate of your external assessment mark. Remember that your ATAR uses your best five scaled results, which can include one Applied subject or an eligible VET qualification. Our QCE ATAR calculator and our guide to converting QCE results to an ATAR explain how.

Western Australia: school mark and exam mark

In Western Australia, each ATAR course produces a combined score from your moderated school mark and your exam mark in equal parts. When you estimate, think about both parts. Your school mark is known by the end of the year, but it will be moderated; your exam mark is the main source of uncertainty.

Make sure your calculator applies the bonuses correctly: 10 per cent of your best scaled score in a language other than English, 10 per cent of your scaled score in Mathematics Methods and 10 per cent of your scaled score in Mathematics Specialist, all added to your best four scaled scores. These bonuses apply even if those courses are not among your best four. Our WACE ATAR calculator and our guide to converting WACE results to an ATAR cover the details.

South Australia and the NT: grades and the 90-credit structure

In South Australia and the NT, Stage 2 subjects are reported as grades from A+ to E-, and each subject combines school assessment, worth 70 per cent, with external assessment, worth 30 per cent. Your ATAR is based on scaled scores from your best 90 credits, made up of three 20-credit subjects and a flexible option of 30 credits.

Because SATAC does not publish scaled scores for each subject, calculators for South Australia and the NT necessarily approximate the scaling step. Enter your expected grades carefully, check that the calculator applies the 90-credit structure, and treat the result as an approximate range. Our SACE ATAR calculator and our guide to converting SACE results to an ATAR explain the structure.

Tasmania: courses over two years

In Tasmania, your TE Score is built from your best eligible Level 3 and Level 4 courses completed over up to two years, with each full course worth up to 15 points. Because some of your courses may already be complete, you may be able to enter part of your result with certainty. Check that your calculator uses the rules that apply from 2025. Our TCE ATAR calculator and our guide to converting TCE results to an ATAR explain the current rules.

ACT: majors, minors and scaled scores

In the ACT, your ATAR is based on your best three major scaled scores and 60 per cent of your next major or minor. Scaled scores are produced through the ACT’s own process for comparing results across colleges and courses, which is harder for an outside calculator to replicate. Enter your results carefully and treat your estimate as approximate. Our ACT ATAR calculator and our guide to how the ACT ATAR is calculated explain the system.

Step-by-step: how an estimate is built in four states

Seeing an estimate built step by step makes it much easier to understand what a calculator is doing and where its uncertainty comes from. The examples below use illustrative figures to show the structure of each state’s calculation. They are not predictions, and the scaled figures are invented for the purpose of illustration, except where a published worked example is cited.

Victoria: from study scores to an aggregate

Imagine a Victorian student, Sophie, with the following scaled study scores: English 32, Mathematical Methods 38, Chemistry 36, Legal Studies 31, Psychology 30 and Health and Human Development 27.

  1. Primary four. Sophie’s English study must be included, so her primary four are English (32) and her next three best scaled studies: Methods (38), Chemistry (36) and Legal Studies (31). Together they total 137.
  2. Increments. Her fifth and sixth studies, Psychology (30) and Health and Human Development (27), each contribute 10 per cent: 3.0 and 2.7.
  3. Aggregate. Sophie’s aggregate is 137 + 3.0 + 2.7 = 142.7.
  4. Conversion. The calculator then compares 142.7 with recent conversion data to estimate her ATAR.

The uncertainty in Sophie’s estimate comes mainly from her study scores, which she has estimated before results, and from the scaling and conversion for her year.

Western Australia: a published worked example

A worked example of a WA TEA calculation, published to explain the process, shows how the pieces fit together. In it, a student’s best four scaled scores are Physics 67.6, Italian 66.5, English 55.4 and Modern History 41.5, giving a subtotal of 231.0. The student then receives a bonus of 10 per cent of their Italian scaled score, 6.7, for a language other than English, and a bonus of 4.1 for mathematics, giving a TEA of 241.8. The example shows that bonuses can apply even when a subject, such as mathematics in this case, is not among the best four.

A WA calculator follows the same steps with your estimated scaled scores, then converts the TEA to an ATAR using recent data. If the calculator forgets the bonuses or applies them incorrectly, the estimate will be wrong even if every scaled score is right.

Queensland: the best five

Imagine a Queensland student, Liam, with six General subject results. A Queensland calculator scales each result using recent QTAC data, then adds Liam’s best five scaled results to form his TEA. His sixth result is not counted, though it would replace a weaker result if one of his other subjects fell. The calculator also checks that Liam has met the English requirement. Finally, it converts the TEA to an estimated ATAR.

Because Liam already knows much of his internal assessment, his main uncertainty is the external assessment, which contributes 25 per cent of most of his subjects and 50 per cent of his mathematics and science subjects, together with the year’s scaling.

NSW: units and English

Imagine a NSW student, Zara, who studies English Advanced, Mathematics Advanced, Mathematics Extension 1, Biology, Economics and Modern History. A NSW calculator converts each HSC mark to a scaled mark, then builds her aggregate from her 2 units of English and her best 8 other units, applying UAC’s rules on how mathematics courses and other combinations can be counted. Her aggregate, out of 500, is then converted to an estimated ATAR.

Zara’s main uncertainties are her HSC marks, each of which combines her moderated assessment mark and her exam mark equally, and the year’s scaling for her courses.

The common thread
In every state, the calculator follows the same pattern: estimate each subject’s result, scale it, combine the counted results under the state’s rules, and convert the total to a rank. The rules in the middle step are what make each state’s calculator different.

Building a rough estimate by hand

You do not need a calculator to understand your likely position. With your state’s published reports and a little patience, you can build a rough estimate yourself. Doing this once is one of the best ways to understand what calculators do.

Step 1: Find the official reports

Locate the most recent scaling report or statistics from your admissions centre. Look for tables that show how results in each subject convert to scaled results, and, if available, how aggregates convert to ATARs.

Step 2: Scale each of your results

For each subject, find the scaled result that corresponds to your expected result. If the table gives values at several points, choose the closest point or estimate between them.

Step 3: Apply your state’s counting rules

Combine your scaled results according to your state’s rules: the right number of subjects or units, compulsory English where required, increments or bonuses where they apply, and any limits on particular kinds of subjects.

Step 4: Convert to a rank

If your state publishes a conversion from aggregate to ATAR, use it to estimate your ATAR. If not, you may be able to find information on the distribution of aggregates or ATARs in the admissions centre’s reports.

Step 5: Compare with a calculator

Compare your hand estimate with a well-built calculator’s estimate. If they are close, you have a good understanding of the process. If they differ, check whether you applied the rules and scaling the same way. This exercise also helps you spot calculators that are not modelling your state’s process properly.

Our guide on how to read a scaling report shows how to find the tables you need.

Understanding one subject’s contribution

An ATAR estimate combines several subjects, which can make it hard to see what each one is doing. Looking at a single subject’s contribution helps you understand your estimate and make better decisions about where to focus.

From your result to a scaled result

Each subject result is converted to a scaled result using that subject’s scaling. In NSW, scaled marks are reported per unit, out of 50. In Victoria, study scores are scaled to produce scaled study scores. In other states, subject results are converted to scaled scores on a common scale. In each case, the same result in two different subjects can produce different scaled results, because the subjects’ cohorts differ.

The conversion is not a simple addition or subtraction. It depends on where your result sits in the subject. At the top of a subject, the scaled result may be well above or below the raw result; in the middle, the difference may be smaller or larger. That is why it is important to look at the scaling at your own level of result rather than at a single average figure.

Whether the subject counts

A subject only contributes to your aggregate if it is among the results your state’s rules count. In NSW, that means your 2 units of English and your best 8 other units. In Victoria, it means your English study, your next three best scaled studies and 10 per cent of your fifth and sixth. In Queensland, it means your best five. In Western Australia, it means your best four, plus any bonuses. A subject that is not counted has no direct effect on your aggregate, though it remains a safety net if another subject goes less well.

How much the subject moves your ATAR

Finally, the subject’s contribution to your aggregate is translated into an effect on your ATAR. As explained above, a given change in aggregate has a larger effect in the crowded middle of the range than near the top. So the same improvement in a subject can move one student’s ATAR more than another’s, depending on where each student’s aggregate sits.

Testing it with a calculator

To see a subject’s contribution for yourself, run your realistic scenario, then raise that subject’s mark by a few points and run it again. The difference between the two estimates is that subject’s marginal effect at your current position. Repeat for each subject. This exercise is one of the most useful things a calculator can do, because it turns an abstract estimate into a practical guide to where your effort will count most.

Keep it in proportion
Marginal effects change as your results change. A subject that does not count today may count tomorrow if another result falls. Use marginal effects to set priorities, not to abandon any subject.

Making your exam estimates more realistic

Because exams carry substantial weight in most states, the realism of your exam estimates often decides how useful your ATAR estimate is. Here are practical ways to make those estimates more grounded.

Use full papers under exam conditions

A mark from a practice paper done with notes, without a time limit or over several sittings is not a good guide to your exam mark. Complete at least one full past paper in each subject under timed, closed-book conditions, then mark it strictly using the official marking guidelines or have a teacher mark it.

Mark against the standard, not your hopes

When you mark your own work, it is easy to be generous. Compare your answers with the marking guidelines and sample responses, and award marks only where your answer clearly meets the criteria. If possible, ask a teacher to check a sample of your marking.

Look at your trend, not your best result

A single excellent practice result may not represent your typical performance. Look at your results across several practice papers and assessments, and use the typical level, not the best one, as your realistic estimate.

Remember that the exam compares you with the state

In many states, your exam result is not just a measure of what you know; it is also compared with every other student’s performance. A mark that would put you near the top of your class may put you in the middle of a strong state cohort. Teacher feedback about how past students’ practice results compared with their final results is especially valuable here.

Allow for exam-day variation

Even well-prepared students have better and worse days. A cautious scenario that assumes your exams go slightly less well than your practice results is a sensible part of any estimate, and it costs you nothing to plan for.

Estimate, prediction and official result

Three words get mixed up here. An estimate is what a calculator gives you now, based on last year’s scaling and the marks you enter. A prediction usually means an estimate built from marks you expect, rather than real ones, so it is rougher still. The official result is the only exact figure, and only your admissions centre can produce it.

Keeping these apart helps you judge any number you see. A confident-looking estimate is still an estimate, and no tool can turn it into the official result before results day.

More terms worth knowing

Beyond estimate, prediction and official result, several other terms come up when you use an ATAR calculator or read about one. Here is what each means in plain language.

  • Raw mark: a mark as awarded by your school or in an exam, before any moderation or scaling.
  • Moderated mark: a school assessment mark after it has been adjusted so that marks from different schools can be compared fairly.
  • Combined mark or score: in some states, the result of combining your moderated school mark with your exam mark.
  • Study score: in Victoria, a score from 0 to 50 that shows your position within a VCE study across the state.
  • Scaled mark or scaled score: a result after scaling has placed it on a common scale with results in other subjects.
  • Aggregate: the total of the scaled results that count towards your ATAR under your state’s rules. It goes by different names in different states, such as the TEA in Queensland and Western Australia, the university aggregate in South Australia and the NT, and the TE Score in Tasmania.
  • Conversion table: a table showing which aggregates correspond to which ATARs in a particular year.
  • Likely range: the span of ATARs that an estimate suggests is most plausible, given the uncertainty in the inputs and data.
  • Central estimate: the single figure in the middle of the likely range.
  • Cohort: the group of students being compared, such as everyone taking a subject in a particular year.
  • Selection rank: your ATAR plus any adjustment factors for a particular course. Universities use your selection rank, not just your ATAR, when they make offers.

The statistics behind an estimate, in plain English

You do not need to be a statistician to use an ATAR calculator well, but a few basic ideas explain a lot about why estimates behave the way they do.

Distributions

A distribution describes how results are spread across a group. If you lined up every student’s aggregate from lowest to highest, you would see that many students sit in the middle, with fewer at the very top and bottom. The ATAR is built from this kind of distribution: it tells you where your aggregate sits in the line, expressed as a rank against your age group.

Percentiles and ranks

A percentile describes the share of a group at or below a particular point. The ATAR works in a similar way: an ATAR of 80 means you performed better than about 80 per cent of your age group in your state. Because the ATAR is a rank, it depends on the whole distribution, not just your own results.

Why a crowded middle makes estimates sensitive

When many students have similar aggregates, a small change in your aggregate moves you past many of them. That is why a few marks can move your estimate noticeably in the middle of the range, and less so near the top, where students are more spread out.

Error and uncertainty

An estimate’s error is the difference between the estimate and the official result. Error comes from many small sources: each subject’s mark, moderation, each subject’s scaling, and the conversion to a rank. Some of these errors push the estimate up and some push it down. When several small errors happen to push in the same direction, the overall error can be larger than any single source would suggest.

Why ranges are more honest than single numbers

A single figure with two decimal places suggests a precision that does not exist before results are released. A range acknowledges the uncertainty. When a calculator says your likely range is, for example, 82 to 86, it is telling you that your official ATAR is more likely to land somewhere in that band than far outside it, not that it is guaranteed to.

Past data as a guide

Every estimate uses the past as a guide to the present. That works well when patterns are stable, as they usually are for large subjects in large states. It works less well when something changes, such as a new syllabus, a change in rules or a shift in which students take a subject. Knowing this helps you judge when to trust an estimate more and when to allow more room.

When estimates are least reliable

Some situations are harder to estimate than others. Very high ranks are the trickiest, because at the top a tiny mark difference moves the rank in ways no calculator can perfectly predict. Unusual subject combinations, and subjects with small cohorts, are also harder to pin down.

Predicted marks, rather than real ones, add their own uncertainty. None of this makes a calculator pointless. It just means you should treat the number as a guide with a margin, and re-run it as your real marks arrive.

Each low-reliability situation in detail

The situations above share a common feature: they make one or more steps of the estimate harder to model. Here is what is happening in each case and how to respond.

  • Very high ranks. Near the top of the range, the courses that interest you may have thresholds separated by fractions of a point. A calculator cannot resolve differences that fine before results. Plan with realistic alternatives, and look at whether additional criteria, such as interviews or admissions tests, will matter as much as the ATAR.
  • Unusual subject combinations. A combination that few students take may interact with your state’s counting rules in ways that a calculator handles less precisely. Check that the calculator applies the rules correctly to your combination, and allow a wider range.
  • Small-cohort subjects. Subjects with few students can scale less predictably. If one or more of your counted subjects has a small cohort, widen your range.
  • Predicted marks. If you are entering marks you hope to achieve, your estimate inherits all the uncertainty in those hopes. Build cautious and optimistic scenarios as well as a realistic one.
  • Early in the senior years. The less of your result that is known, the wider your range. Treat early estimates as exploration rather than prediction.

Special cases calculators find hard

Some students have circumstances that general calculators are not designed for. If any of the following apply to you, an ordinary estimate may be less reliable, and it is worth seeking advice from your school or admissions centre.

Accelerated subjects and results from earlier years

Some students complete one or more senior subjects a year early. Depending on your state’s rules, those results may be able to count towards your ATAR alongside your final-year results. In NSW, for example, UAC’s rules allow courses completed in different years to be included under certain conditions. Many calculators assume all your subjects were completed in the same year, so check how your calculator handles accelerated results, and confirm your state’s rules.

Repeating subjects or Year 12

Students who repeat a subject or the whole of Year 12 may have results from more than one year. Each state has rules about which results can count and how they are combined. A general calculator may not model these rules, so ask your admissions centre how your results will be treated.

VET and Recognised Studies

In several states, vocational qualifications or other recognised studies can contribute to the ATAR within limits. In Queensland, an eligible VET qualification at Certificate III or higher can be one of your five results. In Victoria, scored VCE VET programs can count as studies, and some unscored VET can contribute an increment. In South Australia and the NT, Recognised Studies can contribute up to 20 credits. Not every calculator models these contributions, so check before you rely on an estimate that includes them.

Interstate and international students

If you are applying to a university in another state, your ATAR from your own state is used directly, so you should estimate it using a calculator for the state where you completed Year 12. If you are studying the International Baccalaureate, your results will be converted to a rank using tables published by admissions centres, and an ordinary ATAR calculator will not apply.

Special provisions and misadventure

If illness, injury or other circumstances affect your exams and you apply for special consideration or misadventure, your result may be determined using other evidence. A calculator cannot anticipate that outcome. Talk to your school about how the process works in your state.

Distance education and non-school candidates

Students studying through distance education are ranked under the same rules as everyone else, so an ordinary calculator applies. Students who take a subject as a non-school candidate, such as a language studied outside school, should check how that subject is treated in their state, as the rules and available data may differ.

Myths about ATAR calculators

Because ATAR calculators are used by so many students, a set of myths has grown up around them. Some lead students to trust estimates too much; others lead them to dismiss estimates that could help.

“The calculator with the highest estimate is the right one”

It is tempting to believe the most encouraging estimate. But a higher estimate is not more accurate. It may come from older data, a simplified method or an input error. Judge calculators by their data and method, not by the number they give you.

“If an estimate is wrong, the calculator is broken”

An estimate that differs from your official ATAR is not necessarily a sign of a faulty calculator. Most differences come from the marks entered, moderation, scaling changes or the conversion to a rank. Compare your inputs with your final results before drawing conclusions.

“Calculators are useless because they cannot be exact”

An estimate does not need to be exact to be useful. Knowing that your ATAR is likely to be in the low 80s rather than the high 70s or high 80s is valuable for planning preferences, setting targets and deciding where to focus your effort.

“A calculator can tell me which subjects to choose”

A calculator can show how different results and subjects would combine, but it cannot tell you where you will rank in a subject you have not yet studied. Subject choice should start with your strengths, interests and the requirements of courses you are considering. Our article on common ATAR myths explains why choosing subjects mainly for scaling often backfires.

“Official bodies publish their own exact calculators”

Admissions centres publish the data and rules that calculators rely on, and some provide tools or tables to help students understand the process. But no one can calculate your exact ATAR before results are released, because the current year’s scaling and conversion do not exist until then.

“My friend’s estimate tells me what mine will be”

Two students with similar marks can have quite different estimates, because their subjects scale differently and their results combine differently under the counting rules. Compare your estimate with course thresholds, not with your friends’ estimates.

Common mistakes when using ATAR calculators

Most estimates that turn out to be far from the official result can be traced to a handful of common mistakes. Avoiding them is the easiest way to make your estimate more useful.

1. Entering hoped-for marks instead of realistic ones

Students often enter the marks they want rather than the marks they are likely to achieve. That produces an estimate that is more of a wish than a guide. Use your actual results, trial marks and teacher feedback to choose realistic inputs, and run a separate optimistic scenario if you want to see your best case.

2. Using a calculator built for a different state

Each state’s rules are different. A calculator for NSW will not give a meaningful estimate for a Victorian student, and a national tool that applies one set of rules everywhere will be wrong for most students. Always use a calculator built for the state where you are completing Year 12.

3. Entering the wrong kind of mark

Some calculators expect a final subject result, such as an HSC mark or a study score, while others expect a raw school mark. Entering the wrong kind of mark can distort your estimate. Read the calculator’s instructions and enter the type of result it asks for.

4. Getting units or credits wrong

In NSW, counting units correctly is essential, because your ATAR uses your best 10 units. In South Australia and the NT, credits matter in the same way. Entering a 1-unit course as 2 units, or a 10-credit subject as 20 credits, changes your aggregate. Check each subject’s units or credits.

5. Forgetting compulsory subjects

In NSW, your 2 units of English must count. In Victoria, an English study must be in your primary four. If a calculator lets you leave out English, or if you forget to include it, your estimate may be higher than it should be.

6. Ignoring moderation

Raw school marks are not final. If you enter raw school marks without allowing for moderation, your estimate may be too high or too low. Use exam-style marks where you can, and allow a margin.

7. Treating the central figure as exact

The central estimate is the middle of a range, not a precise prediction. Plan around the whole range, especially when you build your preference list.

8. Running the estimate only once

A single estimate early in the year quickly becomes out of date. Re-run your estimate after each major assessment period and after trial exams.

9. Using old data

Some calculators are not updated regularly. Check which year’s scaling a calculator uses, and prefer one that uses the most recent published report.

10. Comparing estimates with ATAR thresholds instead of selection ranks

Course thresholds are usually lowest selection ranks, which include adjustment factors. If you are eligible for adjustments, compare your estimated selection rank, not just your estimated ATAR, with each course’s threshold.

11. Using an estimate to drop subjects

Some students see that a subject is not currently counting in their estimate and decide to stop working on it. But your final results may differ from your current marks, and that subject could end up counting. Keep working steadily in every subject.

12. Letting an estimate decide how you feel

An estimate is information, not a verdict. A low estimate early in the year is a prompt to plan and focus; a high estimate is a reason to keep going, not to relax. Neither is a measure of your worth.

A quick test you can run

Enter a simple set of results, such as the same mark in every subject, and then change one subject at a time. A well-built calculator will respond differently to changes in different subjects, because they scale differently and contribute differently to your aggregate. If every change has exactly the same effect, the calculator may be averaging marks rather than modelling your state’s process.

You can also test the counting rules. In NSW, try changing your English mark: because English must count, it should always affect your estimate. In Western Australia, try adding or removing Mathematics Methods: the bonus should change your estimate even if Methods is not among your best four. If the calculator does not respond as the rules say it should, treat its estimates with caution.

What-if scenarios: how one change flows through

A calculator is at its most useful when you use it to ask “what if?” Here are some common scenarios and what they usually reveal. The patterns are general, and your own results will depend on your subjects, your state and your marks.

What if one exam goes badly?

Lower one subject’s mark to reflect a disappointing exam and run the estimate again. If the subject is one of your counted results, your estimate will fall; if you have a spare result that can take its place, the fall may be smaller than you fear. This scenario shows the value of keeping every subject strong and, where your state’s rules allow, carrying a buffer subject.

What if I improve my weakest counted subject?

Raise the mark in the weakest subject that still counts towards your aggregate. Because that subject is contributing fully, an improvement there often has a direct effect on your estimate. For many students, this is the most efficient place to focus extra effort.

What if I improve a subject that does not currently count?

Raise the mark in a subject that is currently outside your counted results. Until it rises above another counted result, it will have little or no effect. But if it rises enough to replace a weaker subject, the effect can be noticeable. This scenario shows why subjects outside your best results still matter as protection.

What if scaling changes?

Some calculators let you see estimates based on different years’ scaling. If yours does, compare them. The difference gives you a sense of how much your estimate depends on scaling changes. If it does not, widen your range a little to allow for them.

What if I include an adjustment factor?

Add the adjustment factors you are likely to receive for a particular course to see your estimated selection rank. This often changes which courses look realistic and can help you decide which adjustments are worth applying for.

What if I take an extra subject?

If you are considering an extra subject, add it with a realistic mark and see whether it changes your estimate. If it only counts when another subject goes badly, it is acting as a buffer. Weigh that protection against the time it will take from your other subjects.

Which decisions an estimate should and should not drive

An ATAR estimate is a useful tool for some decisions and a poor basis for others. Being clear about the difference helps you get the benefit without the risks.

Good uses of an estimate

  • Building a preference list with options across your likely range.
  • Setting a target with a sensible buffer above the threshold of the course you want.
  • Choosing study priorities by identifying which subjects move your estimate most.
  • Planning adjustments by seeing which adjustment factors could bring a course within reach and applying for them on time.
  • Researching pathways for courses that sit above your likely range.

Poor uses of an estimate

  • Dropping a subject because it is not currently counting. Your results may change, and that subject could become important.
  • Removing safe options from your preference list because your estimate looks comfortable.
  • Giving up on a goal because an early estimate is below it. Estimates move as results come in, and pathways exist for most courses.
  • Choosing subjects mainly because of how a calculator shows them scaling. Your result in a subject matters more than its scaling.
  • Comparing yourself with others. Estimates are tools for your own planning, not a ranking among friends.

From estimated ATAR to estimated selection rank

Universities use your selection rank, not just your ATAR, when they make offers. Your selection rank is your ATAR plus any adjustment factors you are eligible for, for a particular course at a particular institution. If you want to use an estimate to plan your preferences, you need to estimate your selection rank too.

Step 1: List the adjustments you may be eligible for

Adjustment factors fall into a few broad groups: subject adjustments for strong results in particular subjects, regional or location-based adjustments, equity adjustments for students who have experienced educational disadvantage, elite athlete and performer schemes, and school recommendation schemes. Make a list of the schemes at each university you are interested in, and note which you are likely to be eligible for. Our guide to ATAR bonus points in Australia explains the main categories.

Step 2: Check which courses accept which adjustments

Not every adjustment applies to every course, and institutions usually cap the total adjustment a student can receive. Check the rules for each course on your list. Our guide to bonus points by university is a useful starting point.

Step 3: Add the adjustments to your range

Add the adjustments you expect for each course to each end of your estimated ATAR range. That gives you an estimated selection rank range for that course. Compare it with the course’s recent lowest selection rank.

Step 4: Remember which adjustments require an application

Some adjustments are applied automatically; others, especially equity schemes, require an application with its own deadline. An estimated selection rank that assumes an adjustment you have not applied for will be too high. Our selection rank calculator and our guide to selection rank versus ATAR explain how adjustments work.

Comparing an estimate with course thresholds

An estimate is most useful when you compare it with the courses you are interested in. That comparison is easy to get wrong, because course thresholds are published in several different forms. Here is how to read them alongside your estimate.

Lowest selection rank

The figure most often called a cut-off is the lowest selection rank to which an offer was made for a course in a particular round or intake. It includes adjustment factors, so some students who received offers had ATARs below it. Compare it with your estimated selection rank, not just your estimated ATAR.

ATAR profiles

Many universities publish ATAR or selection rank profiles for their courses, showing the lowest, median and highest ranks of students who received offers. The median is the middle of that group, so half the students offered a place had a lower rank. Treating the median as a minimum can lead you to remove courses you could have been offered.

Guaranteed entry ranks

Some institutions publish a guaranteed entry rank for particular courses: if your selection rank is at or above it and you meet every other requirement, you are guaranteed an offer. If a course you want has one, compare it with the cautious end of your range to judge how safe the course is.

Thresholds move

Thresholds are the result of demand and the number of places in a particular year. They can rise or fall from one year to the next, sometimes by more than a point. When you compare your estimate with a threshold, remember that both numbers carry uncertainty.

Putting it together

For each course, compare your estimated selection rank range with the course’s most recent lowest selection rank and, where available, its profile. Mark each course as aspirational, realistic or safe. That simple classification, based on ranges rather than single numbers, makes building a balanced preference list much easier. Our guide to selection rank cut-offs explains the figures in more detail.

Using estimates to build your preference list

One of the most practical uses of an ATAR estimate is to help you build a sensible preference list before results day. Each admissions centre allows a different number of preferences: UAC allows five, QTAC and TISC allow six, and VTAC allows eight. That makes it important to use each place well.

Order by what you want, not by what you expect

Your preferences should be listed in the order you genuinely want them. You are offered the highest preference you are eligible for, so putting a course you want less above one you want more can cost you the better option. Use your estimate to decide which courses to include, not to reorder them.

Cover your whole range

A strong preference list usually includes at least one aspirational course near or above the top of your estimated range, several realistic courses within it, and at least one safe course below the cautious end. That way, wherever your official ATAR lands, you have an option you are happy with.

Check everything besides the rank

Before you finalise your list, check each course’s prerequisites, assumed knowledge, additional criteria and deadlines. An estimate tells you nothing about whether you meet a prerequisite or have registered for an interview.

Review after results

Once your official ATAR arrives, review your list. Most admissions centres allow you to change your preferences after results are released, up to each round’s deadline. If your ATAR lands at either end of your estimated range, you may want to adjust which courses are on your list. Our 2026 ATAR release dates guide lists the key dates.

Using estimates when choosing subjects or course levels

Students sometimes use a calculator to decide whether to stay in a demanding course or move to a less demanding one, such as a higher or lower level of mathematics or English. A calculator can help with that decision, but only if you use it carefully.

What a calculator can show

A calculator can show how different results in each option would contribute to your estimate. You can enter your likely result in the more demanding course and your likely result in the less demanding one, and compare the two estimates.

What it cannot show

A calculator cannot tell you what your result in each option would be. That is the crucial input, and it depends on where you would rank among the students taking each course. Your teachers are usually the best guide here, because they know how students with your current results have performed in each option in the past.

Other things to weigh

Before you change courses, check whether either option is a prerequisite or assumed knowledge for courses you are interested in, how much of the content and assessment you would need to catch up on, and how the change would affect your workload in other subjects. A modest gain in an estimate is not worth losing access to a course you want.

A sensible approach

Ask your teachers for a realistic estimate of your result in each option. Enter both into a calculator and compare the estimates as ranges. Then weigh the difference against prerequisites, workload and your interest in each option. If the estimates are similar, choose the option that better supports your goals and that you are more likely to stay motivated in.

What happens between your last exam and your ATAR

Many students wonder why their ATAR takes weeks to arrive after their final exam, and why a calculator cannot simply do the same job sooner. The answer lies in the steps that happen behind the scenes, each of which depends on the results of every student in the state.

Marking

Final exams are marked by trained markers following detailed guidelines, with checks to make sure marking is consistent. In large states, tens of thousands of scripts in dozens of subjects must be marked, checked and finalised.

Combining school and exam results

Once exam marks are finalised, school assessment marks are moderated or confirmed according to each state’s process, and combined with exam results to produce each subject result.

Scaling

With every subject result available, admissions centres calculate scaling for the year. This requires the results of all students across all subjects, because scaling compares how each subject’s students perform in their other subjects.

Aggregates and ranks

Each ATAR-eligible student’s aggregate is built under the state’s rules, and the aggregates are ranked to produce ATARs. The conversion from aggregate to ATAR is calculated for the current year’s age group.

Release

Finally, results are checked and released to students on results day, along with the information universities need to make offers.

A calculator can only imitate these steps using past data, because the current year’s version of each step requires every student’s final results. That is the fundamental reason an estimate can be close but cannot be exact. Our 2026 release dates guide shows when each state’s results arrive.

A results-week plan for using your estimate

The days around results day are when your estimate becomes most practical, and also when it is most tempting to rely on it too heavily. A simple plan helps you make the most of it.

Before results

  • Run a final estimate with your most realistic marks, plus cautious and optimistic versions.
  • Write down your range and your estimated selection rank for each course on your list.
  • Check that your preference list covers your whole range, with your genuine first choice at the top.
  • Note the change of preference deadline for the first round that will use ATARs in your state.
  • Check whether any adjustment schemes you are relying on require an application, and whether you have applied.

On results day

  • Use your official ATAR, not your estimate, from the moment it arrives.
  • Add the adjustments you are eligible for to calculate your selection rank for each course.
  • Compare those selection ranks with each course’s recent lowest selection rank.
  • Do not rush. You usually have time before the next change of preference deadline.

In the days after

  • Adjust your preferences if your ATAR landed at either end of your estimated range.
  • Talk to your careers adviser or the universities you are interested in if you have questions.
  • If you believe a subject result is wrong, contact your school or curriculum authority promptly.
  • Compare your final subject results with the marks you entered, so you understand any gap between your estimate and your ATAR.

If your official ATAR differs from your estimate

When your official ATAR arrives, it may be close to your estimate or noticeably different. Either way, the official result is the one that counts. Here is how to understand the difference and what to do next.

Start with your subject results

Compare your final subject results with the marks you entered into the calculator. In most cases, the difference between your estimate and your ATAR can be traced to differences in your subject results. If your exams went better or worse than you expected, that will show up here.

Then consider scaling and conversion

If your subject results were close to what you entered, the difference may come from changes in scaling or in the conversion from aggregate to ATAR. Once the current year’s scaling report is published, you can compare it with the data your calculator used.

If you think a result is wrong

Errors in the ATAR calculation itself are very rare. If you believe one of your subject results is wrong, such as a missing subject or an exam mark that does not match your expectations, contact your school or curriculum authority about that result. Each state has its own process and deadlines for checking results. Your ATAR is recalculated only if an underlying result changes.

Adjust your plans calmly

If your ATAR is below your estimate, look at your preference list, your likely selection ranks and the pathways available for the courses you want. If it is above your estimate, check whether there are courses you left off your list that are now within reach. In both cases, use the change of preference period well. Our guide to what counts as a good ATAR may help you put your result in context.

Why the official ATAR can be trusted

Because so much of this guide is about the uncertainty in estimates, it is worth being clear about the official result. Your official ATAR is not an estimate. It is calculated by your admissions centre using your final results, the current year’s scaling and the current year’s age group, under published rules that apply equally to every student in the state.

The process involves careful marking, moderation or confirmation of school assessment, statistical scaling and extensive checking before results are released. Errors in the ATAR calculation itself are very rare. If an underlying subject result is corrected through your state’s review process, your ATAR is recalculated.

That is why, once your official ATAR arrives, it should replace every estimate you have made. Estimates are tools for planning before results; the official ATAR is the number universities use.

What teachers and schools can tell you that calculators cannot

A calculator is only one source of information. Your teachers and your school often know things that no calculator can model, and combining both gives you a much better picture.

Where you are likely to finish in each subject

Your teachers see your work every week and know how students with similar results have performed in previous years. They can often give you a realistic sense of where you are likely to finish within a subject, which is exactly the input a calculator needs.

How your school’s results usually compare with the state

Schools that have tracked their students’ results over several years often know how their school assessment marks usually compare with final results. That knowledge can help you judge how moderation might affect your marks.

Which subjects need the most attention

Teachers can help you identify specific skills or topics that, if improved, would lift your result. A calculator can show which subjects move your estimate most; a teacher can show you how to move them.

Course requirements and pathways

Careers advisers know the prerequisites, adjustment schemes and pathways relevant to the courses you are considering, and they can help you build a preference list that works across your estimated range.

For parents and carers: reading an estimate together

Parents and carers often see their child’s ATAR estimate, and it can be hard to know how to respond. A few principles can help keep the conversation useful.

  • Treat it as a range. An estimate is not a promise. Talk about the range, not the single figure.
  • Ask about the inputs. Ask which marks were entered and whether they are realistic. That conversation is often more useful than discussing the number.
  • Focus on what can change. Talk about which subjects or skills could move the result, and what support might help.
  • Look at options across the range. Help your child explore courses and pathways that fit the whole range, including options below the estimate.
  • Keep perspective. The ATAR is one step in a long journey, and there are many routes to university and to a fulfilling career.

If your child is anxious about their estimate, encourage them to talk with their teachers or school counsellor. A calm, informed conversation about the estimate is far more helpful than pressure about a particular number.

Estimates and early offer schemes

Many universities make offers before ATARs are released, through early offer programs and school recommendation schemes. These schemes often consider Year 11 results, school assessments, personal statements or other criteria rather than the ATAR alone. If you are applying through one, an ATAR estimate can still be useful, but in a different way.

How early offers relate to estimates

Early offer schemes do not use your estimated ATAR directly. They use the criteria each scheme sets out, such as your Year 11 results in relevant subjects. However, some early offers are conditional, for example on achieving a minimum ATAR or meeting a prerequisite. In those cases, an estimate helps you judge how comfortably you are likely to meet the condition.

Keep working after an early offer

An early offer can be a great relief, but it is worth continuing to work steadily. Conditions may apply, scholarships may depend on your final results, and your ATAR may matter if you change your mind about your course. A strong finish also keeps more options open.

Check the details

Each scheme has its own eligibility criteria, application process and deadlines. Read the details carefully, and ask your careers adviser which schemes are relevant to you.

Forums, AI tools and other informal estimates

Students often look for estimates beyond calculators: in online forums, in group chats and, increasingly, from general-purpose AI chatbots. These sources can be helpful for discussion, but they have particular limitations.

Forums and group chats

Forums and group chats often include students’ own results and experiences, which can be useful for a sense of what is possible. But individual stories are not representative, and figures are often remembered imperfectly or based on older years. Use them for ideas and support, not as a source of data.

General AI chatbots

A general-purpose AI chatbot may produce an ATAR estimate if you ask for one, but unless it is working from current official scaling data and your state’s exact rules, its estimate may be based on general patterns or outdated information. It may also present a confident-looking number without showing how it was calculated. If you use one, ask it to explain its method and sources, and check any figures against your admissions centre’s published reports.

Older siblings and friends

People who have recently completed Year 12 can offer valuable perspective, but their experience reflects their own subjects, school and year. The system and the scaling may have changed since then.

The best combination

The most reliable picture usually comes from combining a well-built, state-specific calculator, your teachers’ feedback and current official information from your admissions centre. Informal sources can add perspective but should not replace these.

What estimates usually get right

Most of this guide is about the limits of ATAR calculators, so it is worth ending with what they generally do well. When used sensibly, a good estimate is reliable on several important points.

  • Your broad position. A good estimate usually tells you whether you are heading for, say, the 60s, 70s, 80s or 90s, even if it cannot tell you the exact figure.
  • Which subjects matter most. The relative effect of each subject on your aggregate is usually clear, even if the absolute figures shift a little.
  • The direction of change. When your results improve, your estimate rises; when they fall, it falls. Tracking an estimate over time shows whether you are moving in the right direction.
  • The realism of your goals. Comparing your range with course thresholds tells you which courses are realistic, which are a stretch and which may require a pathway.
  • How your state’s rules affect you. Running an estimate helps you understand how your subjects combine under your state’s rules, which is useful knowledge in itself.

  1. I am using a calculator built for my state.
  2. The calculator names its data source and year.
  3. I have entered the type of mark the calculator asks for.
  4. My subjects, units or credits are entered correctly.
  5. Compulsory subjects, such as English, are included where required.
  6. My marks are realistic, based on real results, practice exams and teacher feedback.
  7. I have run cautious, realistic and optimistic scenarios.
  8. I am planning around the range, not the central figure.
  9. I have estimated my selection rank for each course, including adjustments I am likely to receive.
  10. I will re-run my estimate after my next major assessment.

Questions to ask about any estimate

Whenever you see an ATAR estimate, whether it comes from a calculator, a teacher, a friend or a website, these questions will help you judge how much weight to give it.

  1. Which state’s rules does it use?
  2. Which year’s data is it based on?
  3. What marks were entered, and how realistic are they?
  4. Does it scale each subject at the right level of result?
  5. Does it include compulsory subjects and apply bonuses and limits correctly?
  6. Does it convert the aggregate to a rank using published data?
  7. Does it show a range, or only a single number?
  8. How would it change if one exam went better or worse than expected?
  9. Is it an ATAR estimate or a selection rank estimate?
  10. When will I next update it?

An estimate that stands up to these questions is a genuinely useful planning tool. One that does not should be treated with caution, however encouraging the number looks.

If you will not receive an ATAR

Not every Year 12 student receives an ATAR. Some choose subject combinations that do not meet the ATAR requirements, often because they are heading into an apprenticeship, TAFE or work. Nationally, a December 2025 report from the Mitchell Institute at Victoria University estimated that around 64 per cent of Year 12 students receive an ATAR.

If you will not receive an ATAR, an ATAR calculator is not relevant to you, but university is still possible. Many universities offer pathways that do not rely on an ATAR, including diplomas, enabling programs, TAFE qualifications, portfolio-based entry and special admission schemes. If you are interested in university study, talk to your careers adviser about which of these routes suit your interests and plans.

Keeping estimates in perspective

For many students, checking an ATAR estimate can become a source of stress. A number appears on the screen, and it can feel like a judgement. It is worth stepping back from that feeling.

An estimate is a planning tool

An estimate exists to help you plan: to set a target, focus your effort and build a sensible preference list. It is not a verdict on your ability, your effort or your future. A disappointing estimate is information you can act on; an encouraging one is a reason to keep going.

Check it at sensible intervals

Running an estimate after each major assessment period is useful. Running it every day is not. Frequent checking rarely changes what you should do and can increase anxiety. Set a regular time to update it, and focus on your study in between.

Remember the bigger picture

The ATAR is one step in a long journey. There are many routes into university, including pathways, transfers and later study, and many fulfilling careers that do not depend on a particular ATAR. If worry about your estimate is affecting your sleep, mood or study, talk to someone you trust, such as a parent, teacher or school counsellor.

Ten things to remember about calculator accuracy

  1. An estimate is not your ATAR. Only your admissions centre can produce your official ATAR, and only once every result is in.
  2. Your inputs matter most. Realistic marks, based on real results, practice exams and teacher feedback, make the biggest difference to accuracy.
  3. Use a calculator built for your state. Each state’s rules are different, and a calculator must model them correctly.
  4. Check the data year. The most recent official scaling report gives the best guide to your year.
  5. Scaling and conversion change every year. No calculator can know this year’s figures in advance.
  6. Moderation can move school marks. Treat raw school marks as approximate.
  7. The middle of the range is sensitive. A few marks can move your ATAR by a point or more there.
  8. The top of the range is decided on fine margins. Treat very high estimates as approximate and plan alternatives.
  9. Think in ranges. Run cautious, realistic and optimistic scenarios and plan across them.
  10. Compare selection ranks with thresholds. Add your expected adjustments before comparing with any course.

Used with these points in mind, an ATAR calculator is one of the most useful planning tools available to Year 12 students. It cannot tell you the future, but it can help you prepare for it.

Common questions

Can an ATAR calculator predict my exact ATAR?

No. Your exact ATAR depends on how every student performs and on the exact scaling for that year, which is only known once results are finalised. A good calculator gives a close estimate, not the exact figure.

Why do ATAR calculators give different results?

Usually because they use different scaling data or methods. One might use the latest official report while another uses old figures, or takes shortcuts like averaging marks instead of scaling and combining them properly.

What data do accurate calculators use?

The most accurate calculators use the latest official scaling report from your state, scale each subject, and combine your best results the way your state does, rather than simply averaging your marks.

How close is an ATAR estimate to the real result?

With good scaling data, an estimate is usually within a few points of the final ATAR. It is most accurate through the middle and upper range, and more approximate at the very top.

Should I trust my ATAR estimate?

Trust it as a guide, not a guarantee. Use it to set targets and plan your preferences, re-run it as real marks come in, and compare it with your course cut-off with a few points of buffer.

What is the difference between an ATAR estimate and a prediction?

An estimate uses last year’s scaling and the marks you enter. A prediction usually means an estimate built from marks you expect rather than real ones, so it is rougher. Only your admissions centre produces the exact official result.

When are ATAR estimates least reliable?

Estimates are least reliable at very high ranks, where tiny mark differences move the rank, and for unusual subject combinations or small-cohort subjects. Predicted marks add further uncertainty.

Why can’t an ATAR calculator be exact?

Because your ATAR is a rank against your whole age group in your state, it depends on how every other student performs that year. Scaling and the conversion from aggregate to ATAR are also recalculated each year from the current results. None of that information exists until results are finalised, so any calculation beforehand has to rely on past data and on the marks you enter.

What is the biggest source of error in an ATAR estimate?

The marks you enter. If they are optimistic, your estimate will usually be too high; if they are cautious, it will usually be too low. Moderation, scaling changes and the conversion to a rank also play a part, but realistic inputs make the biggest difference to how close an estimate lands.

Do ATAR calculators account for moderation?

Most calculators cannot fully account for moderation, because it depends on how your school group performs in the final exams, which is not known in advance. If you enter raw school marks, treat your estimate as approximate. Using exam-style marks, such as trial results marked against official guidelines, can reduce the effect.

Does a calculator use this year’s scaling?

No. This year’s scaling is calculated from this year’s results, which are not available until results are released. A calculator uses the most recent published scaling, which describes the previous year’s students. For most large subjects, scaling changes only modestly from year to year, but some subjects move more.

How often should I re-run my ATAR estimate?

Re-run it after each major assessment period, after trial or practice exams and whenever your marks change noticeably. Each time, enter your most realistic marks and record the result. Tracking your estimate over time is more useful than any single figure, because it shows whether your results are moving in the right direction.

Is an ATAR estimate more accurate later in Year 12?

Usually, yes. As more of your final-year assessment is completed, more of your result is known and your inputs become more realistic. The range of plausible ATARs narrows. The final exams and the current year’s scaling still add uncertainty, so even a late estimate should be treated as a range.

Why does my estimate change so much when I change one mark?

If your aggregate sits in the crowded middle of the range, a small change can move you past many students, so your estimate can change noticeably. The effect also depends on whether the subject is counted and how it scales at your level. Near the top, the same change in aggregate usually moves your ATAR less.

Why does improving one subject not change my estimate?

That subject is probably not among the results your state counts. Until it rises above a weaker counted result, it will have little or no effect on your aggregate. It still matters, though, because if another subject goes less well, it may become one of your counted results.

Are ATAR calculators more accurate in some states than others?

Yes. Accuracy depends partly on how much each state publishes and how complex its process is. Where admissions centres publish detailed scaling and conversion data, calculators can model the process more closely. In South Australia and the NT, for example, SATAC does not publish scaled scores for each subject, so estimates there are more approximate.

How accurate are NSW ATAR calculators?

NSW calculators can be built on UAC’s detailed annual scaling report, which makes careful estimates possible. The main uncertainties are the marks you enter, the moderation of school assessment marks and year-to-year changes in scaling. Because each HSC mark combines your moderated assessment mark and your exam mark equally, your exam performance has a large effect.

How accurate are Victorian ATAR calculators?

Victorian calculators can use VTAC’s scaling report and conversion data, so the process can be modelled closely. The main uncertainty is the study score itself, which reflects your position in each study across the state. If you can estimate your study scores realistically, Victorian estimates can be quite useful for planning.

How accurate are Queensland ATAR calculators?

Queensland calculators can use QTAC’s published information on scaling and the ATAR. Because internal assessment contributes 75 per cent of most General subjects and 50 per cent of mathematics and science subjects, and students often know much of it before the external assessment, part of the result is fairly predictable. The external assessment and the year’s scaling remain the main uncertainties.

How accurate are WA ATAR calculators?

WA calculators can use TISC’s published statistics on scaling and the relationship between the TEA and the ATAR. Each ATAR course combines a moderated school mark and an exam mark equally, so exams are a major source of uncertainty. A good WA calculator must also apply the bonuses for a language other than English and for Mathematics Methods and Specialist correctly.

How accurate are SA and NT ATAR calculators?

Estimates for South Australia and the NT are generally more approximate, because SATAC does not publish scaled scores for each subject. Calculators have to approximate the scaling step. They can still be useful for planning if you treat the result as a range and apply the 90-credit structure correctly.

How accurate are Tasmanian ATAR calculators?

Tasmania’s TE Score rules changed for students from 2025, so calculators relying on older data may not reflect the current rules. Because the TE Score can include courses completed over two years, some of your result may already be known. Check that your calculator uses the current rules and treat the estimate as a range.

How accurate are ACT ATAR calculators?

The ACT uses its own process to scale results across colleges and courses, which is harder for an outside calculator to replicate than a single statewide exam system. ACT estimates are useful for planning, but they are generally more approximate than estimates for the largest states.

Can I use a NSW calculator if I study in another state?

No. Each state has its own subjects, scaling and counting rules. A calculator built for NSW will not give a meaningful estimate for a student in Victoria, Queensland or any other state. Use a calculator built for the state where you are completing Year 12, even if you plan to apply to a university interstate.

Should I enter my school marks or my exam marks?

It depends on what the calculator asks for. Some calculators expect a final subject result, such as an HSC mark or a study score, while others expect separate school and exam marks. Read the instructions carefully. If you are estimating a final result yourself, combine realistic estimates of both your school assessment and your exam performance.

Can I use trial marks in an ATAR calculator?

Yes, and trial marks are often the best guide you have to your exam performance. But trials are school-based, may be harder or easier than the final exam and are marked against your school’s standards. Use them as a guide, combine them with your school assessment results and teacher feedback, and treat the estimate as a range.

Why did my estimate drop after I entered my trial marks?

Your trial marks may have been lower than the marks you entered before, or they may have changed which subjects count. Trial exams are often demanding, and many students find their trial marks lower than their earlier school assessment marks. A drop is a prompt to plan and focus, not a final verdict.

Should I trust an estimate based on Year 11 results?

Treat it as a very rough guide. Year 11 results give a first indication of how you are performing, but much can change in Year 12. In most states, Year 11 results do not count directly towards the ATAR, although in Tasmania some may count towards the TE Score. Use early estimates to explore, not to predict.

What is a likely range on an ATAR calculator?

A likely range is the span of ATARs that an estimate suggests is most plausible, given the uncertainty in your inputs and in the data. It is more honest than a single figure because it acknowledges that the current year’s scaling and conversion are not yet known. Plan your preferences across the whole range.

Why do some calculators show a range instead of one number?

Because a single number with two decimal places suggests a precision that does not exist before results are released. Showing a range acknowledges the uncertainty from your inputs, moderation, scaling changes and the conversion to a rank. Calculators that show a range are usually being more honest about what they can do.

Do ATAR calculators include bonus points?

Most ATAR calculators estimate your ATAR, which does not include adjustment factors. To estimate your selection rank for a course, you need to add any adjustments you are eligible for at that institution. Some tools, such as a selection rank calculator, help with this step. Remember that some adjustments require an application.

How do I estimate my selection rank?

Start with your estimated ATAR range. Then list the adjustment factors you are likely to receive for each course, such as subject, regional or equity adjustments, and add them to each end of your range. Check each institution’s rules and caps. Compare the result with each course’s recent lowest selection rank.

Is a calculator estimate the same as my school’s prediction?

Not necessarily. A calculator uses published data and the marks you enter, while a school prediction may use the school’s own records of how past students’ results compared with their final outcomes. Both can be useful. If they differ, ask your teachers how their prediction was made and compare the inputs.

Can teachers predict my ATAR more accurately than a calculator?

Teachers can often judge where you are likely to finish in their subject better than you can, which improves the inputs to an estimate. Schools with years of data may also know how their marks usually compare with final results. Combining a teacher’s view with a well-built calculator usually gives the most reliable picture.

Why do online ATAR calculators sometimes give very high estimates?

Common reasons include optimistic inputs, a method that averages raw marks instead of scaling them, older scaling data, ignoring compulsory subjects such as English, or counting more subjects than your state allows. Check the calculator’s method and your inputs before relying on a surprisingly high figure.

Why do some calculators give lower estimates than others?

A calculator may use more recent or more conservative data, apply your state’s rules more strictly, or include compulsory subjects that another calculator omits. Lower is not necessarily less accurate. Compare the data source, method and inputs of each tool rather than choosing the one that gives the number you prefer.

Do ATAR calculators work for students who repeat Year 12?

Not always. Students who repeat Year 12 or particular subjects may have results from more than one year, and each state has rules about which results can count. Many calculators assume all results come from a single year. Ask your admissions centre how your results will be treated and use the estimate with caution.

Do ATAR calculators work for accelerated students?

Some do and some do not. If you completed a subject a year early, it may be able to count under your state’s rules, but many calculators assume all subjects are completed in the same year. Check how your calculator handles accelerated results and confirm your state’s rules with your school.

Can an ATAR calculator include VET?

Some can, but not all. In Queensland, an eligible VET qualification at Certificate III or higher can be one of your five results. In Victoria, scored VCE VET programs can count as studies and some unscored VET can contribute an increment. In South Australia and the NT, Recognised Studies can contribute up to 20 credits. Check whether your calculator models these rules.

Can IB students use an ATAR calculator?

An ordinary ATAR calculator does not apply to International Baccalaureate results. IB results are converted to a rank comparable with the ATAR using tables published by admissions centres. If you are an IB student, use your admissions centre’s current conversion information instead.

Does the ATAR calculator know how hard my exams were?

No. The difficulty of a particular exam affects raw marks, but processes such as moderation and scaling are designed to place results on a fair, comparable footing. A calculator cannot know in advance how this year’s papers will be marked or how other students will perform on them.

Does my school affect the accuracy of my estimate?

Your school does not add a bonus or penalty to your ATAR, but it can affect how your school assessment marks are moderated. If your school group performs differently in the exam from what its school marks suggested, your moderated marks can move. That is one reason raw school marks give only an approximate estimate.

What should I do if my estimate and my official ATAR are very different?

Compare your final subject results with the marks you entered. In most cases, the difference comes from your subject results. If your results were close to your inputs, the difference may come from scaling or conversion changes. If you believe a subject result is wrong, contact your school or curriculum authority about that result.

Can I ask for my ATAR to be checked if it is lower than my estimate?

A difference from your estimate is not, by itself, a reason to think your ATAR is wrong. Errors in the ATAR calculation are very rare. If you believe one of your underlying subject results is wrong, each state has a process for checking or reviewing results. Your ATAR is recalculated only if an underlying result changes.

Are ATAR calculators useful at all if they cannot be exact?

Yes. A good estimate tells you your broad position, which subjects matter most, whether you are improving and which courses are realistic. Those are valuable for planning preferences, setting targets and deciding where to focus. The key is to treat the estimate as a range rather than a promise.

How do I know if a calculator is up to date?

A trustworthy calculator names the official report it uses and the year that report covers, and often states when it was last updated. If you cannot find this information, be cautious. Scaling reports are published after each year’s results, so an up-to-date calculator should use the most recent available report.

What makes a calculator transparent?

A transparent calculator explains its data sources, the year of its data, the steps it follows and its limitations. It shows a range rather than presenting a single figure as exact. Our methodology page sets out this information for our calculators, so you can judge the estimates for yourself.

Why do two good calculators sometimes disagree slightly?

Even well-built calculators can differ slightly if they use different years of data, handle certain rules a little differently or round results at different steps. A small difference between two good calculators is useful information, because it shows how sensitive your estimate is to data and method. Plan around the range they suggest together.

Is it a good idea to use several ATAR calculators?

It can be, as long as each one is built for your state and uses recent official data. Comparing them can help you see how sensitive your estimate is. Avoid giving weight to tools that average raw marks or apply one set of rules to every state, and always check that you entered identical inputs in each.

Why do averaging tools give misleading ATARs?

An average raw mark ignores scaling, the rules for which results count and the conversion from aggregate to rank. In NSW, UAC explains that most HSC courses have a median mark above 75 while the median ATAR is usually around 70, and marks around 70 typically lead to ATARs in the 50s. An average mark can therefore be far from an ATAR.

What is the difference between an aggregate and an ATAR?

An aggregate is the total of the scaled results that count under your state’s rules. The ATAR is a rank that shows where that aggregate sits relative to your age group. The same aggregate can correspond to slightly different ATARs in different years, depending on how all students performed.

What is a conversion table?

A conversion table shows which aggregates correspond to which ATARs in a particular year. Some admissions centres publish one, such as the table VTAC releases on results day. Calculators use past conversion data to estimate your ATAR from your aggregate, which is one reason estimates cannot be exact.

Why is the top of the ATAR range harder to estimate?

Near the top, the courses that interest students often have thresholds separated by fractions of a point, and small, unpredictable differences in exam performance can decide which side of a threshold you land on. In ATAR points, estimates near the top may move less for a given change in aggregate, but the decisions there depend on finer margins.

Why is the middle of the ATAR range so sensitive?

Many students have similar aggregates in the middle of the range, so a small change in your aggregate moves you past a large number of them. That means a few marks can move your ATAR by a point or more. Realistic inputs matter most for students in this part of the range.

Should I widen my range if I take small subjects?

Yes. Subjects with small cohorts can scale less predictably from year to year, because a few students with unusual results can shift the pattern. If one or more of your counted subjects has a small cohort, allow a wider range around your estimate.

Can a calculator tell me which subjects to choose?

A calculator can show how different results would combine, but it cannot tell you where you will rank in a subject you have not studied. Choose subjects based on your strengths, interests and the requirements of courses you are considering. Use scaling only as a tie-breaker between subjects you are equally strong in.

Should I drop a subject that is not counting in my estimate?

Usually not. Your final results may differ from your current marks, and a subject that is not counting now could become one of your counted results if another subject goes less well. Where your state’s rules allow, an extra subject can act as a buffer. Weigh that protection against the time it takes.

How should I use my estimate to set a target?

Find the recent lowest selection rank for the course you want, add any adjustments you are likely to receive, and aim a little above that figure to allow for movement in thresholds and uncertainty in your estimate. Then use your calculator to see which subjects could close any gap.

How many preferences can I list?

It depends on your admissions centre. UAC allows five preferences, QTAC and TISC allow six, and VTAC allows eight. Use your estimate to make sure your list includes aspirational, realistic and safe options across your likely range, listed in the order you genuinely want them.

Should my estimate change the order of my preferences?

No. List courses in the order you genuinely want them. You are offered the highest preference you are eligible for, so putting a course you want less above one you want more can cost you the better option. Use your estimate to decide which courses to include, not to reorder them.

What if my estimate is below the course I want?

Look at the size of the gap. If it is small, check adjustment factors you might be eligible for and focus your effort on the subjects that move your estimate most. Keep the course on your list if you genuinely want it. If the gap is large, research pathways into the course as well.

What if my estimate is well above the course I want?

That is encouraging, but do not remove safe options or ease off. Your estimate is a range, and your final results may differ. Keep working steadily, check that you meet every other requirement for the course, and consider whether there are courses you would want more that are now realistic.

Do calculators consider special consideration or misadventure?

No. If illness or other circumstances affect your exams and you apply for special consideration or misadventure, your result may be determined using other evidence. A calculator cannot anticipate that outcome. Ask your school how the process works in your state.

Can I estimate my ATAR straight after my exams?

You can, using your best judgement of how each exam went, but it is subject to the same limits as any estimate and is often affected by how you feel about each paper. It can help with planning, but it is usually wise to wait for official results before making major decisions.

Do universities look at my estimated ATAR?

No. Universities use your official ATAR, together with any adjustment factors, when they make offers through the main rounds. Some early offer schemes use other criteria, such as Year 11 results, but they do not use your estimated ATAR from a calculator.

Do early offers depend on my estimated ATAR?

Early offer and school recommendation schemes use their own criteria, often Year 11 results, school assessments or personal statements, not a calculator estimate. Some early offers are conditional on a minimum ATAR or prerequisite, and an estimate can help you judge how comfortably you are likely to meet that condition.

Can AI chatbots estimate my ATAR?

A general-purpose AI chatbot may produce a figure, but unless it is working from current official scaling data and your state’s exact rules, its estimate may be based on general patterns or outdated information. Ask it to explain its method and sources, and check any figures against your admissions centre’s published reports.

Are forum ATAR estimates reliable?

Forum posts can show what other students experienced, but individual stories are not representative and figures are often remembered imperfectly or based on older years. Use forums for ideas and support, not as a source of data for your own estimate.

What does garbage in, garbage out mean for ATAR calculators?

It means a calculator can only be as good as the marks you give it. If you enter realistic marks based on real results, practice exams and teacher feedback, the estimate can be useful. If you enter wishful marks, the estimate will be wishful too, however good the calculator’s data and method.

How do I make my exam estimates realistic?

Complete full past papers under timed, closed-book conditions, mark them strictly against official marking guidelines or ask a teacher to mark them, look at your typical result rather than your best, and remember that the exam compares you with the whole state. Build a cautious scenario that allows for an off day.

Does the ATAR calculator know my rank in my school?

No. A calculator only knows the marks you enter. Your rank within your school group is important, because moderation in most states preserves the order of students within a school group. Your teachers can tell you how your rank compares and what that might mean for your final results.

Why might my estimate be too low?

You may have entered cautious marks, your exams may go better than your practice results suggest, your subjects may scale more strongly this year, or your moderated marks may rise if your school group performs strongly in the exams. A low estimate is a planning tool, not a final verdict.

Why might my estimate be too high?

You may have entered optimistic marks, your exams may go less well than expected, your subjects may scale less strongly this year, or moderation may lower your school marks. You may also have used a calculator that averages raw marks or omits compulsory subjects. Run a cautious scenario to see a realistic floor.

Is it normal for the official ATAR to differ from the estimate?

Yes. Some difference is normal and expected, because the official ATAR uses your final results and the current year’s scaling and conversion, none of which a calculator can know in advance. For students who enter realistic marks into a good calculator, the official result is usually close, but rarely identical.

Do ATAR calculators get better over time?

A calculator becomes more useful for you as more of your results are known. Calculators themselves are updated as new scaling reports are published each year. After a major change in a state’s rules, they may need a few years of new data before their estimates settle.

What are the limits of the calculators on this site?

Our calculators cannot predict your raw marks, cannot know how this year’s students will perform and cannot replace your official ATAR. They also cannot tell you which universities will make you an offer. They use recent official data, apply your state’s rules and show a likely range so that you can plan with an honest estimate.

Where can I check the data a calculator uses?

Go to the official report the calculator names, such as UAC’s scaling report, VTAC’s scaling report, QTAC’s reports or TISC’s tertiary entrance statistics. Look up the scaling for your subjects at your level of result and check the rules for building the aggregate. Our guide to reading a scaling report explains how.

Should parents rely on an ATAR estimate?

Parents can find an estimate helpful for understanding their child’s broad position and planning options, but they should treat it as a range, ask about the inputs and focus on what can still change. A calm conversation about options across the range is more useful than pressure about a particular number.

Can an estimate tell me if I will get into medicine?

Not on its own. Medicine is very competitive, and most undergraduate and provisional-entry programs consider an admissions test such as the UCAT and an interview alongside academic results. Very high ATAR estimates are also the hardest to pin down precisely. Use an estimate as one input and plan alternatives.

Is an estimate useful if I am aiming for a pathway rather than direct entry?

Yes. An estimate can help you see whether direct entry is realistic, which pathway programs suit your likely range and whether adjustment factors could make a difference. It can also help you choose a related course with a lower threshold as a starting point for a later transfer.

What is the most important thing to remember about ATAR calculators?

A calculator gives an estimate, not your ATAR. Use one built for your state with recent official data, enter realistic marks, treat the result as a range and re-run it as your results come in. Used that way, an estimate is a genuinely helpful planning tool.

What is the difference between accuracy and precision in an ATAR estimate?

Precision is about detail, such as showing an estimate to two decimal places. Accuracy is about how close the estimate is to the official result. A range such as 85 to 89 is less precise than 87.35, but if your ATAR turns out to be 86.10, the range was accurate and the precise figure was not.

Why do calculators show estimates to two decimal places?

Because the official ATAR is reported to two decimal places, many calculators use the same format. Before results are released, though, the decimal places carry little real information. It is more useful to round the estimate mentally and think in terms of a range.

Is an HSC Band 6 the same as a high ATAR?

Not exactly. A band describes a standard of achievement in one course, based on your HSC mark. Your ATAR depends on your scaled marks across your best 10 units, including 2 units of English. Strong bands usually go with a strong ATAR, but there is no fixed conversion from bands to an ATAR.

How do NSW extension courses affect an estimate?

In NSW, 1-unit extension courses are reported out of 50 and count as single units in your best 10. They often attract strong cohorts. A calculator must handle them correctly, including UAC’s rules on how related courses, such as different levels of mathematics, can be counted together.

What study score should I enter in a Victorian calculator?

Enter your most realistic estimate of each study score, based on where you are likely to rank in the study across the state. The VCAA sets the mean study score at 30 with a standard deviation of about 7, so roughly two-thirds of students receive between about 23 and 37. Your SAC results, practice exams and teacher feedback all help.

Does the Victorian language adjustment change my estimate?

It can. VTAC applies a policy adjustment to the scaling of languages, which affects the scaled study scores of language students. A good Victorian calculator includes this adjustment. Its effect on your ATAR depends on your result in the language and on your whole combination of studies.

Do Queensland Applied subjects count in an ATAR estimate?

One Applied subject can contribute to a Queensland ATAR when combined with four General subjects. Applied subjects have no external assessment, so their results are based entirely on internal assessment. Check that your calculator lets you include an Applied subject and applies Queensland’s best-five rule correctly.

Why does my WA estimate change when I add Mathematics Methods?

In Western Australia, 10 per cent of your scaled score in Mathematics Methods is added to your TEA as a bonus, even if Methods is not among your best four. The same applies to Mathematics Specialist and to your best language other than English. A good WA calculator should reflect these bonuses.

What is the maximum TEA in Western Australia?

The maximum TEA is 430: four scaled scores of up to 100 each, plus bonuses of up to 10 each for a language other than English, Mathematics Methods and Mathematics Specialist. Very few students reach the maximum, but the structure shows why the bonuses matter for an estimate.

Why are SA and NT ATAR estimates less precise?

SATAC converts subject results into scaled scores but does not publish scaled scores for each subject, and it advises students not to choose subjects based on historical scaling. Calculators therefore have to approximate the scaling step. Estimates are still useful for planning, but they should be treated as approximate ranges.

How many credits count towards an SA or NT ATAR?

Your university aggregate is based on your best 90 credits of eligible study: three 20-credit subjects and a flexible option of 30 credits. Up to 20 credits of Recognised Studies can be included. A calculator must apply this structure correctly to give a sensible estimate.

Did the Tasmanian ATAR rules change recently?

Yes. From 2025, the Tasmanian TE Score is built from a student’s best Level 3 and Level 4 course scores over up to two years, with each course worth up to 15 points. Calculators that rely on older rules or older data may not reflect the current system, so check which rules your calculator uses.

What does an ATAR of 80 mean in Tasmania?

The University of Tasmania explains that an ATAR of 80 means a TE Score at or above that of 80 per cent of Tasmanian Year 12 school leavers. The ATAR is designed to be comparable between states, so the same ATAR has the same meaning wherever it was earned.

How is the ACT ATAR estimated?

The ACT ATAR is based on your best three major scaled scores plus 60 per cent of your next major or minor. Scaled scores come from the ACT’s own process for comparing results across colleges and courses, which is harder for an outside calculator to replicate, so ACT estimates are generally approximate.

What was the median ATAR in the ACT in 2025?

The median ATAR among ACT students who received one in 2025 was 78.35, and 78.8 per cent of ATAR recipients had an ATAR of 65 or above. About 61 per cent of the 5,235 students who received a Senior Secondary Certificate received a Tertiary Entrance Statement.

How many students received an ATAR in Queensland in 2025?

About 30,167 Queensland students received an ATAR in 2025, and 37 received the top ATAR of 99.95. The Queensland TEA is the sum of the best five scaled results, with a maximum of 500, and ATARs of 30 or below are reported as 30.00 or less.

How many Victorian students received an ATAR in 2025?

VTAC reported 49,310 ATARs for 2025, up from 47,461 in 2024, with 42 students receiving the top ATAR of 99.95. The average ATAR in 2024 was 69.52. Victorian students take a very wide variety of study combinations, which calculators must handle correctly.

How many NSW students received an ATAR in 2025?

UAC reported that 60,432 NSW students received an ATAR in 2025, with a median of 70.75. About 17.3 per cent of ATAR recipients had 90 or above, 34.6 per cent had 80 or above and 51.3 per cent had 70 or above. Fifty-three students received the top ATAR of 99.95.

Why do HSC marks and ATARs differ so much in the middle?

They are on different scales. HSC marks describe achievement in each course, while the ATAR is a rank against your whole age group. UAC explains that most HSC courses have a median mark above 75, while the median ATAR is usually around 70, and that marks around 70 typically lead to ATARs in the 50s.

Do practical subjects make my estimate less reliable?

They can. Results in subjects with performances, folios, practical exams or major works are often harder to predict than written exam results. If you take one or more of these subjects, use your teachers’ feedback on your practical work and allow a slightly wider range around your estimate.

What is an estimate log?

An estimate log is a simple record of your ATAR estimates over time. For each entry, you note the date, the calculator and data year, the marks you entered and your cautious, realistic and optimistic estimates. A log turns separate numbers into a trend and makes it easy to understand any difference when your official ATAR arrives.

How can I build a rough ATAR estimate by hand?

Find your admissions centre’s latest scaling data, look up the scaled result for each of your subjects at your expected level, combine them under your state’s rules and, where a conversion table is published, convert the total to an ATAR. Comparing your hand estimate with a calculator is a good way to understand the process.

Why does it take weeks to get my ATAR after my last exam?

Exams must be marked and checked, school assessment must be moderated or confirmed and combined with exam results, scaling must be calculated from every student’s results, and aggregates must be built and ranked. Each step depends on all students’ final results, which is also why a calculator cannot do the same job in advance.

Is the official ATAR ever wrong?

Errors in the ATAR calculation itself are very rare. The process involves careful marking, moderation, scaling and checking, and it applies the same rules to every student. If one of your underlying subject results is corrected through your state’s review process, your ATAR is recalculated.

Should I keep checking my estimate every day?

No. Running an estimate after each major assessment period is useful, but checking it every day rarely changes what you should do and can increase anxiety. Set a regular time to update it, focus on your study in between, and talk to someone you trust if worry about your estimate is affecting you.

Does a calculator know about this year’s syllabus changes?

Not directly. A calculator uses past scaling data, which reflects the syllabus and the students of that past year. If a subject’s syllabus or cohort has changed significantly, its scaling may differ, and estimates for students taking it will be less precise.

Can scaling make my estimate go down even if my marks go up?

Not within one calculator using fixed data: higher marks lead to a higher estimate. But between years, scaling can change, so your official ATAR could be lower than an estimate based on the previous year’s scaling even if your marks matched your inputs. That is one reason estimates are ranges.

Do bigger states have more accurate estimates?

Larger states often have more stable year-to-year patterns because their subjects have larger cohorts, which can help calculators. Accuracy also depends on how much each state publishes and how complex its process is. In every state, the realism of your inputs remains the biggest factor.

What should I do with a very cautious estimate?

Use it as a floor for planning, and make sure your preference list includes good options at or below it. But also run a realistic scenario, so that you do not leave courses off your list that you could reach. After results, review your preferences in case your official ATAR is higher than your cautious estimate.

What should I do with a very optimistic estimate?

Treat it as your best case, not your expectation. Use it to identify aspirational courses worth listing first, but make sure your list also covers your realistic and cautious estimates. Keep working steadily so that your final results give you the best chance of reaching the top of your range.

Should I compare my estimate with a course’s median ATAR?

Be careful. A median is the middle of the group of students offered a place, so half of them had a lower rank. Compare your estimated selection rank range with the lowest selection rank, and use the median and highest ranks only to understand where students offered places typically sat.

What is a guaranteed entry rank?

Some institutions publish a guaranteed entry rank for particular courses. If your selection rank is at or above it and you meet every other requirement, you are guaranteed an offer. If a course you want has one, compare it with the cautious end of your estimated range to judge how safe the course is.

Can a calculator tell me whether to drop to a lower maths course?

Only partly. It can compare how different results in each course would contribute to your estimate, but it cannot tell you what your result in each course would be. Ask your teachers for a realistic estimate of your result in each option, and weigh the difference against prerequisites, workload and your interest.

Do I need an ATAR calculator if I am not getting an ATAR?

No. If your subject combination will not lead to an ATAR, an ATAR calculator is not relevant. University is still possible through pathways such as diplomas, enabling programs, TAFE qualifications, portfolio entry and special admission schemes. Your careers adviser can help you find routes that suit you.

How many Year 12 students receive an ATAR?

A December 2025 report from the Mitchell Institute at Victoria University estimated that around 64 per cent of Year 12 students nationally receive an ATAR. The share varies between states. Students who do not receive an ATAR often complete Year 12 through vocational or other pathways.

Why does my estimate differ from my friend’s when our marks are similar?

Your subjects may scale differently, your results may combine differently under your state’s counting rules, and one of you may have entered a different type of mark. Two students with similar raw marks can have quite different aggregates and therefore different estimates.

Can I trust an estimate that ignores English?

No, if you are in NSW or Victoria. In NSW, your 2 units of English must count towards your ATAR, and in Victoria an English study must be in your primary four. A calculator that leaves English out can overstate your estimate, especially if English is one of your weaker subjects.

Does an estimate include my Year 11 results?

In most states, ATAR calculators use final-year results, because that is what the ATAR is based on. In Tasmania, eligible Level 3 and Level 4 courses completed over two years can count towards the TE Score, so a Tasmanian calculator should let you include them.

What is the most common input mistake?

Entering hoped-for marks instead of realistic ones is the most common. Other frequent mistakes include using a calculator for the wrong state, entering the wrong type of mark, getting units or credits wrong and forgetting compulsory subjects such as English.

Should I include subjects I am not sure will count?

Yes. Include every subject you are taking. A good calculator will choose your best results under your state’s rules. Including all your subjects also lets you see how a subject outside your current best results would act as a buffer if another subject goes less well.

Can I estimate my ATAR if I have special provisions in exams?

Special provisions, such as extra time or a reader, are designed to give students fair access to exams and do not change how results are scaled or combined. You can use a calculator in the usual way. If misadventure or illness affects an exam, ask your school about your state’s process.

How do I estimate my exam mark realistically?

Complete full past papers under timed, closed-book conditions, mark them strictly against official marking guidelines or ask a teacher to mark them, and use your typical result rather than your best. Remember that the exam compares you with the whole state and allow for an off day in a cautious scenario.

Does the date I run a calculator matter?

Yes, in two ways. The later in Year 12 you run it, the more of your result is known, so your inputs are more realistic. And the calculator’s data may be updated when a new scaling report is published, so an estimate run after an update may differ slightly from one run before it.

Should I share my estimate with my school?

It can help. Sharing your estimate and the marks you entered with a teacher or careers adviser lets them check your inputs and give feedback on whether your expectations are realistic. They can also help you build a preference list that works across your range.

Is it worth paying for an ATAR estimate?

Not usually. Well-built calculators that use official data are freely available, and your teachers can help you make your inputs realistic. Whatever tool you use, no one can calculate your exact ATAR before results are released, so be wary of any service that promises precision.

What should I do with my estimate in the week before results?

Run a final estimate with realistic, cautious and optimistic marks, write down your range and estimated selection ranks, check that your preference list covers the whole range with your first choice at the top, and note the change of preference deadline for the first ATAR-based round in your state.

Should I still look at my estimate once my official ATAR arrives?

Only to understand any difference. From the moment your official ATAR arrives, use it, together with any adjustment factors, for every decision. Comparing your final subject results with the marks you entered can explain why your official ATAR differs from your estimate.

Is an estimate the same as a selection rank?

No. Most calculators estimate your ATAR. Your selection rank for a course is your ATAR plus any adjustment factors you are eligible for at that institution. To compare yourself with a course threshold, add your expected adjustments to your estimated ATAR range.

Why might a teacher’s estimate be lower than a calculator’s?

Your teacher may expect your exam result to be lower than your school marks, may know how your school’s marks usually compare with final results, or may be allowing for moderation. Ask how they arrived at their estimate, and consider building a cautious scenario that reflects their view.

Why might a teacher’s estimate be higher than a calculator’s?

Your teacher may expect you to improve in the final exam, may know that your school group usually performs strongly, or may think the marks you entered are too cautious. Talk through the difference with them and consider whether a realistic scenario should sit a little higher.

Can I rely on an estimate for scholarship applications?

Scholarships that depend on academic results usually use your official ATAR or other final results, not an estimate. An estimate can help you judge which scholarships are realistic to apply for, but check each scholarship’s criteria and deadlines, as some close before results are released.

Do calculators work for students in remote or regional areas?

Yes. Students in remote and regional areas are ranked under the same rules as other students in their state, so a state-specific calculator applies. Regional students may also be eligible for adjustment factors, so it is especially worth estimating your selection rank for each course as well as your ATAR.

How do I know which subjects are counted in my estimate?

A good calculator shows which results are included in your aggregate. If yours does not, apply your state’s rules yourself: for example, your 2 units of English and best 8 other units in NSW, or your best five scaled results in Queensland. Knowing which subjects count helps you set study priorities.

What happens if I enter a subject my calculator does not list?

If a subject is missing from a calculator’s list, the calculator cannot scale it properly. Check whether the subject goes by another name, whether it is eligible to count towards the ATAR in your state, and whether another calculator for your state includes it. Do not substitute a different subject, as its scaling may be quite different.

Should I use the previous year’s ATAR thresholds with my estimate?

Yes, as a guide. The most recent thresholds are the best indication you have before results, but they can move. Compare your estimated selection rank range with them, classify each course as aspirational, realistic or safe, and build a preference list that works across your range.

Why does my calculator say my estimate is approximate?

Because it is. The current year’s scaling and conversion from aggregate to ATAR are not known until results are released, and your entered marks may differ from your final results. A calculator that says so is being honest about its limits, which is a good sign.

What is the single best way to improve my estimate’s accuracy?

Make your inputs realistic. Use your actual school results, trial or practice exams marked strictly against official guidelines, and your teachers’ views on where you are likely to finish. Then use a calculator built for your state with recent official data, and treat the result as a range.

What is the difference between an ATAR estimate and a target ATAR?

An estimate is a calculation of where your current or expected results are likely to place you. A target is the ATAR or selection rank you are aiming for, usually set a little above the threshold of a course you want. Comparing the two shows how far you need to move and which subjects could help you get there.

Can my estimate help me decide how to spend my study time?

Yes. By raising one subject’s mark at a time in a calculator, you can see which subjects move your estimate most at your current position. Those are often the best places to focus extra effort. Keep working in every subject, though, because your results may change which subjects count.

Are estimates useful for students aiming for courses with low thresholds?

Yes. Even when a course’s threshold is comfortably below your likely range, an estimate helps you check that you have a buffer, decide whether to add more ambitious courses above it on your preference list and confirm that you meet every other requirement, such as prerequisites.

Why do some calculators ask for my school?

Some tools ask for your school so they can describe your situation or use school-level information, but your school does not add a bonus or penalty to your ATAR. If a calculator uses school information, check how it uses it and remember that the official calculation applies the same rules to every student in your state.

What is the best single sentence to remember about ATAR calculators?

A good calculator, built for your state and fed realistic marks, gives a useful range for planning, but only your admissions centre can give you your actual ATAR.
