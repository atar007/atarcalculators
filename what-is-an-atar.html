What is an ATAR?

If you are in Year 12, you have heard the word ATAR more times than you can count. This guide explains what it actually is, in plain English, and clears up the biggest thing students get wrong about it.

The ATAR (Australian Tertiary Admission Rank) is a number between 0.00 and 99.95 that shows where you rank against your Year 12 age group. It is a rank, not a mark. An ATAR of 80.00 means you finished ahead of about 80% of students. Universities use it to compare students who studied completely different subjects, and it is the main number used for entry into most undergraduate courses.

Key takeaways

  • The ATAR is a rank from 0.00 to 99.95, not a mark or a percentage of questions you got right.
  • An ATAR of 80.00 means you ranked in the top 20% of your age group.
  • It lets universities compare students who studied different subjects fairly.
  • The highest possible ATAR is 99.95; scores below 30 are usually reported as “less than 30”.
  • Every state uses the same ATAR, worked out by a different admissions centre (UAC, VTAC, QTAC and others).
  • Your ATAR comes from your scaled results, so how your subjects scale matters.
  • The ATAR is one way into university, not the only way — many pathways exist.

What does ATAR stand for?

ATAR stands for Australian Tertiary Admission Rank. Each word tells you something useful. Australian means it is used across the country. Tertiary Admission means it exists to help you get into tertiary study, which is university and some other courses. Rank is the most important word, and it is the one most students miss.

The ATAR is a rank. It places you in order against everyone else your age, from top to bottom. It does not measure how many marks you scored, or what percentage of an exam you got right. It measures where you finished compared with other students.

That one idea explains almost everything else about the ATAR. Once you see it as a rank, the odd maximum, the scaling, and the way “good” depends on your goal all start to make sense.

Each word, in a little more detail

Australian. The ATAR is a national measure. Every state and territory produces ATARs on the same scale, and universities across Australia accept them. A student who completes Year 12 in Hobart can use their ATAR to apply in Brisbane, Perth or Sydney without any conversion.

Tertiary. Tertiary education is the stage after secondary school. In everyday use it usually means university, although the ATAR is also used for some other higher education providers. Most vocational courses at TAFE use their own entry requirements rather than the ATAR.

Admission. The ATAR exists to help institutions decide whom to admit. It is a selection tool. It is not a qualification in itself, and it is not printed on your Year 12 certificate in most states; it is issued separately by the organisation that calculates it.

Rank. The ATAR tells you your position relative to other people, not the amount you learned. That is why it is reported in a way that looks like a percentage but behaves like a place in a queue.

What the ATAR is not

  • It is not a mark out of 100.
  • It is not the percentage of questions you answered correctly.
  • It is not an average of your subject results.
  • It is not a pass or fail grade.
  • It is not your Year 12 certificate, such as the HSC, VCE or QCE.
  • It is not a measure of intelligence or potential.

Keeping these in mind clears up much of the confusion students and parents have when they first see an ATAR.

Why a single number is useful

Year 12 students in Australia take hundreds of different subjects in thousands of combinations. Victoria alone has reported more than 24,000 unique study combinations in a single year. Universities need a way to compare students fairly across all those combinations. The ATAR does that by converting each student’s results into a single rank that means the same thing whatever subjects were studied. It is a practical solution to a genuinely difficult problem.

A rank, not a mark

This is the single most important idea about the ATAR, so it is worth being clear. Your ATAR is not a score out of 100. It is a percentile rank.

An ATAR of 80.00 does not mean you got 80% on your exams. It means you finished ahead of about 80% of your age group. An ATAR of 95.00 means you finished in the top 5%. An ATAR of 99.95, the highest possible, means you are in the top handful of students in the state.

Because it is a rank, only a fixed share of students can reach each level. Roughly 1% of students get an ATAR of 99 or above, no matter how strong the year group is. That is by design. The ATAR is built to sort students into order, not to reward a fixed score.

This also means your ATAR depends on everyone else, not just on you. You could score exactly the same marks in two different years and get slightly different ATARs, because the students you are ranked against are different each time.

Reading a rank correctly

Here is a simple way to read any ATAR. Take the number and say: “I performed better than about this percentage of my age group in my state.” An ATAR of 65 means better than about 65 per cent. An ATAR of 90 means better than about 90 per cent, or in the top 10 per cent. An ATAR of 99 means better than about 99 per cent, or in the top 1 per cent.

The phrase “of my age group” matters. The ATAR does not compare you only with your classmates, your school or even only with students who received an ATAR. It compares you with everyone of a similar age in your state, including young people who left school early or did not complete enough eligible subjects. That is why the shares above refer to the whole age group.

Why only a fixed share can reach each level

Because the ATAR describes position within the age group, the share of the age group at or above each ATAR is fixed by definition. About 10 per cent of the age group sits at 90 or above, about 1 per cent at 99 or above, and so on. A particularly strong year does not create more 99s; it simply means the students who reach 99 had to perform even more strongly.

Among students who actually receive an ATAR, the shares are higher, because ATAR recipients are a subset of the age group who tend to be academically stronger on average. In NSW in 2025, UAC reported that about 17.3 per cent of ATAR recipients had an ATAR of 90 or above, even though an ATAR of 90 represents the top 10 per cent of the age group.

Why your ATAR depends on other students

Two students with identical marks in different years can receive slightly different ATARs because the students they are compared with are different. In practice, the age group in each state is large and behaves fairly consistently, so the differences are usually small. But they exist, and they are the main reason no one can calculate your exact ATAR before results are released.

How a percentile rank works, with examples

The ATAR works in a similar way to a percentile rank. If percentiles are unfamiliar, a few examples make the idea concrete.

A simple illustration

Imagine 1,000 people lined up in order of their results, with the lowest at one end and the highest at the other. A person whose result is higher than 800 of the others is at roughly the 80th percentile. A person whose result is higher than 950 of the others is at roughly the 95th percentile. The percentile tells you the share of the group below you, not your score.

The ATAR applies the same idea to your state’s age group. If your aggregate is higher than that of about 80 per cent of your age group, your ATAR is about 80. If it is higher than that of about 95 per cent, your ATAR is about 95.

Why percentiles bunch in the middle

Results across a large group tend to cluster around the middle and thin out at the extremes. That has an important consequence. In the crowded middle, a small improvement in your results can move you past many people, so your percentile can rise noticeably. Near the top, where people are more spread out, the same improvement moves you past fewer people, so your percentile changes less in absolute terms, although each step there separates a very small group.

Who you are ranked against: the age group

One of the least understood features of the ATAR is the group it compares you with. Many students assume they are ranked only against other Year 12 students who receive an ATAR. In fact, the ATAR is designed to reflect your position relative to your whole age group in your state.

Why the whole age group?

Using the whole age group means the ATAR has a stable meaning from year to year. If the ATAR were based only on students who receive one, its meaning would shift whenever more or fewer students chose an ATAR pathway. By anchoring it to the age group, an ATAR of 80 means roughly the same position in the population each year, whatever the participation rate.

What this means for typical ATARs

Because many young people in the age group do not receive an ATAR, and those who do tend to be academically stronger on average, the typical ATAR among recipients is well above 50. UAC reported a median ATAR of 70.75 in NSW in 2025, and VTAC reported an average ATAR of 69.52 in Victoria in 2024. An ATAR around 70 is therefore a typical result for a student who receives one, not a below-average one.

What this means for comparisons

When you read statistics about ATARs, check whether they describe the whole age group or only ATAR recipients. An ATAR of 80 places you in the top 20 per cent of your age group, but around a third of NSW ATAR recipients achieved 80 or above in 2025. Both statements are correct; they describe different groups.

Not everyone receives an ATAR

A December 2025 report from the Mitchell Institute at Victoria University estimated that around 64 per cent of Year 12 students nationally receive an ATAR. In NSW in 2025, 68,896 students were awarded the HSC and 60,432 received an ATAR. Students who do not receive an ATAR often follow vocational or other pathways, and many go on to university later through other routes.

Reading an ATAR: six quick examples

  • Sami receives 72.40. He performed better than about 72 per cent of his age group in his state. Among students who received an ATAR, he is close to the middle.
  • Priya receives 88.15. She is in roughly the top 12 per cent of her age group. With an adjustment of a few points for a course that offers one, her selection rank for that course is higher still.
  • Tom receives 61.00. He performed better than about 61 per cent of his age group. Several courses he is interested in have thresholds near his selection rank, and he also looks at a pathway program for a course with a higher threshold.
  • Mei receives 99.10. She is in roughly the top 1 per cent of her age group. For the competitive course she wants, her interview and admissions test will matter as much as her ATAR.
  • Jordan receives “30.00 or less”. His exact ATAR is not reported. He focuses on pathway programs and enabling courses that lead into the degree he wants.
  • Alex does not receive an ATAR. She completed her senior certificate through a vocational pathway and plans to enter university later through a TAFE diploma.

Why the ATAR is out of 99.95, not 100

The odd-looking maximum of 99.95 surprises people. It comes from how the rank is reported. ATARs are given in steps of 0.05, from 0.00 up to 99.95. There is no 100.00, because the very top band is 99.95.

At the other end, most admissions centres do not report an exact number below 30.00. Instead they show “less than 30”. This avoids labelling students with very low ranks, while still letting universities see who has cleared the levels they care about.

The steps of 0.05 also tell you something. In the crowded middle of the scale, a small difference in your marks can move your ATAR by many of those steps, because so many students are packed close together. Near the very top, where students are more spread out, the same mark difference usually moves your ATAR by fewer steps, but each step there separates only a small number of students.

How many steps are on the scale?

Because ATARs are reported in steps of 0.05 from 0.00 to 99.95, the scale contains 2,000 possible values. Queensland’s admissions centre describes the ATAR in exactly these terms, as 2,000 bands. Each band represents roughly the same share of the age group, so the bands near the top separate very small numbers of students. In NSW in 2025, each of the top few bands, from 99.95 down to 99.75, held around 50 students.

Why the scale stops at 99.95

The top value is 99.95 because the scale is built from bands of 0.05, and the highest band is reported as 99.95. The scale therefore never shows 100.00. Reaching 99.95 means your position was in the top 0.05 per cent of your age group, not that your marks were perfect.

How low ATARs are reported

Admissions centres do not usually report exact ATARs below 30. In Queensland, they are reported as “30.00 or less”. In South Australia, ATARs below 30.00 are not shown on the Tertiary Entrance Statement. Other states use similar conventions. This practice does not affect anyone’s ability to apply for courses with higher thresholds, and it avoids attaching a precise label to results that rarely matter for selection.

Why two decimal places?

The two decimal places reflect the 0.05 steps, not a claim of extreme precision about your ability. They allow universities to distinguish between students near course thresholds, which is especially important for competitive courses where many applicants have similar ranks.

Why Australia uses a rank rather than a mark

Many students wonder why the ATAR is a rank at all. Why not simply average students’ marks? There are good reasons for the design, along with some trade-offs.

Different subjects, different scales

Marks in different subjects are not directly comparable, because each subject has different content, assessment and students. A rank built from scaled results allows a fair comparison across thousands of combinations of subjects.

A stable meaning over time

Average marks can drift from year to year as exams and marking change. Because the ATAR represents a position within the age group, an ATAR of 80 means roughly the same thing each year. That helps universities compare applicants from different years, including students who apply after a gap year.

Fine distinctions where they matter

Universities often need to choose between many applicants with similar results. A rank reported in steps of 0.05 allows fine distinctions near course thresholds.

The trade-offs

A rank depends on other students, which can feel uncomfortable, and it can be hard to understand. It also reduces a student’s achievement to a single number, which is why universities use adjustment factors, additional criteria and alternative pathways alongside it.

ATAR versus marks versus GPA

Students often mix up three different things: their marks, their ATAR, and a GPA. They are not the same, and it helps to keep them apart.

Your marks are how you did in a single subject, like 85 in Biology. Your ATAR is a rank that combines your scaled marks across subjects and places you against your whole age group. A GPA (grade point average) is something you get later, at university, by averaging your course grades.

So the ATAR sits between school and university. It takes your school marks, turns them into a single national rank, and uses that rank to help you get in. Once you are at university, your GPA takes over, and your ATAR stops mattering.

Marks and the ATAR are on different scales

A subject mark and an ATAR can look similar, but they measure different things on different scales. UAC explains that most HSC courses have a median mark above 75, while the median ATAR is usually around 70, and that students with marks around 70 typically receive ATARs in the 50s, while marks around 80 typically lead to an ATAR around 80. So a student who averages 75 in their HSC courses has not “got a 75 ATAR”; their ATAR depends on how their scaled marks compare across the whole state.

GPA and WAM take over at university

Once you start university, your results are recorded as grades in each unit, and universities combine them into a grade point average or a weighted average mark. These measures matter for internal transfers, scholarships, honours and some graduate programs. Your ATAR becomes much less relevant once you have university results. ATAR versus selection rank

Universities rarely use your ATAR on its own. They use your selection rank, which is your ATAR plus any adjustment factors you are eligible for, for a particular course at a particular institution. Understanding the difference is essential when you compare yourself with course thresholds.

What adjustment factors are

Adjustment factors, often called bonus points, are added to your ATAR to create your selection rank. They may be awarded for strong results in particular subjects, for living or studying in particular areas, for experiencing educational disadvantage, for elite sporting or artistic performance, or through school recommendation schemes. Each institution sets its own schemes and limits.

One ATAR, many selection ranks

You receive one ATAR, but you can have a different selection rank for each course you apply for, depending on which adjustments each institution offers and which ones you are eligible for. A student with an ATAR of 82 might have a selection rank of 82 for one course and 87 for another.

Course thresholds are usually selection ranks

The figures often called cut-offs are usually lowest selection ranks: the lowest selection rank to which an offer was made in a particular round. Because they include adjustment factors, some students who received offers had ATARs below the published figure. When you compare yourself with a course, compare your likely selection rank, not just your ATAR.

Some adjustments need an application

Some adjustment factors are applied automatically, while others, particularly equity schemes, require an application with its own deadline. If you think you may be eligible, check each scheme well before results day. Our guides to selection rank versus ATAR and ATAR bonus points explain the main schemes, and our selection rank calculator can help you estimate yours.

Your Year 12 results versus your ATAR

On results day, most students receive two kinds of information: their subject results from their curriculum authority and their ATAR from their admissions centre. The two look different in every state, and it helps to know what each one tells you.

NSW: HSC marks and bands

Each HSC course result is reported as a mark, usually out of 100, and a performance band, with Band 6 the highest in most courses. Your HSC mark combines your moderated assessment mark and your exam mark in equal parts. Your ATAR is calculated separately by UAC from scaled marks in your best 10 units, including 2 units of English. A strong set of bands usually goes with a strong ATAR, but there is no fixed conversion between them.

Victoria: study scores

Each VCE study is reported with a study score from 0 to 50, showing your position in that study across the state. The mean is set at 30. Your ATAR is calculated by VTAC from your scaled study scores, using your English study, your next three best studies and 10 per cent of your fifth and sixth.

Queensland: subject results

Each General subject produces a result that combines internal assessment and external assessment. Your ATAR is calculated by QTAC from the best five scaled results, which may include one Applied subject or eligible VET qualification.

Western Australia: combined scores and scaled scores

Each ATAR course produces a combined score from your moderated school mark and your exam mark. TISC scales those scores and calculates your TEA and ATAR, adding bonuses for a language other than English and for Mathematics Methods and Specialist.

South Australia and the NT: grades and scaled scores

Stage 2 subjects are reported as grades from A+ to E-. SATAC converts subject results to scaled scores, builds your university aggregate from your best 90 credits and calculates your ATAR, which appears on your Tertiary Entrance Statement.

Tasmania: course scores and the TE Score

Your eligible Level 3 and Level 4 course results contribute to your TE Score, which is converted to an ATAR. Courses completed over up to two years can count.

ACT: course scores and scaled scores

ACT students receive results for each course through the ACT’s own system, and their ATAR is based on their best three major scaled scores plus 60 per cent of their next major or minor.

Why the two are different

Subject results describe how you performed in each subject. The ATAR describes your overall position relative to your age group after your results have been placed on a common scale and combined. It is entirely possible to have strong subject results and an ATAR that seems lower than expected, or modest-looking marks and a strong ATAR, depending on how your subjects scaled and how your results combined.

Who works out your ATAR?

You sit Year 12 with your state’s school authority, but your ATAR is calculated by a separate body called a tertiary admissions centre. Which one depends on where you study:

  • NSW and ACT — UAC (the Universities Admissions Centre).
  • Victoria — VTAC.
  • Queensland — QTAC.
  • South Australia and the NT — SATAC.
  • Western Australia — TISC.
  • Tasmania — worked out through UTAS with TASC.

These centres all produce the same thing: an ATAR on the same 0.00 to 99.95 scale. The methods differ slightly from state to state, but the result means the same everywhere. That is what lets a student in Perth and a student in Sydney be compared for the same course.

It also means your school does not set your ATAR, and neither do the universities. A separate, state-level body does the sums, using the same rules for every student.

Two kinds of organisation, two different jobs

Two kinds of organisation are involved in producing your ATAR, and it helps to know which does what. Curriculum and assessment authorities set the senior subjects, run the exams and issue your subject results and certificate. Tertiary admissions centres, or in some states a university or related body, take those results, scale them, build your aggregate and calculate your ATAR. Admissions centres also process university applications and coordinate offers.

Why a separate body calculates the ATAR

Keeping the ATAR calculation separate from schools and individual universities means every student is ranked using the same rules, by an organisation that has no stake in any particular school’s results. It also means universities across the country can rely on ATARs from any state.

What admissions centres do besides the ATAR

Most admissions centres also run the application and preference system for many universities in their state, publish course information and thresholds, administer some adjustment and equity schemes, and release offers in rounds. In Tasmania, students usually apply directly to the University of Tasmania. Our guides to UAC, VTAC, QTAC, TISC and SATAC explain each one.

Where your ATAR actually comes from

Your ATAR is not your raw marks. It is built from your scaled results. Scaling is the step that adjusts each subject so that a result in one subject means the same as the same result in another.

The short version of the process is this. First, your results in each subject are scaled, based on how strong the group taking that subject was. Then your best scaled results are added together into an aggregate. Finally, that aggregate is ranked against everyone else and turned into your ATAR.

Scaling is why students talk so much about which subjects “scale well”. A subject scales up when the students taking it are strong across all their subjects, and down when the group is broader. It is not that hard subjects get a bonus — it is that strong cohorts lift a subject. We explain the full process in how is ATAR calculated.

The three steps in more detail

Step 1: Your subject results. Your curriculum authority produces a result for each subject, usually combining school assessment and external assessment. In most states, school assessment is moderated so that marks from different schools can be compared. In NSW, each HSC mark combines your moderated assessment mark and your exam mark in equal parts. In Western Australia, each ATAR course combines a moderated school mark and an exam mark equally. In South Australia and the NT, Stage 2 subjects are 70 per cent school assessment and 30 per cent external assessment. In Queensland, external assessment contributes 25 per cent of most General subjects and 50 per cent of mathematics and science subjects. In Victoria, each study score combines school-assessed coursework and examinations.

Step 2: Scaling. The admissions centre adjusts each subject’s results so that they sit on a common scale. Scaling looks at how the students in each subject perform across all their subjects. A subject whose students are strong overall is placed relatively high; a subject whose students are less strong overall is placed lower. Scaling also varies along the range of results within each subject.

Step 3: The aggregate and the rank. Your best scaled results are combined into an aggregate under your state’s rules. All aggregates are then ranked, and your position is expressed as an ATAR relative to your age group.

Why scaling exists

Without scaling, students would gain or lose simply because of which subjects they chose. A mark of 80 in a subject taken by a very strong group of students represents a different level of competition from a mark of 80 in a subject with a broader group. Scaling evens this out so that students can choose subjects based on their interests and strengths. Our guides to ATAR scaling and scaling myths go further.

Why only your best results count

Most states count only a set number of your best results, with some rules about which subjects must be included. That design protects students from being penalised for a single weaker subject, and it gives students some freedom to take a subject they enjoy without worrying that a modest result in it will drag down their ATAR.

Scaling in more depth

Scaling is the part of the ATAR that causes the most confusion, so it is worth a closer look. The aim is simple: to make results in different subjects comparable. The way each state achieves that aim differs in its details.

The basic idea

Imagine two subjects. In the first, the students are, on average, very strong across all their subjects. In the second, the students are a broader group. A mark of 80 in the first subject reflects success against tougher competition than a mark of 80 in the second. Scaling adjusts results so that a given scaled result represents the same level of performance relative to the whole group of students, whichever subject it comes from.

Scaling uses your other subjects

To judge the strength of each subject’s students, scaling looks at how those students performed in their other subjects. That is why it is sometimes described as an inter-subject process. It does not depend on how difficult the subject’s content or exam was; it depends on who took it.

Scaling is not a single number

Scaling adjusts results along the whole range of each subject. A subject might lift results noticeably at the top and much less in the middle, or the reverse. VTAC notes, for example, that the scaling adjustment for a study differs at each study score. That is why a single headline figure, such as “this subject adds five points”, can be misleading for any particular student.

How the states approach scaling

  • NSW: UAC scales HSC marks so that each course’s scaled marks reflect the strength of its students. Scaled marks are reported per unit, out of 50.
  • Victoria: VTAC scales study scores, and applies a policy adjustment to the scaling of languages.
  • Queensland: QTAC uses an iterative inter-subject scaling process across all eligible students’ results. Its 2025 data showed that no subject was scaled up at every point in its range.
  • Western Australia: TISC uses a process it describes as average marks scaling, after school marks have been moderated and combined with exam marks.
  • South Australia and the NT: SATAC converts subject results into scaled scores but does not publish scaled scores for each subject, and advises students not to choose subjects based on historical scaling.
  • Tasmania and the ACT: local processes produce the scores used to calculate the ATAR.

What scaling means for students

For most students, scaling is best thought of as a background fairness mechanism. It allows you to choose subjects you are good at and interested in without being penalised for that choice. Trying to outguess scaling by choosing subjects you are less suited to usually backfires, because your result within each subject matters more than how the subject scales.

Moderation: making school marks comparable

Most states combine school assessment with external assessment. Because schools set and mark their own tasks, their marks are not directly comparable. Moderation is the process that adjusts school marks so they can be compared fairly across schools.

How moderation usually works

In NSW, Victoria and Western Australia, school marks are adjusted using the external exam results of the same group of students. The order of students within each school group is generally kept, but the size of the marks can change to match how the group performed in the exam. In Queensland, internal assessment is quality assured through endorsement and confirmation, in which the QCAA reviews assessment instruments and samples of student work, and the QCAA has stated that external assessment results do not scale internal results. In South Australia and the NT, school assessment for Stage 2 subjects is moderated to ensure grading is consistent with each subject’s performance standards.

Why it matters to you

Moderation means that your position within your school group is important. If you rank highly in your school group, moderation will generally keep you there, even if the size of your marks changes. It also means that raw school marks are not final, which is one reason ATAR estimates before results day can only ever be approximate.

How each state builds the ATAR

Every state produces an ATAR on the same scale, but each builds the aggregate in its own way. Here is a summary of the main rules. Our guide to how the ATAR is calculated and the state guides linked below go into more detail.

NSW

Your ATAR is based on an aggregate of scaled marks in 10 units of ATAR courses: your best 2 units of English plus your best 8 units from your remaining courses, with no more than 2 units of Category B courses included. Scaled marks are out of 50 per unit, so the aggregate is out of 500. See how the NSW ATAR is calculated.

Victoria

Your aggregate is built from your best scaled study score in the English group plus your next three best scaled study scores, which together form your primary four, plus 10 per cent of your fifth and sixth scaled study scores, or an eligible VET increment. The aggregate can range from zero to over 210. VTAC applies a policy adjustment to the scaling of languages, and there are rules about combinations such as multiple mathematics studies. See how the Victorian ATAR is calculated.

Queensland

Your Tertiary Entrance Aggregate is the sum of your best five scaled results, from General subjects or from four General subjects plus one Applied subject or one eligible VET qualification at Certificate III or higher. The maximum is 500. You must also achieve a C or better in an English subject. See how the Queensland ATAR is calculated.

Western Australia

Your Tertiary Entrance Aggregate is the sum of your best four scaled scores, plus 10 per cent of your best scaled score in a language other than English, 10 per cent of your scaled score in Mathematics Methods and 10 per cent of your scaled score in Mathematics Specialist. The bonuses apply even if those courses are not in your best four, and the maximum TEA is 430. See how the WA ATAR is calculated.

South Australia and the Northern Territory

Your university aggregate is calculated from the scaled scores of your best 90 credits of Stage 2 study: at least 60 credits from 20-credit Tertiary Admission Subjects, or valid pairs of 10-credit subjects, and a flexible option that can include up to 20 credits of Recognised Studies. Scaled scores are out of 20 for 20-credit subjects and 10 for 10-credit subjects, so the aggregate is out of 90. See how the SA ATAR is calculated and the NT calculation.

Tasmania

From 2025, your TE Score is built from your best eligible Level 3 and Level 4 course scores over up to two years, with each course worth up to 15 points and a total of 60 to 75 points from four or five courses. The TE Score is then converted to an ATAR. See how the Tasmanian ATAR is calculated.

ACT

Your ATAR is based on the sum of your best three major scaled scores plus 60 per cent of your next best major or minor. Scaled scores come from the ACT’s own process for comparing results across colleges and courses. See how the ACT ATAR is calculated.

Who can get an ATAR

Not every Year 12 student is eligible for an ATAR. Each state sets requirements about how many eligible subjects you must complete and which ones must be included. Here are the main requirements, in brief. Always check your admissions centre’s current rules.

NSW

You must satisfactorily complete at least 10 units of ATAR courses, including at least 8 units of Category A courses, 2 units of English, three Board Developed courses of 2 units or more, and courses from at least four subject areas. UAC also explains that some institutions allow certain applicants who complete between 5 and 9 units of ATAR courses, including at least 1 unit of English, to use a Limited ATAR, calculated from their best 5 units.

Victoria

You need study scores in at least four eligible studies, including one from the English group, and you must satisfy VTAC’s other requirements. Your aggregate can include up to six results.

Queensland

You need five General subjects, or four General subjects plus one Applied subject or one eligible VET qualification at Certificate III or higher, and a C or better in an English subject.

Western Australia

Your ATAR is calculated from scaled scores in ATAR courses, and a minimum of four ATAR courses is needed. Universities in Western Australia also generally require the WACE and evidence of English competence for admission.

South Australia and the NT

You must complete the SACE or NTCET and at least 90 credits of eligible Stage 2 study under SATAC’s rules, with at least 60 credits from 20-credit Tertiary Admission Subjects and up to 20 credits of Recognised Studies, completed within a limited number of attempts.

Tasmania

You need the TCE and a Satisfactory Achievement or better in at least four eligible Level 3 or Level 4 courses.

ACT

You must meet the Board of Senior Secondary Studies’ requirements for a Tertiary Entrance Statement, which include completing a sufficient package of tertiary courses.

Check early
Eligibility depends on the subjects you choose in Year 10 and Year 11. If you want to keep the ATAR option open, check your state’s requirements before you finalise your subjects, and talk to your school if you are unsure.

How your subject choices shape your ATAR

The subjects you choose in Year 10 and Year 11 shape your ATAR in three ways: they determine whether you are eligible, they determine which results can count, and they influence how well you perform. Understanding all three helps you choose wisely.

Eligibility

Each state requires a minimum pattern of eligible subjects. In NSW, for example, you need at least 10 units of ATAR courses, including at least 8 units of Category A courses and 2 units of English. In Queensland, you need five General subjects or four General subjects plus one Applied subject or eligible VET qualification. If your subjects do not meet these requirements, you will not receive an ATAR.

Which results count

Your state’s rules decide which of your results are included. English must count in NSW and Victoria. In Queensland, only your best five count, so a sixth subject can act as a buffer. In Western Australia, your best four count, with bonuses for some subjects. Knowing these rules helps you see which subjects matter most and how much room you have for a weaker result.

How well you perform

The most important factor in your ATAR is how well you perform relative to other students in each subject. A strong result in a subject you are good at usually contributes more than a weak result in a subject that tends to scale well. That is why the best general advice is to choose subjects you are good at and enjoy, while meeting the prerequisites for courses you are interested in.

The role of scaling in subject choice

Scaling is often the first thing students think about when choosing subjects, but it should usually be the last. Scaling reflects the strength of each subject’s students, it changes from year to year, and its effect varies along the range of results. It can be a reasonable tie-breaker between two subjects you are equally strong in, but it is a poor reason to take a subject you dislike or struggle with. Our guides to the highest scaling subjects and whether hard subjects lead to a higher ATAR explore this further.

Prerequisites first

Before you finalise your subjects, check the prerequisites and assumed knowledge for courses you might want to study. A prerequisite is a firm requirement, and missing one can close a door regardless of your ATAR.

A closer look at NSW: units, categories and extension courses

NSW has the largest number of ATAR recipients in the country, and its rules include some details that often confuse students.

Units, not subjects

NSW counts units rather than subjects. Most HSC courses are 2 units, and extension courses are usually 1 unit. Your ATAR is based on 10 units: your best 2 units of English plus your best 8 units from your remaining courses. If you take 12 units, the 2 weakest units outside English do not count.

Category A and Category B

HSC courses that can count towards the ATAR are classified as Category A or Category B. To be eligible for an ATAR, you need at least 8 units of Category A courses. No more than 2 units of Category B courses can be included in your ATAR calculation. Category B courses are typically vocational courses.

Subject areas

Eligibility also requires courses from at least four subject areas. A subject area is a broader field, such as mathematics or English, which may contain several courses. A student who takes several courses in the same area, such as multiple mathematics and English courses, needs to make sure they still meet the four-subject-area requirement.

Extension courses

Extension courses are reported out of 50 and count as single units. They often attract strong cohorts, and they can contribute well to an aggregate if your results are strong. They also add workload, so weigh their benefit against the time they take.

English Studies and the HSC exam

NESA notes that English Studies only counts towards the ATAR if the student sits the optional HSC exam. Students taking English Studies who want an ATAR should check this requirement carefully.

Our guides to how the NSW ATAR is calculated, HSC scaling and converting HSC marks to an ATAR go into more detail.

A closer look at Victoria: study scores and increments

Victoria’s system is built around study scores, and several features make it different from other states.

Study scores are already ranks

A VCE study score, from 0 to 50, shows your position within a study across the state. The VCAA sets the mean at 30 with a standard deviation of about 7, so roughly two-thirds of students in a study receive between about 23 and 37. A study score of 40 or more places a student well above the middle of the study.

The English group

One of your primary four must come from the English group of studies. VTAC uses your best scaled score from that group, which may be English, English as an Additional Language, English Language or Literature.

Increments

Your fifth and sixth studies contribute 10 per cent of their scaled study scores. Alternatively, an eligible VET program without a study score can contribute an increment. Increments allow students who take more studies to gain a little extra, without those studies counting in full.

Languages

VTAC applies a policy adjustment to the scaling of languages, which affects the initial scaled average of each language study. It is not a fixed number of points added to the ATAR, and its effect depends on each student’s result and combination.

A closer look at the other states and territories

Queensland: General, Applied and VET

Queensland’s ATAR is built from the best five scaled results. Most students use five General subjects, but one of the five can be an Applied subject or an eligible VET qualification at Certificate III or higher. General subjects have three internal assessments and one external assessment; Applied subjects are assessed internally. Because QTAC’s scaling is an iterative process across all eligible students, the position of each subject is worked out afresh every year. Students need a C or better in an English subject to be eligible, but English only counts towards the TEA if it is among their best five. Our guides to how the Queensland ATAR is calculated and QCE scaling go further.

Western Australia: the bonuses

Western Australia is the only state where bonuses for particular subjects are built directly into the aggregate used to calculate the ATAR. Students receive 10 per cent of their best scaled score in a language other than English, 10 per cent of their scaled score in Mathematics Methods and 10 per cent of their scaled score in Mathematics Specialist, whether or not those courses are among their best four. That makes the maximum TEA 430 rather than 400. Our guides to how the WA ATAR is calculated and WACE scaling explain more.

South Australia and the NT: the 90 credits

In South Australia and the NT, the university aggregate is built from the scaled scores of your best 90 credits of Stage 2 study. At least 60 of those credits must come from 20-credit Tertiary Admission Subjects, or valid pairs of 10-credit subjects counted in place of a 20-credit subject. The remaining 30 credits form a flexible option, which can include up to 20 credits of Recognised Studies, such as some VET. Scaled scores are out of 20 for each 20-credit subject and out of 10 for each 10-credit subject, so the aggregate is out of 90. SATAC’s rules also limit how many attempts can be used. Our guides to SACE scaling and SATAC explain the details.

Tasmania: two years of courses

From 2025, the Tasmanian TE Score is built from a student’s best eligible Level 3 and Level 4 course scores across up to two years, with each full course worth up to 15 points and a total of 60 to 75 points from four or five courses. Because courses completed in Year 11 can count, Tasmanian students have more of their result in hand before their final year than students in most other states. The University of Tasmania explains that an ATAR of 80 means a TE Score at or above that of 80 per cent of Tasmanian Year 12 school leavers. Our guides to TCE scaling and TASC and UTAS go further.

ACT: majors and minors

In the ACT, students study courses packaged as majors and minors. The ATAR is based on the best three major scaled scores plus 60 per cent of the next major or minor. Because the ACT has no statewide external exams in the same form as other states, its scaling process compares results across colleges in its own way. In 2025, the median ATAR among ACT recipients was 78.35. Our guide to UAC for ACT students explains how ACT applications work.

A full worked example

Numbers make this clearer. Imagine a student who takes five subjects and, after scaling, ends up with these scaled results out of 50: English 38, Maths 40, Biology 42, Business 35 and Geography 30.

The admissions centre takes the student’s best results and adds them into an aggregate. The strongest subjects — Biology, Maths and English here — do most of the work. The weakest, Geography, barely moves the total, and might not count at all once the better results are in.

That aggregate is then lined up against every other student’s aggregate in the state. If this student’s aggregate sits higher than 87% of them, their ATAR is about 87.00. Notice what happened: the student did not need to top any single subject. Consistent, strong results across a few subjects produced a high rank.

This is why a spare subject is useful. If one subject goes badly, your best results still count, and the weak one quietly drops out of your aggregate.

The same idea in four states

The worked example above shows the general principle. Because each state builds the aggregate differently, here is how the same idea plays out in four systems. The figures for Victoria, Queensland and NSW are invented for illustration; the Western Australian figures come from a published worked example of a TEA calculation.

Victoria

Imagine a student with scaled study scores of 32 in English, 38 in Mathematical Methods, 36 in Chemistry, 31 in Legal Studies, 30 in Psychology and 27 in Health and Human Development. Their primary four are English (32), Methods (38), Chemistry (36) and Legal Studies (31), which total 137. Their fifth and sixth studies add 10 per cent each: 3.0 for Psychology and 2.7 for Health and Human Development. The aggregate is 142.7, which is then converted to an ATAR using that year’s table.

Western Australia

In the published example, a student’s best four scaled scores are Physics 67.6, Italian 66.5, English 55.4 and Modern History 41.5, which add to 231.0. The student receives 10 per cent of their Italian scaled score, 6.7, as a language bonus, and a mathematics bonus of 4.1, giving a TEA of 241.8. The mathematics course is not among the best four, but its bonus still counts.

Queensland

Imagine a student with six General subjects. After scaling, their five best results are added together to form their TEA, out of a maximum of 500. Their sixth result does not count, but it would replace a weaker result if one of the other five were lower. The student also needs a C or better in an English subject to be eligible.

NSW

Imagine a student taking 12 units, including English Advanced. Their best 2 units of English are included automatically. Their best 8 units from their remaining courses are then added, with no more than 2 units of Category B courses. Two units are left over and do not count. The aggregate, out of 500, is ranked to produce the ATAR.

What all four have in common

In every state, the ATAR rewards strong results across a set of subjects, applies some rules about which subjects must or may count, and turns the total into a rank. No state requires you to top any single subject. Consistent, strong results in the subjects that count are what produce a high ATAR.

Following one student from results to an offer

To see how all the pieces fit together, follow one illustrative NSW student, Ella, from her final exams to a university offer. The figures are invented to show the process, not to predict any real result.

1. Ella’s HSC marks

Ella takes English Advanced, Mathematics Advanced, Biology, Chemistry, Legal Studies and Business Studies, a total of 12 units. For each course, NESA combines her moderated school assessment mark and her exam mark in equal parts to produce her HSC mark. Her school group performed a little more strongly in the Chemistry exam than its school marks suggested, so her moderated Chemistry assessment mark is slightly higher than her raw school mark.

2. Scaling

UAC scales each of Ella’s HSC marks, based on how the students in each course performed across all their courses. Her scaled marks are expressed per unit, out of 50. Some of her courses scale a little above her HSC marks at her level of result, and some a little below.

3. Her aggregate

UAC adds the scaled marks for her 2 units of English and her best 8 units from her other courses. One of her 2-unit courses, Business Studies, has the lowest scaled mark and is left out. Her aggregate is out of 500.

4. Her ATAR

UAC ranks Ella’s aggregate against all aggregates and expresses her position relative to her age group. Her ATAR is 84.35, meaning she performed better than about 84 per cent of her age group in NSW.

5. Her selection ranks

Ella is eligible for a subject adjustment at one university for her strong Chemistry result, and for a regional adjustment at another. For her first-preference course, her selection rank is 86.35; for her second preference at another institution, it is 84.35, because that institution does not offer the adjustments she is eligible for.

6. Her offer

Her first-preference course’s lowest selection rank in the previous year was 85.50. In Ella’s year, the threshold for the first ATAR-based round is a little higher, but her selection rank of 86.35 is still above it, and she meets the course’s assumed knowledge. She receives an offer for her first preference in the first round, accepts it before the deadline, and starts university the following year.

What Ella’s story shows

Ella’s ATAR came from consistent results across her best 10 units, not from topping any one course. Her weakest course did not count. Her selection rank, not her ATAR alone, decided her offer. And she listed her genuine first choice first, so she received the offer she wanted most.

What counts as a good ATAR?

Because the ATAR is a rank, a “good” one depends on what you want to do with it. The median ATAR for students who receive one sits around 70. So an ATAR of 70 is genuinely middle of the pack, not a poor result.

As a rough guide, an ATAR of 80 or above opens most university courses. An ATAR of 90 or above is competitive for in-demand degrees. The most competitive courses, such as medicine and law at leading universities, often ask for 95 and above. We cover this in detail in what is a good ATAR.

The most useful way to think about it is to work backwards. Find the course you want, look up its cut-off, and aim a little above it. That turns a vague goal into a clear target.

What the latest figures show

Published data from admissions centres helps put any ATAR in context. In NSW in 2025, UAC reported a median ATAR of 70.75, with about 51.3 per cent of ATAR recipients at 70 or above, 34.6 per cent at 80 or above and 17.3 per cent at 90 or above. In Victoria, VTAC reported an average ATAR of 69.52 in 2024. In the ACT in 2025, the median ATAR was 78.35. These figures describe students who received an ATAR, not the whole age group.

Good depends on the goal

A good ATAR for one student may be a disappointing one for another, because their goals differ. A student aiming for a course with a threshold of 70 who receives 76 has done very well. A student aiming for a course with a threshold of 95 who receives 93 may need to consider adjustment factors or a pathway. The most useful definition of a good ATAR is one that gets you where you want to go.

Look at selection ranks, not just ATARs

When you compare your ATAR with a course, remember that universities use selection ranks, which include adjustment factors. A student whose ATAR is a little below a course’s lowest selection rank may still receive an offer if they are eligible for adjustments.

Reading ATAR statistics correctly

ATAR statistics appear in news reports, school newsletters and university brochures. They are accurate, but they are easy to misread. Three checks help.

Who is being counted?

Some figures describe the whole age group, and others only students who received an ATAR. An ATAR of 90 represents the top 10 per cent of the age group, but in NSW in 2025 about 17.3 per cent of ATAR recipients had 90 or above. Both statements are correct.

Median or average?

A median is the middle value; an average is the total divided by the number of students. For ATARs, the two are usually similar but not identical. UAC reported a median of 70.75 in NSW in 2025, while VTAC reported an average of 69.52 in Victoria in 2024.

Threshold, median or profile?

For courses, a lowest selection rank is the lowest rank that received an offer in a round. A course median is the middle of the group offered places, so half had lower ranks. Universities’ ATAR profiles often show both, along with the highest rank. Treating a median as a minimum can lead you to remove courses you could have been offered.

School results

Schools sometimes publish figures such as their median ATAR or the share of students above 90. These describe a group in a particular year, not what any individual student will receive. Your ATAR reflects your own results under the same rules as every other student.

What different ATARs look like

It can help to see what kinds of results tend to sit behind different ATARs. The profiles below are general descriptions, not predictions. Your own ATAR depends on your subjects, how they scale and how your results combine.

Around 50

An ATAR around 50 places a student ahead of about half of their age group. In NSW, UAC explains that students with HSC marks around 70 typically receive ATARs in the 50s, which surprises many students who assume marks and ATARs are on the same scale. A student at this level has completed an ATAR pattern of study and has a range of options, including many pathway programs and some courses with lower thresholds.

Around 70

An ATAR around 70 is close to the typical result among ATAR recipients. Students at this level usually have solid results across their subjects. Many courses at many institutions have thresholds in this region, and adjustment factors can widen the options further.

Around 80

An ATAR around 80 places a student in roughly the top 20 per cent of their age group. In NSW, UAC explains that students with marks around 80 typically receive an ATAR around 80. Students at this level generally have strong results in most of their subjects.

Around 90

An ATAR around 90 places a student in roughly the top 10 per cent of their age group. Students at this level usually have strong results across all the subjects that count, often in subjects that scale well at their level. Many in-demand courses are within reach.

Around 99

An ATAR around 99 places a student in roughly the top 1 per cent of their age group. Students at this level have outstanding results across their counted subjects. For the most competitive courses, other criteria such as interviews and admissions tests often matter as much as the ATAR at this level.

Remember
These descriptions are general. Two students with similar marks can receive different ATARs because their subjects scale differently and their results combine differently. A calculator built for your state is the best way to see where your own results might land.

What different ATARs can open

Students often want to know which courses a particular ATAR will open. The honest answer is that it depends on the institution, the campus, the year, your adjustment factors and every other requirement of the course. But some general principles hold.

Thresholds vary widely

The same type of course can have very different thresholds at different institutions, and sometimes at different campuses of the same institution. A course in a popular field at a metropolitan campus may have a much higher threshold than a similar course elsewhere. Thresholds also move from year to year with demand and the number of places.

Selection ranks, not ATARs

Published thresholds are usually lowest selection ranks, which include adjustment factors. Your selection rank for a course may be higher than your ATAR if you are eligible for adjustments, which can bring additional courses within reach.

Some courses look beyond the rank

Medicine, dentistry, some health courses, creative and performance courses and some education courses often use additional criteria, such as admissions tests, interviews, portfolios, auditions or personal statements. For these, the ATAR is only part of the picture.

How to improve your ATAR

Because the ATAR is built from your results, improving it comes down to improving your results in the subjects that count. There are no shortcuts, but there are strategies that make your effort count for more.

Know which subjects count

Start by understanding your state’s rules. If English must count, it deserves serious attention. If only your best five or best four results count, identify which subjects are likely to be in that group and which are acting as buffers. A calculator can show which subjects move your estimate most at your current level.

Focus on consistency

Your ATAR rewards strong results across several subjects more than a single outstanding result. Steady work in every subject protects you if one subject goes less well than expected, and it keeps more subjects competing for a place in your best results.

Use feedback

Teachers’ feedback on assessments is one of the most valuable resources you have. Act on it: rework weak answers, ask how a response could have earned more marks and apply that advice in your next task.

Practise under exam conditions

Exams carry substantial weight in most states. Complete full past papers under timed, closed-book conditions, mark them against official marking guidelines and focus your revision on the areas where you lose the most marks.

Look after the basics

Sleep, regular breaks, exercise and a sustainable routine support concentration and memory across a long year. Students who burn out in Term 3 often find it hard to recover for the final exams.

Check your adjustments

Your ATAR is fixed once released, but your selection rank can be higher if you are eligible for adjustment factors. Check the schemes at each university you are considering and apply for any that require an application.

Do you always need an ATAR?

No. The ATAR is the main path into university straight from Year 12, but it is not the only one. Many students enter through other routes.

Common alternatives include a diploma or a TAFE qualification that leads into a degree, an enabling or foundation program, a portfolio or audition for creative courses, and special entry schemes for students who faced disadvantage. If your ATAR is lower than you hoped, these pathways are worth knowing about early.

Some courses do not use the ATAR at all, and some students choose not to sit for one. Not receiving an ATAR does not close the door to university — it just means you take a different way in.

The main routes that do not rely on an ATAR

  • University diplomas and college programs. Many universities, or colleges linked to them, offer diplomas that lead into the second year of a related degree once completed successfully.
  • TAFE qualifications. A diploma or advanced diploma from TAFE can give you a basis for admission, and sometimes credit towards a degree.
  • Enabling and foundation programs. These build academic skills and can give you a basis for admission. Many are available without tuition fees for eligible domestic students.
  • Portfolio, audition and interview entry. Creative and performance courses often assess a portfolio or audition alongside, or instead of, an ATAR.
  • Special entry and equity schemes. Many institutions consider applicants who have experienced educational disadvantage through schemes that look beyond the ATAR.
  • Mature-age entry. Adults can often apply on the basis of work experience, prior study or a test such as the Special Tertiary Admissions Test.
  • Early offer schemes. Some universities make offers before ATARs are released, using Year 11 results, school recommendations or other criteria.

Choosing not to receive an ATAR

Some students choose a senior pathway that does not lead to an ATAR, because they plan to enter an apprenticeship, TAFE course or employment. That can be an excellent choice. If they later decide to go to university, many of the routes above remain open to them.

If you are not sure

If you are unsure whether you will want to go to university, it can be worth keeping the ATAR option open by choosing a pattern of subjects that meets your state’s eligibility requirements. Talk to your careers adviser about whether that is practical alongside your other interests.

The ATAR for regional, rural and remote students

Students in regional, rural and remote areas receive their ATAR under exactly the same rules as students in cities. Their subjects are scaled in the same way, and their results are combined using the same counting rules. There is no adjustment to the ATAR itself based on where a student lives.

Regional adjustment factors

Many universities offer adjustment factors to students who live or study in regional or remote areas. These are added to the ATAR to create a selection rank for particular courses. Some are applied automatically based on your address or school, while others may require an application. The size and availability of these adjustments differ between institutions. Our guides to regional bonus points and the regional bonus points calculator explain how they work.

Other support

Some universities offer specific programs, scholarships and accommodation support for regional students, and some fields, such as medicine, have rural entry pathways. Our guides to rural education schemes and universities for regional students cover some of the options.

Equity schemes and the ATAR

Equity schemes recognise that some students have experienced circumstances that affected their education, such as financial hardship, illness, disability, disrupted schooling or caring responsibilities. Under these schemes, eligible students may receive adjustment factors that raise their selection rank, or be considered through a separate selection process.

How equity schemes work

Most admissions centres run an equity scheme on behalf of their participating universities. In NSW and the ACT, UAC runs Educational Access Schemes. In Victoria, VTAC runs the Special Entry Access Scheme. In Queensland, QTAC runs an Educational Access Scheme. Other states have similar arrangements. Students usually apply with supporting documents, and each institution decides how to use the information.

Equity schemes do not change your ATAR

Like other adjustment factors, equity adjustments raise your selection rank for particular courses. Your ATAR stays the same.

Apply early

Equity schemes usually require an application with its own deadline and supporting documents. If you think you may be eligible, start early and ask your school’s careers adviser or wellbeing staff for help. Our guide to educational access scheme adjustments explains more.

Special entry schemes beyond the ATAR

Alongside the standard ATAR-based selection, universities run a range of special entry schemes. These recognise that the ATAR does not capture every student’s potential or circumstances.

Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander entry

Many universities offer dedicated entry pathways for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander students. These may involve an application, an interview or a preparation program, and they often come with academic and cultural support once students begin their studies. If this applies to you, contact the Indigenous student support unit at each university you are interested in to learn about its pathways.

Elite athletes and performers

Many institutions offer adjustment factors or special consideration for students who compete or perform at a high level, recognising the time commitment involved. Eligibility criteria and the size of any adjustment differ. Our guide to elite athlete adjustments explains more.

School recommendation schemes

Some universities consider school recommendations, often based on Year 11 results and other criteria, and may make offers before ATARs are released. These schemes usually have their own applications and deadlines.

Subject-based adjustments

Many institutions add adjustment factors for strong results in particular subjects, such as languages or mathematics, especially when those subjects are relevant to a course. Our guide to subject bonus points covers the common schemes.

Why these schemes exist

Special entry schemes reflect a recognition that a single rank cannot capture everything relevant to a student’s readiness for university. They broaden access while keeping the ATAR as a common reference point.

The ATAR and TAFE

Most TAFE courses do not use the ATAR. They usually have their own entry requirements, which may include completion of a senior certificate, particular subjects, an entry assessment, a portfolio or relevant experience. Some TAFE courses in some states use a separate selection score.

TAFE can also be a pathway to university. Completing a TAFE diploma or advanced diploma can give you a basis for admission to some degrees, and sometimes credit towards them. If you are interested in both TAFE and university, ask about pathway arrangements between institutions.

Disability provisions, illness and misadventure

Students sometimes worry that illness, injury, disability or difficult circumstances will unfairly affect their ATAR. Every state has processes designed to give students fair access to assessment and to respond when something goes wrong.

Provisions for students with disability or other needs

Students with disability, medical conditions or other needs can apply for provisions or adjustments in exams, such as extra time, rest breaks, a reader or writer, or separate supervision. The names of these arrangements differ between states: in NSW they are called disability provisions, in Victoria special examination arrangements, and in Queensland access arrangements and reasonable adjustments. These arrangements are designed to give students fair access to the assessment; they do not change how results are scaled or combined into an ATAR.

Illness and misadventure

If illness, injury or another unexpected event affects your performance in, or prevents you from attending, an exam, each state has a process for considering your circumstances. In NSW, students can lodge an illness or misadventure application. In Victoria, students may apply for a Derived Examination Score. Other states have similar processes. Supporting evidence is usually required, and there are strict deadlines.

How these processes relate to the ATAR

If your application is successful, the result for the affected exam or subject may be determined using other evidence, and that result then flows into your ATAR in the usual way. Your ATAR is not adjusted separately. If your circumstances have affected your education more broadly, you may also be eligible for equity schemes that raise your selection rank for particular courses.

Act quickly

If something affects your exams, tell your school straight away. Your school can explain the process in your state, help you gather evidence and make sure your application is lodged on time.

Is the ATAR the same in every state?

Yes, the ATAR itself is national. A 90.00 in Tasmania is treated the same as a 90.00 in Queensland when you apply for university anywhere in the country.

What changes between states is the exam system behind it, and the admissions centre that does the sums. NSW students sit the HSC, Victorian students the VCE, Queensland students the QCE, and so on. Each feeds into the same national rank at the end.

The states also combine your subjects a little differently. NSW counts ten units of scaled marks; Victoria uses an English study plus your best three, with a small contribution from two more; Queensland uses your best five scaled results. Different inputs, same 0.00 to 99.95 output.

Why the ATAR is comparable between states

Although each state builds its aggregate differently, every state converts its aggregates into ranks against its own age group. Because the ATAR represents position within the age group, an ATAR of 85 means roughly the same position wherever it was earned. Admissions centres and universities treat ATARs from different states as equivalent.

Applying interstate

If you apply to a university in another state, your ATAR is passed between admissions centres and used directly. You do not need to convert it. What can differ is timing: ATARs are released on different dates in different states, and that can affect which offer round first includes your result. In 2026, ATARs are released between 10 December in Victoria and 20 December in Western Australia, as our 2026 release dates guide explains.

Adjustments can differ for interstate applicants

Some adjustment factors, such as regional or location-based adjustments, depend on where you live or study. As an interstate applicant, the adjustments available to you may differ from those available to local students. Check each institution’s schemes.

A short history of the ATAR

The ATAR is newer than many people think. Before it, each state had its own Year 12 rank with its own name. Queensland used the OP, Victoria used the ENTER, New South Wales and the ACT used the UAI, and several other states used the TER.

Most states replaced these with the national ATAR around 2009 and 2010. Queensland held on to the OP the longest, moving to the ATAR only in 2020. Since then, every state and territory has used the same rank. If a parent talks about their “OP” or “ENTER score”, that is the older system. See ATAR vs OP vs ENTER for the full story.

Why the change mattered

Before the ATAR, each state’s rank had its own name and, in some cases, its own scale. That made interstate comparisons more confusing for students, parents and universities. Moving to a single name and a common scale made it easier to understand what a rank meant wherever it was earned.

Why Queensland was different

Queensland’s OP was not a percentile rank like the other states’ measures. It placed students in 25 bands, with 1 the highest. Moving to the ATAR involved a larger change to Queensland’s senior system, including the introduction of external assessment in General subjects, which is why it came later. Talking to parents about older ranks

Many parents completed Year 12 under one of the older systems. Their experience can be valuable, but some details have changed, especially in Queensland. If a parent’s advice is based on an OP, an ENTER or a UAI, it is worth checking how it applies to the ATAR today.

International and mature-age students

International students studying an Australian Year 12 overseas can receive an ATAR, often reported as a notional ATAR. Students who take the International Baccalaureate receive a score that is converted to an ATAR-equivalent for university applications.

If you are a mature-age student who did not finish Year 12, you usually will not have an ATAR, and you do not need one. Universities offer separate entry for mature-age applicants, often using an aptitude test such as the STAT, previous study, or work experience instead.

Students studying an Australian curriculum overseas

Some schools outside Australia teach an Australian senior curriculum, such as the HSC, the VCE, the WACE or the SACE. Students who complete these programs may receive an ATAR, sometimes reported as a notional ATAR, depending on the program and the admissions centre. If you are studying an Australian curriculum overseas, ask your school how your results will be reported.

International Baccalaureate students

Students who complete the International Baccalaureate Diploma, in Australia or overseas, receive an IB score rather than an ATAR. Admissions centres publish tables that convert IB scores into a rank comparable with the ATAR, so IB students can apply for the same courses on an equivalent basis. Check your admissions centre’s current conversion table and any subject requirements.

Students from overseas school systems

International students who complete an overseas qualification other than an Australian curriculum or the IB are usually assessed by universities using their own entry requirements for that qualification, often alongside English language requirements. These students do not usually receive an ATAR.

Mature-age applicants in more detail

Universities and admissions centres have several ways of assessing mature-age applicants. Depending on your age and background, you may be assessed on previous tertiary study, vocational qualifications, work experience, completion of an enabling or preparation program, or a test such as the Special Tertiary Admissions Test (STAT). Each institution publishes the categories it accepts. If you completed Year 12 some years ago and received an ATAR, that ATAR does not expire, and it may still be considered alongside your more recent achievements.

Students who repeat Year 12

Some students repeat Year 12, or particular subjects, to improve their results. Each state has rules about which results can count and how results from different years are combined. In NSW, for example, UAC’s rules allow results from courses completed in different years to be included under certain conditions. If you are thinking of repeating, check your state’s rules before you decide, and consider alternatives such as enabling programs or diplomas.

How the ATAR compares with measures overseas

Students who have studied or might study overseas sometimes wonder how the ATAR compares with other countries’ systems. The short answer is that the ATAR is unusual in being a single percentile-style rank, while many other systems rely on grades or test scores.

Grade-based systems

Some systems report a grade in each subject, and universities set entry requirements in terms of grades in particular subjects. Applicants are compared on those grades rather than on a single rank.

Test and grade combinations

Other systems combine school grades with standardised admissions tests and other evidence, such as essays and activities. Universities weigh these together rather than relying on a single number.

International Baccalaureate

The IB Diploma is offered in many countries, including Australia. IB results are reported as a total score, which Australian admissions centres convert to a rank comparable with the ATAR using published tables.

What this means for you

If you are applying to an Australian university with an overseas qualification, the university will assess it using its own requirements for that qualification. If you are applying overseas with an ATAR, check how the institution treats it; some universities outside Australia accept the ATAR, often alongside your subject results.

What students get wrong about the ATAR

The biggest mistake is treating the ATAR as a percentage. It is a rank. A close second is choosing subjects only because they “scale well”, then scoring poorly in them. A strong result in a subject you are good at almost always beats a weak result in a high-scaling one.

Another common error is thinking your school decides your ATAR. It does not. Your ATAR is worked out at the state level against every student, so a strong student is treated the same whatever school they attend. We bust more of these in common ATAR misconceptions.

A final one is panic about a single bad assessment. Because your ATAR uses your best results across a whole year, one poor task rarely decides it. Consistency matters far more than any single mark.

More common misunderstandings

  • “An ATAR of 50 is average.” Among students who receive an ATAR, the typical result is around 70, because the ATAR compares you with your whole age group.
  • “Bonus points change your ATAR.” They change your selection rank for particular courses. Your ATAR stays the same.
  • “A cut-off is a minimum ATAR.” Published thresholds are usually lowest selection ranks from a past round. They include adjustments and can move from year to year.
  • “Hard subjects always scale up.” Scaling reflects the strength of a subject’s students, not its difficulty, and it can move results up or down.
  • “You can work out your exact ATAR in advance.” Scaling and the conversion to a rank are recalculated each year, so every advance calculation is an estimate.
  • “Your ATAR follows you for life.” It matters most for entry to your first course. Once you have university results, they quickly become more important.
  • “English does not matter.” In NSW, your 2 units of English must count, and in Victoria an English study must be in your primary four.

Our article on common ATAR myths explains these and many others in detail.

Why these mistakes matter

Misunderstandings about the ATAR can lead to poor decisions: choosing subjects for the wrong reasons, dropping a subject that would have protected you, removing safe options from a preference list, or feeling discouraged by a perfectly solid result. Understanding what the ATAR is, and what it is not, helps you avoid them.

Is the ATAR fair? Strengths and criticisms

The ATAR is widely used, but it is also debated. Understanding both its strengths and the criticisms made of it helps you see it in perspective.

What the ATAR does well

  • A common measure. It lets universities compare students who studied very different combinations of subjects, in every state, on the same scale.
  • Published rules. The rules for calculating it are published, and the same rules apply to every student in a state.
  • Independence. It is calculated by bodies separate from schools and individual universities.
  • Fairness across subjects. Scaling aims to ensure students are not rewarded or penalised simply for their choice of subjects.

Criticisms and limitations

  • A narrow measure. The ATAR reflects academic results in a set of subjects at one point in time. It does not capture creativity, practical skills, motivation or many qualities that matter at university and beyond.
  • Pressure. Many students and educators point to the stress that can come from focusing on a single number.
  • Differences in opportunity. Average results differ between groups of students, reflecting differences in circumstances and opportunity. That is one reason universities offer equity adjustments and alternative pathways.
  • Complexity. Scaling and state-by-state rules are hard to understand, which allows myths to spread.

How the system has responded

Universities have broadened the ways students can gain entry, with adjustment factors, early offer schemes, portfolio entry, pathway programs and other routes. The Mitchell Institute at Victoria University has noted that university places and pathways have grown over the past two decades, and that only around 64 per cent of Year 12 students now receive an ATAR. The ATAR remains the main measure for school leavers, but it is one of several routes in.

Year 10: choosing subjects

In Year 10, most students choose their senior subjects. This is the moment to check your state’s ATAR eligibility rules, the prerequisites for courses you might want to study and which subjects you are likely to do well in. Choices made here shape the next two years.

Year 11: building foundations

Year 11 is where you build the knowledge and habits Year 12 depends on. In most states, Year 11 results do not count directly towards the ATAR, but in Tasmania eligible courses completed over two years can contribute to the TE Score, and some early offer schemes consider Year 11 results. Year 11 is also a good time to test whether your subjects suit you.

Year 12: assessments and exams

In Year 12, school assessments and final exams produce the results that make up your ATAR. Steady work across the year, good use of feedback and thorough exam preparation all matter. This is also when you research courses, apply through your admissions centre and list your preferences.

Results and offers

First year of university

Reading your results on results day

On results day, you may see several documents or screens: your subject results from your curriculum authority, your ATAR from your admissions centre and, in some states, a separate statement of tertiary entrance results. Knowing what to look for makes the day less confusing.

Check your subject results first

Your subject results show how you performed in each subject. Check that every subject you completed is listed and that each result looks as you would expect. If a subject is missing or a result seems clearly wrong, contact your school or curriculum authority promptly, as review processes have deadlines.

Then look at your ATAR

Your ATAR is your overall rank. Read it as a position: the approximate percentage of your age group you performed better than. Remember that it does not include adjustment factors.

Look for scaled results if your state shows them

Some states report scaled scores or similar information alongside the ATAR. These can help you understand which subjects contributed most to your aggregate.

Work out your selection ranks

Add any adjustment factors you are eligible for at each institution to calculate your selection rank for each course on your list. Compare those ranks with each course’s most recent lowest selection rank.

Note your deadlines

Write down the next change of preference deadline and any offer acceptance deadlines. Then take some time before you make changes.

What universities see when you apply

When you apply for a course, the university receives more than your ATAR. Knowing what it sees helps you understand how decisions are made.

  • Your ATAR. The rank calculated from your Year 12 results.
  • Your selection rank for the course. Your ATAR plus any adjustment factors you are eligible for at that institution.
  • Your subject results. Used to check prerequisites and assumed knowledge, and to apply subject-based adjustments.
  • Your preferences. Where the course sits on your list, which decides which offer you receive if you are eligible for more than one.
  • Any additional information. Such as equity scheme applications, interview or test results, portfolios or personal statements, where the course uses them.

For most courses, the selection rank is the main basis for an offer, provided you meet every other requirement. For courses with additional criteria, those criteria may matter as much as, or more than, the rank.

Using your ATAR to make decisions

Once you have your ATAR, or a realistic estimate of it, you can use it to make practical decisions about your applications. Here is a simple approach.

Work out your selection rank for each course

Add any adjustment factors you are eligible for at each institution to your ATAR. That gives you a selection rank for each course on your list.

Classify each course

Compare each selection rank with the course’s most recent lowest selection rank. A course whose threshold is well below your selection rank is likely to be a safe option. One whose threshold is close to your selection rank is realistic. One whose threshold is above your selection rank is aspirational.

Order your preferences by what you want

List courses in the order you genuinely want them, with your first choice at the top. You are offered the highest preference you are eligible for, so the order does not change your chance of being offered any course; it decides which offer you receive when you are eligible for more than one. Make sure your list includes at least one safe option.

Check every other requirement

For each course, confirm that you meet the prerequisites and any additional criteria, and that you have registered for any interviews, tests or auditions.

Respond to offers on time

When you receive an offer, read it carefully and respond before the acceptance deadline. In most systems, accepting an offer does not stop you receiving an offer for a higher preference in a later round, but check your admissions centre’s rules.

How universities use your ATAR

The ATAR is the most widely used measure for selecting school leavers into undergraduate courses, but universities use it in more ways than many students realise.

Ranking applicants

For most courses, universities rank applicants by selection rank, which is the ATAR plus any adjustment factors. Offers are made from the top of the ranking down until the places for that round are filled, provided applicants meet every other requirement and have listed the course as a preference. The lowest selection rank that receives an offer becomes that round’s published threshold.

Guaranteed entry

Some institutions publish a guaranteed entry rank for particular courses. If your selection rank is at or above it and you meet every other requirement, you are guaranteed an offer. Not every course has one.

Combined with other criteria

Many courses use the ATAR alongside other criteria. Medicine, dentistry and some health courses often use an admissions test such as the UCAT and an interview. Creative and performance courses may use a portfolio or audition. Some courses ask for a personal statement or questionnaire. In these cases, the ATAR may be a threshold you must reach, or one part of a combined score.

Prerequisites and assumed knowledge

Universities also check whether you have completed the subjects a course requires. A prerequisite is a firm requirement; assumed knowledge is what the course expects you to know, and if you have not studied it, you may be advised to complete a bridging course.

Scholarships

Some scholarships use the ATAR as one of their criteria, often alongside other factors such as financial need, location or leadership. Many have their own applications and deadlines.

Early offers

Some universities make offers before ATARs are released, through early offer or school recommendation schemes that use Year 11 results or other criteria. Some of these offers are conditional on achieving a minimum ATAR or meeting a prerequisite.

What happens to your ATAR once you start university

Your ATAR matters most in the months after Year 12, when it is used for your first entry to university. After that, its importance fades quickly.

University results take over

Once you have university results, they become the main measure of your academic performance. Internal transfers, scholarships, honours entry and postgraduate admission usually depend on your university grades, expressed as a grade point average or weighted average mark.

Your ATAR does not expire

Your ATAR remains part of your record, and you can use it to apply for university in later years. As you complete further study or gain work experience, institutions may consider those achievements as well, and may use whichever basis for admission gives you the strongest rank.

Employers rarely ask

Employers generally focus on more recent and relevant evidence, such as university results, qualifications, work experience and skills. The ATAR is rarely part of hiring decisions once you have further study or work behind you. Our guide on whether your GPA matters after graduation looks at what employers do consider.

Does your ATAR decide your career?

Many students feel as though their ATAR will determine their whole future. It is worth looking at what it actually does.

It helps decide your first course

The ATAR’s job is to help universities select students for their first undergraduate course. It can influence which course and institution you start at, and that can matter. But it is one step.

Courses are not the same as careers

Many careers are open to graduates from several different courses, and many graduates work in fields unrelated to their degree. Postgraduate study, further training and work experience all shape careers far more than the ATAR.

There are many routes to the same destination

Students who do not reach a particular course straight from school often get there later, through a related course and an internal transfer, a pathway program or postgraduate study. Some careers that are associated with very high ATARs, such as medicine, can also be reached through graduate-entry programs after a first degree.

What lasts

Skills, knowledge, habits, relationships and experience are what shape a career over decades. The ATAR is a useful tool at one moment, but it is not a measure of what you will achieve.

Common situations, and what the ATAR means for each

The ATAR plays out differently depending on a student’s situation. Here are some common scenarios and what the ATAR means in each.

Your ATAR is higher than you expected

Congratulations. Review your preference list before the next change of preference deadline: courses you left off because they seemed out of reach may now be realistic. Check prerequisites and other requirements before you add them.

Your ATAR is lower than you expected

Take a breath. Work out your selection rank for each course, including adjustments you are eligible for, and compare it with each course’s threshold. Look at related courses at other institutions and at pathway programs into the course you want. Remember that many students reach their goal by a different route.

Your ATAR is just below a course’s threshold

Check whether adjustment factors could close the gap, whether the course has places in later rounds and whether a related course or pathway could lead into it. Keep the course on your list if you genuinely want it; thresholds can move between rounds.

You did not receive an ATAR

That does not close the door to university. Look at diplomas, enabling programs, TAFE pathways, portfolio entry and special admission schemes, and talk to the universities you are interested in.

You are moving interstate

Your ATAR can be used directly in another state. Check when the other state’s admissions centre will receive it and which offer round will first include it, and check whether any adjustment schemes apply to interstate applicants.

You want a gap year

Your ATAR remains valid. You can often defer an offer for up to a year, or apply again the following year using the same ATAR. Check each course’s deferral rules before you accept an offer.

You have changed your mind about your course

Many students change direction. You can usually change your preferences before each round’s deadline, and once at university, internal transfers based on your university results are common.

Talking about the ATAR with friends and family

The ATAR can become a source of comparison and pressure, especially around results day. A few habits can help keep conversations healthy.

  • You do not have to share your ATAR. It is your information. Share it only if and when you want to.
  • Compare with your goals, not with others. Your ATAR is useful in relation to the courses you want, not in relation to your friends’ results.
  • Remember who posts. On results day, the students who share their results most loudly are often those with the highest ATARs, which can give a distorted picture.
  • Focus on next steps. Conversations about preferences, pathways and plans are usually more useful than conversations about the number itself.
  • Ask for support if you need it. If results-day stress or disappointment is weighing on you, talk to someone you trust, such as a parent, teacher, careers adviser or school counsellor.

For parents and carers: the ATAR explained

Many parents and carers completed Year 12 under a different system, or in another country, and find the ATAR confusing. Here are the key points in brief.

  • It is a rank, not a mark. An ATAR of 80 means your child performed better than about 80 per cent of their age group in your state.
  • Around 70 is typical. Among students who receive an ATAR, the median is around 70, so an ATAR in the 60s or 70s is not a poor result.
  • Scaling is fair, not a trick. It places results from different subjects on a common scale so that students are not rewarded or penalised for their subject choices.
  • Subject choice should follow strengths. Students usually achieve their best ATAR by taking subjects they are good at and enjoy, while meeting any prerequisites for courses they are interested in.
  • Bonus points affect the selection rank. Adjustment factors are added to the ATAR for particular courses; the ATAR itself does not change.
  • There are many pathways. Diplomas, enabling programs, TAFE, portfolio entry and mature-age entry all lead to university.
  • Perspective matters. The ATAR is one step in a long journey. Encouragement, routine and support usually help more than pressure about a particular number.

  4. The typical ATAR among students who receive one is about 50. False. It is around 70.
  5. Scaling rewards subjects for being difficult. False. It reflects the strength of the students taking each subject.
  6. Bonus points change your ATAR. False. They change your selection rank for particular courses.
  7. In NSW, English always counts towards the ATAR. True. Your best 2 units of English must be included.
  8. In Western Australia, a bonus for Mathematics Methods can count even if Methods is not in your best four. True.

  10. You need an ATAR to go to university. False. Many pathways do not rely on the ATAR.
  11. The ATAR replaced the Queensland OP for students finishing Year 12 in 2020. True.
  12. Your ATAR expires after a few years. False. It remains on record, although other achievements may become more relevant.

ATAR quick facts

  • ATAR stands for Australian Tertiary Admission Rank.
  • It ranges from 0.00 to 99.95 in steps of 0.05, giving 2,000 possible values.
  • It is a rank against your age group in your state, not a mark.
  • An ATAR of 80 places you in roughly the top 20 per cent of your age group.
  • The typical ATAR among students who receive one is around 70.
  • Each state’s admissions centre, or equivalent body, calculates the ATAR from scaled results.
  • ATARs are comparable between states.
  • Universities use your selection rank, which is your ATAR plus any adjustment factors.
  • The ATAR replaced the UAI, ENTER and TER from 2009 and 2010, and the Queensland OP from 2020.

Glossary of ATAR terms

Adjustment factors

Points added to your ATAR for a particular course to create your selection rank, often called bonus points.

Admissions centre

The organisation that calculates or coordinates the ATAR and processes university applications in a state or region, such as UAC, VTAC, QTAC, TISC or SATAC.

Aggregate

The total of your counted scaled results, used to calculate your ATAR. It goes by different names in different states, such as the TEA in Queensland and Western Australia, the university aggregate in South Australia and the NT, and the TE Score in Tasmania.

Age group

The population the ATAR compares you with: people of a similar age in your state, including those who do not receive an ATAR.

Category A and Category B

In NSW, HSC courses that count towards the ATAR are classified as Category A or Category B. At least 8 units of Category A courses are needed for eligibility, and no more than 2 units of Category B courses can be included in the calculation.

Guaranteed entry rank

A selection rank at or above which some institutions guarantee an offer for a particular course, provided all other requirements are met.

Lowest selection rank

The lowest selection rank to which an offer was made for a course in a particular round, often called the cut-off.

Moderation

The process that adjusts school assessment marks so that marks from different schools can be compared fairly.

Percentile rank

A position expressed as the share of a group at or below that point. The ATAR works in a similar way, relative to your age group.

Preferences

The ordered list of courses you apply for through an admissions centre. You are offered the highest preference you are eligible for.

Prerequisite

A subject or level of achievement you must have to be eligible for a course.

Scaling

The process that places results from different subjects on a common scale, based on how the students in each subject perform across all their subjects.

Selection rank

Your ATAR plus any adjustment factors for a particular course at a particular institution.

Study score

In Victoria, a score from 0 to 50 showing your position within a VCE study, with the mean set at 30.

Tertiary Admission Subjects

In South Australia and the NT, the Stage 2 subjects that can count towards the university aggregate.

More terms you may come across

Board Developed course

In NSW, a course with a syllabus developed by NESA. Only Board Developed courses can count towards the ATAR.

Primary four

In Victoria, your best scaled study score from the English group plus your next three best scaled study scores, which count in full towards your aggregate.

Increment

In Victoria, the 10 per cent contribution of your fifth and sixth scaled study scores, or an eligible VET increment, to your aggregate.

Tertiary Entrance Aggregate (TEA)

The name for the aggregate used in Queensland and Western Australia.

Tertiary Entrance Statement

A statement of tertiary entrance results issued to eligible students in some states, including South Australia and the ACT.

Recognised Studies

In South Australia and the NT, studies other than Tertiary Admission Subjects, such as some VET, that can contribute up to 20 credits towards the university aggregate.

Limited ATAR

In NSW, a rank that some institutions allow certain applicants to use when they have completed between 5 and 9 units of ATAR courses, calculated from their best 5 units.

Change of preference

The period, before each offer round, in which you can change the courses on your preference list and their order.

Common questions

What does ATAR stand for?

ATAR stands for Australian Tertiary Admission Rank. It is the national rank used to compare Year 12 students for entry into university and other tertiary courses.

Is the ATAR out of 100?

No. The ATAR runs from 0.00 to 99.95, in steps of 0.05. There is no 100.00, and it is a rank rather than a score out of 100.

What's the difference between an ATAR and a mark?

A mark is how you did in one subject. An ATAR is a rank that combines your scaled results and places you against your whole age group, so universities can compare students who studied different subjects.

What is the maximum ATAR?

The highest possible ATAR is 99.95. It means you are among the very top students in your state for that year.

What is the lowest ATAR you can get?

Admissions centres usually report anything below 30.00 as “less than 30” rather than an exact number. Everyone who receives an ATAR has passed Year 12; a low ATAR is not a fail.

Does Queensland use the ATAR?

Yes. Queensland switched from the OP system to the ATAR in 2020. Queensland students now receive an ATAR worked out by QTAC, the same as the rest of the country.

Can your ATAR change after you receive it?

No. Once your ATAR is released it is fixed. What can change is your selection rank for a course, if a university adds adjustment factors on top of your ATAR.

Do all universities use the ATAR?

Most undergraduate courses use the ATAR as a main entry measure, but not all. Some use portfolios, auditions, aptitude tests or interviews, and many offer pathways that do not rely on the ATAR at all.

Can international students get an ATAR?

Students taking an Australian Year 12 curriculum, including overseas, can receive an ATAR, sometimes reported as a notional ATAR. International Baccalaureate scores are also converted to an ATAR-equivalent for applications.

What does an ATAR of 80 mean?

An ATAR of 80 means you performed better than about 80 per cent of your age group in your state, placing you in roughly the top 20 per cent. It does not mean you scored 80 per cent in your subjects. Among students who actually receive an ATAR, a larger share sit at 80 or above, about a third in NSW in 2025.

What does an ATAR of 90 mean?

An ATAR of 90 means you performed better than about 90 per cent of your age group in your state, placing you in roughly the top 10 per cent. In NSW in 2025, about 17.3 per cent of students who received an ATAR had 90 or above, because ATAR recipients tend to be stronger academically than the age group as a whole.

What does an ATAR of 70 mean?

An ATAR of 70 means you performed better than about 70 per cent of your age group in your state. Among students who receive an ATAR, 70 is close to the typical result: UAC reported a median of 70.75 in NSW in 2025, and VTAC reported an average of 69.52 in Victoria in 2024.

What does an ATAR of 50 mean?

An ATAR of 50 means you performed better than about half of your age group in your state. Because many people in the age group do not receive an ATAR, and those who do tend to be stronger on average, an ATAR of 50 is below the typical result among ATAR recipients, which is around 70.

What does an ATAR of 99.95 mean?

An ATAR of 99.95 is the highest possible rank. It means your position was in the top 0.05 per cent of your age group in your state. It does not require perfect marks. In 2025, 53 students received 99.95 in NSW, 42 in Victoria and 37 in Queensland.

Is the ATAR a percentage?

No. The ATAR looks like a percentage but works like a rank. It tells you the approximate percentage of your age group you performed better than, not the percentage of marks you earned. An ATAR of 75 means better than about 75 per cent of your age group, not 75 per cent of the available marks.

Is the ATAR the same as a percentile?

It works in a similar way. The ATAR expresses your position relative to your age group in your state, which is why an ATAR of 80 means you performed better than about 80 per cent of that group. It is reported between 0.00 and 99.95 in steps of 0.05 and is calculated from scaled results under each state’s rules.

Who is in the age group the ATAR compares me with?

The ATAR is designed to reflect your position relative to people of a similar age in your state, including those who left school early or did not receive an ATAR. That is why the typical ATAR among students who receive one is around 70 rather than 50.

Why is the median ATAR around 70 and not 50?

Because the ATAR compares you with your whole age group, including people who do not receive an ATAR. Students who receive an ATAR tend to be stronger academically than the age group as a whole, so the middle of the ATAR recipient group sits above the middle of the age group, at around 70.

How many students get an ATAR each year?

In 2025, about 60,432 students received an ATAR in NSW, 49,310 in Victoria and around 30,167 in Queensland, with smaller numbers in the other states and territories. Nationally, a December 2025 Mitchell Institute report estimated that around 64 per cent of Year 12 students receive an ATAR.

How many students get 99.95?

Only a few dozen in each of the largest states. In 2025, 53 NSW students, 42 Victorian students and 37 Queensland students received the top ATAR of 99.95. The smaller states and territories have fewer.

How many possible ATAR values are there?

Because ATARs are reported in steps of 0.05 from 0.00 to 99.95, there are 2,000 possible values. Queensland’s admissions centre describes the ATAR as having 2,000 bands. Each represents roughly the same share of the age group.

Why is there no ATAR of 100?

The ATAR is reported in bands of 0.05, and the highest band is reported as 99.95. The scale therefore never shows 100.00. Reaching 99.95 means your position was in the top 0.05 per cent of your age group, not that your marks were perfect.

Are ATARs below 30 reported?

Usually not as exact numbers. In Queensland, ATARs at or below 30 are reported as 30.00 or less. In South Australia, ATARs below 30.00 are not shown on the Tertiary Entrance Statement. Other states use similar conventions.

Who calculates the ATAR in NSW?

UAC, the Universities Admissions Centre, calculates the ATAR for NSW students from their HSC results. NESA, the NSW Education Standards Authority, sets the HSC courses, runs the exams and issues HSC results.

Who calculates the ATAR in Victoria?

VTAC, the Victorian Tertiary Admissions Centre, calculates the ATAR for Victorian students from their scaled VCE study scores. The VCAA sets the VCE studies, runs the exams and issues study scores.

Who calculates the ATAR in Queensland?

QTAC, the Queensland Tertiary Admissions Centre, calculates the ATAR for Queensland students from their subject results. The QCAA sets the senior subjects, runs external assessment and issues subject results.

Who calculates the ATAR in Western Australia?

TISC, the Tertiary Institutions Service Centre, calculates scaled scores and ATARs for Western Australian students. SCSA, the School Curriculum and Standards Authority, sets the WACE courses and runs the exams.

Who calculates the ATAR in South Australia and the NT?

SATAC, the South Australian Tertiary Admissions Centre, calculates the university aggregate and ATAR for students completing the SACE in South Australia or the NTCET in the Northern Territory.

Who calculates the ATAR in Tasmania?

In Tasmania, the ATAR is worked out through the University of Tasmania with TASC, the Office of Tasmanian Assessment, Standards and Certification. Students’ eligible course results form a TE Score, which is converted to an ATAR.

Who calculates the ATAR in the ACT?

ACT students’ results are scaled through the ACT’s own process managed by the Board of Senior Secondary Studies, and the ATAR is based on their best three major scaled scores plus 60 per cent of their next major or minor. UAC handles university applications for ACT students.

Does my school calculate my ATAR?

No. Your school provides assessment marks, but your ATAR is calculated at the state level by an admissions centre or equivalent body, using the same rules for every student. Your school does not add a bonus or penalty to your ATAR.

Do universities calculate the ATAR?

Generally not. ATARs are calculated by admissions centres or equivalent bodies, which then pass them to universities. In Tasmania, the University of Tasmania is involved in working out the ATAR. Universities use your ATAR, plus any adjustment factors, to make offers.

What is scaling?

Scaling is the process that places results from different subjects on a common scale so that they can be added together fairly. It looks at how the students in each subject perform across all their subjects. It can move results up or down, and its effect varies along the range of results in each subject.

Do hard subjects get a bonus in the ATAR?

No. Scaling reflects the strength of the students taking each subject, not how hard the subject is. A subject taken by students who are strong across all their subjects tends to scale up. A strong result in a subject you are good at usually contributes more than a weak result in a subject that scales well.

What is an aggregate?

An aggregate is the total of your counted scaled results, built under your state’s rules. It goes by different names, such as the TEA in Queensland and Western Australia, the university aggregate in South Australia and the NT and the TE Score in Tasmania. Aggregates are ranked to produce ATARs.

How many subjects count towards the ATAR?

It depends on your state. NSW counts 10 units, including 2 units of English. Victoria counts your English study and next three best studies, plus 10 per cent of two more. Queensland counts your best five results, Western Australia your best four plus bonuses, and South Australia and the NT your best 90 credits.

Does English have to count towards my ATAR?

In NSW, your best 2 units of English must be included. In Victoria, an English study must be in your primary four. In Queensland, you need a C or better in an English subject to be eligible, but English only counts if it is among your best five. Other states have their own English requirements.

What is the difference between an ATAR and a selection rank?

Your ATAR is a single rank calculated from your Year 12 results. Your selection rank is your ATAR plus any adjustment factors you are eligible for, for a particular course at a particular institution. You have one ATAR but may have several selection ranks.

What are adjustment factors?

Adjustment factors, often called bonus points, are added to your ATAR to create your selection rank for particular courses. They may be awarded for strong results in particular subjects, for location, for educational disadvantage, for elite sporting or artistic performance or through school recommendation schemes.

Do bonus points change my ATAR?

No. Bonus points, or adjustment factors, change your selection rank for particular courses at particular institutions. Your ATAR stays the same. That is why you should compare your likely selection rank, not just your ATAR, with each course’s threshold.

What is a cut-off?

A cut-off usually refers to the lowest selection rank to which an offer was made for a course in a particular round. It includes adjustment factors and reflects demand and the number of places that year, so it is a guide rather than a fixed minimum or a guarantee.

Is the ATAR the same as my HSC, VCE or QCE?

No. Your senior certificate, such as the HSC, VCE or QCE, shows that you completed senior secondary school. The ATAR is a separate rank used for university admission and is issued by an admissions centre or equivalent body. You can receive a senior certificate without an ATAR.

Can I get a certificate without an ATAR?

Yes. Many students complete their senior certificate through a pattern of study that does not lead to an ATAR, often because they are heading into an apprenticeship, TAFE or work. Not receiving an ATAR does not mean you failed Year 12.

Can I fail the ATAR?

No. The ATAR is a rank, not a pass or fail grade. A lower ATAR means a lower position relative to your age group, not a failure. Passing or completing Year 12 is recorded by your senior certificate, which is separate from the ATAR.

Is an ATAR from one state valid in another?

Yes. ATARs are designed to be comparable between states, and admissions centres pass them to each other. An ATAR of 85 from Western Australia is treated as an ATAR of 85 by a university in NSW or Victoria. The main difference is the timing of releases in different states.

Why do states calculate the ATAR differently if it is national?

Each state has its own senior curriculum, assessment system and certificate, so the way results are combined into an aggregate differs. The final step, ranking aggregates against the age group, is designed so that an ATAR means the same position wherever it was earned.

When did the ATAR start?

The ATAR replaced the UAI in NSW and the ACT and the ENTER in Victoria from 2009, and the TER in South Australia, the NT, Western Australia and Tasmania from 2010. Queensland replaced the OP with the ATAR for students completing Year 12 in 2020.

What was the UAI?

The UAI, or Universities Admission Index, was the rank used in NSW and the ACT before the ATAR. It was replaced by the ATAR from 2009. Like the ATAR, it was a rank used for university admission.

What was the ENTER?

The ENTER, or Equivalent National Tertiary Entrance Rank, was the rank used in Victoria before the ATAR. It was replaced by the ATAR from 2009.

What was the TER?

The TER, or Tertiary Entrance Rank, was the rank used in South Australia, the Northern Territory, Western Australia and Tasmania before the ATAR. It was replaced by the ATAR from 2010.

How was the Queensland OP different from the ATAR?

The OP, or Overall Position, placed Queensland students in 25 bands, with 1 the highest, rather than giving a percentile-style rank. Queensland moved to the ATAR for students completing Year 12 in 2020, along with broader changes to its senior system, including external assessment in General subjects.

Is the ATAR the only way into university?

No. The ATAR is the main route for school leavers, but universities also offer diplomas, enabling programs, TAFE pathways, portfolio and audition entry, special entry schemes, early offer schemes and mature-age entry. Many students reach university through these routes every year.

Does a low ATAR stop me going to university?

Not necessarily. Many courses have a range of thresholds across institutions, and adjustment factors can raise your selection rank. Pathway programs, diplomas and enabling courses can also lead into degrees. Talk to the universities you are interested in about the options.

Do I need an ATAR as a mature-age student?

No. Mature-age applicants are often assessed on previous study, vocational qualifications, work experience, a preparation or enabling program, or a test such as the Special Tertiary Admissions Test. Each institution publishes the categories of mature-age entry it accepts.

How do IB students apply to Australian universities?

Students who complete the International Baccalaureate Diploma apply through admissions centres in the usual way. Their IB score is converted to a rank comparable with the ATAR using tables published by admissions centres, so they can compete on an equivalent basis.

When will I receive my ATAR in 2026?

In 2026, ATARs are released between Thursday 10 December in Victoria and Sunday 20 December in Western Australia. South Australia and the NT are expected on 14 December, NSW, the ACT and Tasmania on 16 December and Queensland on 18 December. Check your admissions centre’s website for confirmed details.

When do university offers come out?

In 2026, the first ATAR-based offers arrive on Wednesday 23 December in NSW, the ACT, Victoria and Queensland, on Thursday 24 December in Western Australia and on Thursday 14 January 2027 in South Australia and the NT. Further rounds follow through January and February.

Can I change my preferences after I get my ATAR?

Most admissions centres allow you to change your preferences after results are released, up to each round’s deadline. Many students adjust their lists once they can compare their selection rank with course thresholds. Check the change of preference deadline for the round you are targeting.

How do universities use my ATAR?

For most courses, universities rank applicants by selection rank, which is the ATAR plus any adjustment factors, and make offers from the top down until places are filled. Applicants must also meet prerequisites and any other requirements. Some courses combine the ATAR with interviews, tests, portfolios or auditions.

What is a guaranteed entry rank?

Some institutions publish a guaranteed entry rank for particular courses. If your selection rank is at or above it and you meet all other requirements, you are guaranteed an offer. Not every course has a guaranteed entry rank.

What is a prerequisite?

A prerequisite is a subject, or a level of achievement in a subject, that you must have completed to be eligible for a course. It is a firm requirement. Assumed knowledge is different: it is what a course expects you to know, and you may still be eligible without it.

Does my ATAR affect scholarships?

Some scholarships use the ATAR as one of their criteria, often alongside factors such as financial need, location or leadership. Many have their own applications and deadlines. Check each university’s scholarship information early.

Does my ATAR matter after I start university?

Much less. Once you have university results, they become the main measure of your academic performance for transfers, scholarships, honours and further study. Your ATAR remains on record and can be used to apply in later years, but it quickly becomes less important.

Does my ATAR expire?

No. Your ATAR remains part of your record, and you can use it to apply for university in later years. As you complete further study or gain work experience, institutions may consider those achievements as well.

Do employers ask for your ATAR?

Very rarely. Employers generally focus on more recent and relevant evidence, such as university results, qualifications, work experience and skills. The ATAR is rarely part of hiring decisions once you have further study or work behind you.

How can I improve my ATAR?

Improve your results in the subjects that count. Understand your state’s rules, work consistently across all your subjects, act on teacher feedback, practise full exams under timed conditions and look after sleep and routine. Your selection rank can also be higher if you are eligible for adjustment factors.

Should I choose subjects based on scaling?

Not mainly. Your result in a subject matters more than its scaling, and scaling changes from year to year. Choose subjects you are good at and enjoy, meet the prerequisites for courses you are interested in and use scaling only as a tie-breaker between subjects you are equally strong in.

Does Year 11 count towards the ATAR?

In most states, the ATAR is based mainly on final-year results, although Year 11 builds the foundations you need. In Tasmania, eligible Level 3 and Level 4 courses completed over two years can count towards the TE Score, and some early offer schemes consider Year 11 results.

Can VET subjects count towards the ATAR?

In several states, yes, within limits. In Queensland, an eligible VET qualification at Certificate III or higher can be one of your five results. In Victoria, scored VCE VET programs can count and some unscored VET can contribute an increment. In South Australia and the NT, Recognised Studies can contribute up to 20 credits.

What are Category A and Category B courses?

In NSW, HSC courses that can count towards the ATAR are classified as Category A or Category B. To be eligible for an ATAR you need at least 8 units of Category A courses, and no more than 2 units of Category B courses can be included in your ATAR calculation.

What is a Limited ATAR?

In NSW, UAC explains that some institutions allow certain applicants who complete between 5 and 9 units of ATAR courses, including at least 1 unit of English, to use a Limited ATAR. It is calculated from the best 5 units rather than the usual 10.

What do I need to be eligible for an ATAR in NSW?

You must satisfactorily complete at least 10 units of ATAR courses, including at least 8 units of Category A courses, 2 units of English, three Board Developed courses of 2 units or more and courses from at least four subject areas.

What do I need to be eligible for an ATAR in Queensland?

You need five General subjects, or four General subjects plus one Applied subject or one eligible VET qualification at Certificate III or higher, and you must achieve a C or better in an English subject.

What do I need to be eligible for an ATAR in South Australia?

You must complete the SACE and at least 90 credits of eligible Stage 2 study under SATAC’s rules, with at least 60 credits from 20-credit Tertiary Admission Subjects and up to 20 credits of Recognised Studies, within a limited number of attempts.

What do I need to be eligible for an ATAR in Tasmania?

You need the TCE and a Satisfactory Achievement or better in at least four eligible Level 3 or Level 4 courses. From 2025, your TE Score is built from your best eligible course scores over up to two years.

How is the WA ATAR calculated?

Your TEA is the sum of your best four scaled scores, plus 10 per cent of your best scaled score in a language other than English, 10 per cent of your scaled score in Mathematics Methods and 10 per cent of your scaled score in Mathematics Specialist. The maximum TEA is 430. The TEA is then converted to an ATAR.

How is the Victorian ATAR calculated?

VTAC adds your best scaled study score in the English group and your next three best scaled study scores, then adds 10 per cent of your fifth and sixth scaled study scores or an eligible VET increment. The aggregate, which can range up to over 210, is converted to an ATAR.

How is the Queensland ATAR calculated?

QTAC adds your best five scaled results to form your Tertiary Entrance Aggregate, with a maximum of 500. The results can come from five General subjects or four General subjects plus one Applied subject or eligible VET qualification. The TEA is then converted to an ATAR.

How is the NSW ATAR calculated?

UAC adds the scaled marks of your best 2 units of English and your best 8 units from your remaining courses, including no more than 2 units of Category B courses. Scaled marks are out of 50 per unit, so the aggregate is out of 500. The aggregate is then converted to an ATAR.

How is the SA and NT ATAR calculated?

SATAC calculates your university aggregate from the scaled scores of your best 90 credits, with scaled scores out of 20 for 20-credit subjects and out of 10 for 10-credit subjects. The aggregate, out of 90, is then converted to an ATAR.

How is the ACT ATAR calculated?

Your ATAR is based on the sum of your best three major scaled scores plus 60 per cent of your next best major or minor. Scaled scores come from the ACT’s own process for comparing results across colleges and courses.

Is the ATAR fair?

The ATAR is designed to compare students fairly by placing results from different subjects on a common scale and applying the same published rules to everyone in a state. Critics point out that it is a narrow measure and that average results differ between groups of students, which is one reason universities offer adjustment factors and alternative pathways.

Why do some people criticise the ATAR?

Common criticisms are that it is a narrow measure of academic results at one point in time, that focusing on one number can create pressure, that average results differ between groups with different opportunities and that its rules are complex. Supporters point to its common scale, published rules and independence.

Is the ATAR used outside Australia?

Some universities outside Australia accept the ATAR, often alongside your subject results, when assessing applications from Australian students. If you plan to apply overseas, check how each institution treats the ATAR.

How does the ATAR compare with A-levels or the SAT?

The ATAR is a single percentile-style rank, while many other systems rely on subject grades, standardised test scores or a combination of grades, tests and other evidence. Australian universities assess overseas qualifications using their own requirements for each qualification.

What is a notional ATAR?

Some students who complete an Australian senior curriculum overseas receive an ATAR that is reported as a notional ATAR, depending on the program and the admissions centre. If you are studying an Australian curriculum outside Australia, ask your school how your results will be reported.

Can I estimate my ATAR before results day?

Yes, with an ATAR calculator built for your state. An estimate uses recent official scaling data and the marks you enter, so it is a useful guide for planning, but it cannot be exact, because the current year’s scaling and conversion are only known once all results are in.

How accurate are ATAR estimates?

A well-built calculator using recent data and realistic marks usually gives a useful estimate, but the official ATAR often differs by a few points. Accuracy depends mainly on how realistic your inputs are. Treat any estimate as a range and re-run it as your results come in.

What should parents know about the ATAR?

It is a rank, not a mark; around 70 is a typical result among ATAR recipients; scaling is a fairness mechanism; subject choice should follow strengths; bonus points affect the selection rank, not the ATAR; and there are many pathways to university. Support and perspective help more than pressure.

Is the ATAR a measure of intelligence?

No. The ATAR measures your Year 12 results in your chosen subjects, relative to your age group, at one point in time. It does not measure intelligence, creativity, practical skills, character or potential.

Does a high ATAR guarantee university success?

No. Year 12 results are related to university results on average, but the relationship is far from perfect. Students with modest ATARs often do very well at university, and students with high ATARs can find the transition challenging. Your habits and engagement in first year matter a great deal.

Do private school students get higher ATARs?

There is no adjustment in the ATAR calculation for the type of school a student attends. Every student in a state is ranked using the same rules. Your ATAR reflects your own results, wherever you study.

Can I see my scaled scores?

It depends on your state. Some admissions centres report scaled scores to students, while others report only the ATAR and subject results. Your admissions centre’s results service will show what is available in your state.

What happens if I miss the ATAR requirements?

If your subjects do not meet your state’s ATAR eligibility requirements, you will not receive an ATAR, but you can still complete your senior certificate and reach university through other routes, such as diplomas, enabling programs or TAFE pathways.

Where can I find official information about the ATAR?

Your state’s admissions centre is the best source: UAC for NSW and the ACT, VTAC for Victoria, QTAC for Queensland, TISC for Western Australia and SATAC for South Australia and the NT. In Tasmania, the University of Tasmania and TASC publish information on the TE Score and ATAR.

What is the HSC?

The Higher School Certificate is the senior secondary credential for NSW students, set and examined by NESA. Each HSC course result combines a moderated school assessment mark and an exam mark in equal parts. UAC uses scaled HSC marks to calculate the ATAR.

What is the VCE?

The Victorian Certificate of Education is the senior secondary credential for Victorian students, administered by the VCAA. Each study produces a study score from 0 to 50, which VTAC scales and combines to calculate the ATAR.

What is the QCE?

The Queensland Certificate of Education is the senior secondary credential for Queensland students, administered by the QCAA. QTAC calculates the ATAR from students’ best five scaled results in eligible subjects.

What is the WACE?

The Western Australian Certificate of Education is the senior secondary credential for Western Australian students, administered by SCSA. TISC calculates scaled scores and the ATAR from students’ ATAR course results.

What is the SACE?

The South Australian Certificate of Education is the senior secondary credential for South Australian students. SATAC calculates the university aggregate and ATAR from students’ best 90 credits of eligible Stage 2 study.

What is the TCE?

The Tasmanian Certificate of Education is the senior secondary credential for Tasmanian students, administered by TASC. Eligible Level 3 and Level 4 course results form a TE Score, which is converted to an ATAR.

What is the NTCET?

The Northern Territory Certificate of Education and Training is the senior secondary credential for NT students. It uses the SACE framework, and SATAC calculates NT students’ ATARs using the same rules as South Australia.

What are disability provisions?

Disability provisions, called by different names in different states, are arrangements such as extra time, rest breaks, a reader or writer, or separate supervision that give students with disability or other needs fair access to exams. They do not change how results are scaled or combined into an ATAR.

What happens if I am sick during my exams?

Each state has a process for considering illness or misadventure that affects an exam, such as illness or misadventure applications in NSW and Derived Examination Scores in Victoria. Tell your school straight away, as evidence and deadlines apply. If successful, the affected result may be determined using other evidence.

Does illness or misadventure change my ATAR directly?

No. If an application is successful, the result for the affected exam or subject may be determined using other evidence, and that result then flows into your ATAR in the usual way. Your ATAR is not adjusted separately.

Does my ATAR decide my career?

No. The ATAR helps decide your first university course. Careers are shaped much more by what you study, further training, work experience, skills and the choices you make over many years. Many careers can be reached through several different routes.

Can I become a doctor without a 99 ATAR?

Medicine is very competitive, and undergraduate programs usually look for very high ATARs together with admissions tests and interviews. But there are other routes, including graduate-entry medical programs after a first degree and rural or equity pathways. Check each program’s requirements.

What questions should I ask my careers adviser?

Ask whether your subjects meet the ATAR eligibility rules and course prerequisites, which subjects are likely to count, what range you should plan around, which adjustment schemes you might be eligible for, what the key dates are and what pathways exist if you do not reach your first choice.

Do I have to tell people my ATAR?

No. Your ATAR is your information, and you can choose whether to share it. It is most useful in relation to the courses you want, not in comparison with friends’ results.

Why does everyone online seem to have a high ATAR?

On results day, the students who share their results most loudly are often those with the highest ATARs, which creates a distorted picture. Among students who receive an ATAR, the typical result is around 70.

Is an ATAR of 60 bad?

No. An ATAR of 60 places you ahead of about 60 per cent of your age group. It opens a range of courses and pathway programs, and your selection rank for some courses may be higher once adjustment factors are added. Look at your options rather than the number alone.

Is an ATAR of 75 good?

An ATAR of 75 places you ahead of about 75 per cent of your age group and above the typical result among ATAR recipients. Whether it is good for you depends on the courses you want, their thresholds and any adjustment factors you are eligible for.

Is an ATAR of 85 good?

An ATAR of 85 places you in roughly the top 15 per cent of your age group, a strong result that opens many in-demand courses. As with any ATAR, compare your selection rank with the thresholds of the specific courses you want.

Is an ATAR of 95 good?

An ATAR of 95 places you in roughly the top 5 per cent of your age group, a very high result. For the most competitive courses, other criteria such as interviews and admissions tests may matter as much as the ATAR.

Does a higher ATAR mean a better university?

Not necessarily. Thresholds reflect demand for particular courses at particular institutions in a particular year, not the overall quality of a university. Choose a course and institution that suit your interests, goals and circumstances.

Are ATARs going up or down over time?

The ATAR is designed so that each value represents roughly the same position in the age group each year, so ATARs do not drift upwards or downwards in the way marks can. What changes is how many students receive an ATAR and how thresholds for particular courses move.

Why do course thresholds change each year?

Thresholds are lowest selection ranks that reflect the number of places a course offers and the number and strength of applicants who put it high on their preference list. If demand rises or places fall, the threshold can rise, and vice versa.

Can I get an ATAR if I study by distance education?

Yes. Students studying through distance education or a virtual school are ranked under the same rules as everyone else in their state, provided their subjects meet the eligibility requirements.

Can homeschooled students get an ATAR?

It depends on the state and on whether the student completes an eligible pattern of senior study, for example through a registered provider, distance education or as a non-school candidate. Check your state’s curriculum authority and admissions centre for the options available.

Why is the ATAR a rank instead of a mark?

Because marks in different subjects are not directly comparable, and a rank built from scaled results allows a fair comparison across thousands of subject combinations. A rank also keeps a stable meaning from year to year and allows fine distinctions near course thresholds.

Does NAPLAN count towards the ATAR?

No. NAPLAN results do not count towards the ATAR. They can give students and teachers useful information about literacy and numeracy skills, but the ATAR is calculated only from eligible senior secondary results.

What should I do in Year 10 to prepare for the ATAR?

Build good study habits, explore the subjects you enjoy and do well in, learn your state’s ATAR eligibility rules and check the prerequisites for courses you might be interested in. These steps help you choose senior subjects that keep your options open.

What is the difference between a median and an average ATAR?

A median is the middle value of all ATARs; an average is the total divided by the number of students. They are usually similar but not identical. UAC reported a median ATAR of 70.75 in NSW in 2025, while VTAC reported an average of 69.52 in Victoria in 2024.

What does a school’s median ATAR tell me?

It describes the middle result among that school’s ATAR recipients in a particular year. It does not tell you what any individual student will receive. Your ATAR reflects your own results under the same rules as every other student in your state.

Why might my friend with lower marks have a higher ATAR?

Their subjects may have scaled more strongly at their level of result, their results may have combined more favourably under your state’s counting rules, or their weakest results may not have counted. ATARs depend on scaled results and counting rules, not just raw marks.

Can my weakest subject be left out of my ATAR?

Often, yes. Most states count only a set number of your best results, with some rules about compulsory subjects such as English. If you take more subjects or units than the minimum, your weakest result may not count. Check your state’s rules.

If I take more subjects, will my ATAR be higher?

Not automatically. Extra subjects only help if they produce results strong enough to be among your counted results, or if they act as a buffer when another subject goes less well. They can also take time away from other subjects, so weigh the trade-off.

What is a selection rank used for?

Universities use your selection rank, which is your ATAR plus any adjustment factors, to decide offers for each course. Course thresholds are usually expressed as lowest selection ranks.

Do all universities offer the same adjustments?

No. Each institution sets its own adjustment schemes and caps, and sometimes they differ between courses at the same institution. Compare your likely selection rank for each course rather than assuming the same adjustments apply everywhere.

What is the Educational Access Scheme?

Educational Access Schemes are equity schemes run by UAC in NSW and the ACT and by QTAC in Queensland, among others, for students whose education has been affected by circumstances such as hardship, illness or disrupted schooling. Eligible students may receive adjustments that raise their selection rank.

What is SEAS in Victoria?

SEAS, the Special Entry Access Scheme, is VTAC’s equity scheme for Victorian applicants whose education has been affected by particular circumstances. Eligible applicants may receive adjustments or other consideration from participating institutions.

Do regional students get bonus points?

Many universities offer adjustment factors to students who live or study in regional or remote areas. These raise the selection rank for particular courses, not the ATAR. Some are applied automatically, while others need an application. The rules differ between institutions.

Do TAFE courses use the ATAR?

Most TAFE courses do not use the ATAR. They usually have their own entry requirements, such as completion of a senior certificate, particular subjects, an entry assessment or a portfolio. Some TAFE courses in some states use a separate selection score.

Can TAFE lead to university?

Yes. Completing a TAFE diploma or advanced diploma can give you a basis for admission to some degrees, and sometimes credit towards them. Ask about pathway arrangements between TAFE and the universities you are interested in.

What is an enabling program?

An enabling program is a university preparation course for people who need to build academic skills before starting a degree. Completing one can give you a basis for admission to some courses, and many are available without tuition fees for eligible domestic students.

What is the Special Tertiary Admissions Test?

The Special Tertiary Admissions Test, or STAT, is a test that many admissions centres accept for mature-age applicants and others who meet the eligibility requirements. It assesses skills such as reasoning and comprehension and can provide a basis for admission without an ATAR.

How long does my ATAR last?

Your ATAR does not expire. You can use it to apply for university in later years. As you complete further study or gain work experience, institutions may also consider those achievements.

Can I improve my ATAR after it is released?

Your ATAR is fixed once released, unless one of your underlying results is corrected. You can improve your basis for admission through other routes, such as enabling programs, diplomas, university study or, in some states, repeating subjects or Year 12.

Is my ATAR on my Year 12 certificate?

In most states, no. The ATAR is issued separately by the organisation that calculates it, such as an admissions centre. In South Australia and the NT, it appears on the Tertiary Entrance Statement rather than the SACE certificate.

How do I find out my ATAR?

Your ATAR is usually available through your admissions centre’s online results service on your state’s release date. Check your admissions centre’s website for login details and the exact release time.

What should I do on ATAR results day?

Check your ATAR, work out your selection rank for each course by adding any adjustments you are eligible for, compare those ranks with course thresholds, and review your preferences before the next deadline. Do not rush; you usually have time to think.

Can parents access my ATAR?

Your ATAR is your personal information, released to you through your admissions centre’s service. Whether you share it with your parents or carers is up to you, although many students find it helpful to talk through their options with family.

Does the ATAR reward working hard all year?

Yes, indirectly. In most states, school assessments completed throughout the year contribute to your subject results, alongside final exams. Steady work across the year usually produces stronger results than trying to catch up at the end.

What is the difference between an ATAR course and a non-ATAR course?

An ATAR course is a subject whose results can count towards the ATAR under your state’s rules. Some subjects, such as certain school-developed or vocational courses, count towards the senior certificate but not towards the ATAR. Check which of your subjects are ATAR-eligible.

Do extension subjects help my ATAR?

They can, if your results in them are strong enough to be among your counted results. In NSW, 1-unit extension courses are reported out of 50 and can count as single units in your best 10. Extension subjects also add workload, so weigh that against the potential benefit.

Can I use results from two different years in my ATAR?

In some states, yes, under certain conditions. In NSW, UAC’s rules allow courses completed in different years to be included in certain circumstances, and in Tasmania courses completed over two years can count towards the TE Score. Check your state’s rules.

Why is my ATAR lower than my average mark?

Because the ATAR is a rank against your age group, not an average mark. In NSW, UAC explains that students with marks around 70 typically receive ATARs in the 50s, because many students have similar marks in that range. Your ATAR also depends on scaling and counting rules.

Why is my ATAR higher than my average mark?

Your subjects may have scaled strongly at your level of result, your weakest results may not have counted, or your results may sit in a part of the range where ATARs rise quickly with marks. In NSW, UAC notes that marks around 80 typically lead to ATARs around 80.

Is the ATAR used for postgraduate study?

Not usually. Postgraduate admission is generally based on your undergraduate results, relevant experience and sometimes admissions tests or interviews. The ATAR is used mainly for entry to undergraduate courses straight after school.

Are there special entry schemes for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander students?

Yes. Many universities offer dedicated entry pathways for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander students, which may involve an application, an interview or a preparation program, often with academic and cultural support. Contact the Indigenous student support unit at each university you are interested in.

Do elite athletes get ATAR bonus points?

Many institutions offer adjustment factors or special consideration for students who compete or perform at a high level. These raise the selection rank for particular courses rather than the ATAR. Eligibility and the size of any adjustment differ between institutions.

What are school recommendation schemes?

School recommendation schemes allow some universities to consider school recommendations, often based on Year 11 results and other criteria, and to make offers before ATARs are released. They usually have their own applications and deadlines.

What is a subject adjustment?

A subject adjustment is an adjustment factor that some institutions add for strong results in particular subjects, such as languages or mathematics, often when those subjects are relevant to a course. It raises your selection rank for that course, not your ATAR.

What should I check first on results day?

Check that all your subject results are listed and look as expected, then look at your ATAR. If a subject is missing or a result seems clearly wrong, contact your school or curriculum authority promptly, because review processes have deadlines.

Will I see my scaled scores?

It depends on your state. Some admissions centres report scaled scores or similar information alongside the ATAR, while others report only the ATAR and subject results. Your admissions centre’s results service will show what is available.

What is the primary four in Victoria?

The primary four are your best scaled study score from the English group plus your next three best scaled study scores. They count in full towards your Victorian aggregate, with 10 per cent of your fifth and sixth scaled study scores added on top.

What is a VCE increment?

An increment is the 10 per cent contribution of your fifth and sixth scaled study scores, or an eligible VET increment, to your Victorian aggregate. Increments allow additional studies to add a little to your aggregate without counting in full.

What is a Board Developed course?

In NSW, a Board Developed course is one with a syllabus developed by NESA. Only Board Developed courses can count towards the ATAR, and eligibility requires at least 10 units of ATAR courses, including at least 8 units of Category A courses.

What is a Tertiary Entrance Statement?

A Tertiary Entrance Statement is a statement of tertiary entrance results issued to eligible students in some states, including South Australia and the ACT. In South Australia, it records the university aggregate and ATAR.

What are Recognised Studies in South Australia?

Recognised Studies are studies other than Tertiary Admission Subjects, such as some VET, that can contribute up to 20 credits towards the South Australian and NT university aggregate within SATAC’s rules.

What is the change of preference period?

It is the period before each offer round in which you can change the courses on your preference list and their order. Most admissions centres allow changes after ATARs are released, up to each round’s deadline.

Should I put my dream course first even if it is a stretch?

Yes, if you genuinely want it most. You are offered the highest preference you are eligible for, so listing your dream course first costs you nothing if you do not reach it. Make sure your list also includes realistic and safe options.

How many preferences can I list?

It depends on your admissions centre. UAC allows five preferences, QTAC and TISC allow six, and VTAC allows eight. Use them to cover aspirational, realistic and safe options in the order you genuinely want them.

Can I accept an offer and still get a better one later?

In most admissions systems, accepting an offer does not stop you receiving an offer for a higher preference in a later round, but the rules differ. Check your admissions centre’s guidance before you act, and always respond before the acceptance deadline.

What happens if I miss an offer deadline?

If you do not respond to an offer before its acceptance deadline, the offer can lapse and the place may go to another applicant. Set reminders as soon as you receive an offer, and contact the institution quickly if you have a problem.

Can I defer my university offer?

Many universities allow you to defer the start of most courses, often for up to a year, while keeping your place. Some courses do not allow deferral, and each institution has its own rules. Check before you accept an offer.

Is a gap year bad for my ATAR?

No. Your ATAR stays the same and remains valid. You can usually defer an offer or apply again the following year using the same ATAR, along with any qualifications or experience gained during your gap year.

Do universities prefer students straight from school?

Universities admit students from many backgrounds, including school leavers, gap-year students, transfer students and mature-age applicants. Each group is assessed under the institution’s admission rules; a gap year or later start does not, in itself, disadvantage you.

How do I compare two universities’ thresholds?

Compare your likely selection rank at each institution, including the adjustments each offers you, with each course’s most recent lowest selection rank. Thresholds reflect demand at a particular institution in a particular year, so they are not a measure of course quality.

What is assumed knowledge?

Assumed knowledge is the level of understanding a course expects you to have, usually in particular subjects. Unlike a prerequisite, it is not a firm requirement, but if you have not studied it you may be advised to complete a bridging course.

What is a bridging course?

A bridging course is a short course that helps students reach the assumed knowledge for a university course, for example in mathematics or chemistry, before or early in their studies. Many universities offer them.

Does every student get the same ATAR treatment?

Yes. Within a state, every student’s results are scaled, combined and ranked using the same published rules, whatever school they attend. Differences in selection rank come from adjustment factors, which depend on each institution’s schemes and each student’s eligibility.

What is the ATAR used for besides university entry?

The ATAR’s main purpose is undergraduate university admission. Some scholarships and early offer programs also refer to it. It is not generally used for TAFE entry, postgraduate admission or employment.

Why do people say the ATAR is just a number?

Because, while it matters for first entry to university, it is a narrow measure of Year 12 results at one point in time. It does not capture many qualities that matter in study, work and life, and there are many routes to university and to fulfilling careers.

What do universities see when I apply?

Universities see your ATAR, your selection rank for the course including any adjustments, your subject results, where the course sits on your preference list and any additional information the course uses, such as equity applications, interview or test results, portfolios or personal statements.

When do university applications open?

Applications to admissions centres typically open in the second half of the year before you start university. Some courses have earlier closing dates for interviews, portfolios or auditions, so check each course’s requirements and your admissions centre’s key dates.

Which state releases ATARs first?

In 2026, Victoria releases ATARs first, on Thursday 10 December. Western Australia is last, on Sunday 20 December. The other states and territories fall in between.

Why do states release ATARs on different dates?

Each state’s exams, marking and results processes run on their own timetable, and each admissions centre sets its own release date after subject results are finalised. The dates usually fall within the same few weeks of December.

Is English compulsory for the ATAR in every state?

English plays a role in every state, but in different ways. It must count in NSW and Victoria, it is an eligibility requirement in Queensland, and in other states it is linked to certificate or university admission requirements. Check your state’s rules.

What is the maximum aggregate in each state?

In NSW and Queensland, the aggregate is out of 500. In Western Australia, the maximum TEA is 430. In South Australia and the NT, the university aggregate is out of 90. In Victoria, the aggregate can range up to over 210. Each state then converts its aggregate to an ATAR on the same scale.

How does the ATAR handle students who take very different subjects?

Through scaling, which places results from different subjects on a common scale based on how each subject’s students performed across all their subjects. Results are then combined under each state’s rules and ranked, so students with very different subjects can be compared fairly.

Do universities trust ATARs from other states?

Yes. ATARs are designed to be equivalent between states, and admissions centres pass them to each other for interstate applications. Universities treat an ATAR from another state the same as a local ATAR.

What happens in February and March?

Further offer rounds continue into February, and first semester usually begins in late February or early March, starting with orientation. Once you start university, your university results become the main measure of your academic progress.

What is the best way to understand my own ATAR?

Read it as a position: the approximate percentage of your age group you performed better than. Then add your adjustment factors to work out your selection rank for each course, and compare those ranks with course thresholds. Your teachers and careers adviser can help you interpret it.

Is it normal to feel disappointed with my ATAR?

Yes. Many students feel disappointed when their ATAR is lower than they hoped. It helps to remember that it is one step, that there are many pathways into university and that your options are probably wider than they first seem. Talk to someone you trust if the feeling persists.

Can my ATAR be withheld?

In some circumstances, such as unresolved results issues or incomplete requirements, an ATAR may not be released or may be delayed. If you do not receive an ATAR you expected, contact your admissions centre and school promptly.

Can an Applied subject count towards a Queensland ATAR?

Yes. One Applied subject can be included in a Queensland student’s best five results when combined with four General subjects. Applied subjects are assessed internally, without external assessment.

Why is the WA maximum TEA 430?

Because the TEA is the sum of the best four scaled scores, each out of 100, plus bonuses of 10 per cent of the scaled score in a language other than English, Mathematics Methods and Mathematics Specialist. The three bonuses can add up to 30, giving a maximum of 430.

Do NSW extension courses count as full subjects?

Extension courses in NSW are usually 1 unit and are reported out of 50. They count as single units within the best 10 units used for the ATAR, rather than as full 2-unit courses.

Does English Studies count towards a NSW ATAR?

NESA notes that English Studies only counts towards the ATAR if the student sits the optional HSC exam. Students taking English Studies who want an ATAR should check this requirement and their other eligibility requirements carefully.

What is a subject area in NSW?

A subject area is a broader field, such as mathematics or English, that may contain several courses. NSW ATAR eligibility requires courses from at least four subject areas, so taking several courses in the same area does not by itself meet that requirement.

Which studies are in the Victorian English group?

The English group includes English, English as an Additional Language, English Language and Literature. One study from the English group must be in your primary four, and VTAC uses your best scaled score from the group.

Is a study score of 30 average?

Yes. The VCAA sets the mean study score at 30 in each VCE study, with a standard deviation of about 7. A study score of 30 represents the middle of the students taking that study across the state.

Is a study score of 40 good?

Yes. With a mean of 30 and a standard deviation of about 7, a study score of 40 places a student well above the middle of a study. How much it contributes to the ATAR depends on how the study scales.

Does Queensland scale every subject up or down by a set amount?

No. QTAC’s scaling is an iterative process across all eligible students’ results, and its effect varies along each subject’s range. QTAC’s 2025 data showed that no subject was scaled up at every point in its range.

Is WA the only state with built-in subject bonuses?

Western Australia is the state where bonuses for a language other than English and for advanced mathematics are built into the aggregate used to calculate the ATAR itself. In other states, subject-related bonuses are usually adjustment factors added to the selection rank by individual institutions.

What does 30.00 or less mean?

It means the student’s ATAR is at or below 30, and the exact value is not reported. Queensland uses this wording, and other states have similar conventions. It does not prevent the student from pursuing pathway programs or other routes into university.

Can I get an ATAR if I only take VET subjects?

Usually not on VET alone, because each state requires a pattern of eligible academic subjects. However, some VET can contribute within limits, such as one eligible VET qualification in Queensland or Recognised Studies in South Australia and the NT.

How do I know if a subject is ATAR-eligible?

Your admissions centre publishes lists or rules for the subjects that can count towards the ATAR, and your school’s subject selection guide usually marks them. In NSW, for example, UAC publishes the HSC courses that are Category A or Category B.

Do I need maths for an ATAR?

Mathematics is not compulsory for the ATAR in most states, although some states have numeracy requirements for the senior certificate. Many university courses list mathematics as a prerequisite or assumed knowledge, so check the requirements of courses you might want to study.

Do I need a science for an ATAR?

No state requires a science subject for the ATAR itself. Some university courses list particular sciences as prerequisites or assumed knowledge, so check the requirements of courses you are interested in before choosing your subjects.

Can I mix subjects from different levels?

Yes, within your state’s rules. Students commonly combine more and less demanding courses, and each result is scaled according to its own cohort. Some states have rules about counting related courses together, such as different levels of mathematics, so check how your combination is treated.

What if two of my subjects overlap?

Some states have rules about combinations of closely related subjects, such as restrictions on counting certain mathematics or English courses together. Your admissions centre’s rules explain which combinations are allowed and how they are counted.

Should I aim for the highest ATAR possible?

It is sensible to do your best, but a target based on the courses you want is more useful than chasing the highest number. Find the threshold for your preferred course, add any adjustments you are eligible for, and aim a little above that selection rank.

Why do Tasmanian students know more of their result before Year 12 ends?

Because the Tasmanian TE Score can include eligible Level 3 and Level 4 courses completed over up to two years, some students have already completed courses that count before their final year. That can make part of their result known earlier than in most other states.

How many credits count towards an SA ATAR?

Your best 90 credits of eligible Stage 2 study count. At least 60 must come from 20-credit Tertiary Admission Subjects or valid pairs of 10-credit subjects, and the remaining 30 credits form a flexible option that can include up to 20 credits of Recognised Studies.

What are majors and minors in the ACT?

In the ACT, courses are packaged as majors and minors, reflecting the amount of study in each area. The ATAR is based on the best three major scaled scores plus 60 per cent of the next major or minor.

What is the single most important thing to understand about the ATAR?

That it is a rank, not a mark. Your ATAR shows the approximate percentage of your age group in your state that you performed better than, after your results have been scaled and combined under your state’s rules. Almost everything else about the ATAR follows from that idea.

Where should I start if I am new to the ATAR?

Start by learning that the ATAR is a rank, then check your state’s eligibility rules and which subjects count. Next, look at the prerequisites and thresholds for courses you are interested in, and use a calculator built for your state to see a realistic range for your results.
