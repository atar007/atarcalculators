How is ATAR calculated?

Your ATAR is not your raw marks, and it is not an average. This guide walks through the actual steps, from your subject results to the final rank, in plain English.

Your ATAR is calculated in three steps. First, your result in each subject is scaled to account for how strong the group taking it was. Second, your best scaled results are added into an aggregate. Third, that aggregate is ranked against your whole age group and turned into a percentile from 0.00 to 99.95. The details differ a little by state, but the three steps are the same everywhere.

Key takeaways

  • Your ATAR comes from scaled results, not raw marks or a simple average.
  • Scaling adjusts each subject so the same result means the same thing across subjects.
  • Your best scaled scores are combined into an aggregate; weaker subjects count for less or not at all.
  • The aggregate is ranked against your age group and turned into your ATAR.
  • Most states require an English subject to count towards your ATAR.
  • You cannot “game” scaling — a strong result in any subject beats a weak one in a high-scaling subject.
  • Each state’s admissions centre does the sums, but the final ATAR means the same nationally.

The full pipeline: from classroom to ATAR

The three steps in the summary above are the heart of the ATAR, but they sit inside a longer process that starts in your classroom and ends with a number on your screen in December. Seeing the whole pipeline makes each step easier to understand.

  1. Assessment. Through Year 12, and in some states Year 11, you complete school assessment tasks and, in most subjects, external exams or assessments.
  2. Subject results. Your curriculum authority combines your school assessment and external assessment into a result for each subject. School marks are usually moderated first, so that marks from different schools can be compared.
  3. Scaling. Your admissions centre adjusts each subject’s results onto a common scale, based on how the students in that subject performed across all their subjects.
  4. Aggregate. Your best scaled results are combined under your state’s rules, with any compulsory subjects, increments or bonuses applied.
  5. Rank. Aggregates are ranked, and your position relative to your age group in your state is reported as your ATAR, from 0.00 to 99.95 in steps of 0.05.
  6. Selection rank. When you apply for a course, any adjustment factors you are eligible for are added to your ATAR to create your selection rank for that course.

Two different kinds of organisation run this pipeline. Curriculum and assessment authorities, such as NESA in NSW, the VCAA in Victoria, the QCAA in Queensland, SCSA in Western Australia and the SACE Board in South Australia, handle steps 1 and 2. Admissions centres, such as UAC, VTAC, QTAC, TISC and SATAC, handle steps 3 to 5, and universities apply step 6.

Before scaling: how school marks are moderated

Before scaling can happen, each subject needs a result that is comparable across schools. That is the job of moderation, a step that many students never hear about but which affects almost everyone.

The problem moderation solves

Schools set their own assessment tasks and mark them themselves. One school’s tasks may be harder than another’s, and one school’s teachers may mark more generously. A school mark of 85 at one school does not necessarily represent the same achievement as 85 at another. Moderation adjusts school marks so that they can be compared fairly.

How it works in the states with exams

In NSW, Victoria and Western Australia, moderation uses the external exam results of each school’s group of students. Broadly, the school marks are adjusted so that they match the spread and level of the same group’s exam results. The order of students within the school group is generally kept, but the size of the marks and the gaps between them can change. In NSW, the moderated assessment mark is then combined equally with the exam mark to produce the HSC mark. In Western Australia, the moderated school mark is averaged with the exam mark to produce a combined score.

Queensland’s approach

In Queensland, internal assessment in General subjects is quality assured through endorsement and confirmation. The QCAA endorses assessment instruments before they are used and confirms schools’ marks by reviewing samples of student work. The QCAA has stated that external assessment results do not scale internal assessment results. The final subject result combines confirmed internal marks with the external assessment, which contributes 25 per cent in most subjects and 50 per cent in mathematics and science subjects.

South Australia and the NT

In South Australia and the NT, school assessment makes up 70 per cent of each Stage 2 subject and external assessment the other 30 per cent. School assessment is moderated to make sure grading is consistent with each subject’s performance standards across schools.

What moderation means for you

Because moderation usually preserves your order within your school group, your rank in your class matters. If you are near the top of your group in a subject, moderation will generally keep you there. It also explains why raw school marks can change before they reach your ATAR, and why any estimate built on raw school marks is approximate.

Step 1: Your subjects are scaled

The first step is scaling. Every subject you take is adjusted so that a result in one subject is worth the same as the same result in another. This is done by the admissions centre, not your school.

Scaling works by looking at how the students in a subject perform across all of their subjects. If the group taking a subject is strong overall, that subject scales up. If the group is broader, it scales down. Your own ranking within the subject never changes — scaling only changes how your score counts towards your ATAR.

A quick example: a raw mark of 40 in a subject with a very strong cohort might scale up to 44, while the same raw 40 in a broad-entry subject might scale down to 36. Same raw mark, different scaled value, because the competition differs.

Scaling in plain language

Think of each subject as a race run on a different track. Some tracks are full of very fast runners; others have a wider mix. Finishing fifth on the first track is a stronger performance than finishing fifth on the second. Scaling is the process that recognises this, so that a strong finish against strong competition counts accordingly.

The way scaling measures the strength of each track is by looking at how its runners performed on the other tracks they ran on. If the students in a subject did well across their other subjects, the subject’s cohort is judged to be strong, and its results are placed relatively high on the common scale.

Scaling keeps your order within the subject

Scaling never changes the order of students within a subject. If you are ahead of another student in Chemistry before scaling, you are still ahead after scaling. What changes is where the whole group’s results sit relative to other subjects.

Scaling differs along the range

Scaling is not a single number added to every student’s mark. It can lift results at the top of a subject more than in the middle, or the reverse, depending on how the students at each level performed in their other subjects. VTAC, for example, notes that the adjustment for a study is different at each study score. That is why the example above uses different scaled values for the same raw mark in different subjects, rather than a fixed bonus.

A note on the example figures

The figures in the example above, such as a raw 40 scaling to 44 in one subject and 36 in another, are illustrations of the principle. Real scaled values depend on the subject, the state, the year and the point in the range. Official scaling reports from admissions centres show the actual values for each year.

How scaling is actually done

You do not need to understand the mathematics of scaling to do well, but knowing the broad approach helps you trust the process and see through common myths.

Inter-subject comparison

Every state’s scaling compares subjects using the performance of the students who take them. Because students take several subjects, each student provides a link between the subjects they took. By looking at these links across tens of thousands of students, admissions centres can estimate how strong each subject’s cohort is relative to the others.

Iterative methods

Some states use iterative methods, which repeat a calculation until the results settle. QTAC, for example, describes its scaling as an iterative inter-subject process. In an iterative method, each subject’s scaled results are estimated, those estimates are used to recalculate the strength of every subject’s students, and the process repeats until the scaled values stop changing meaningfully.

Average marks scaling in Western Australia

TISC describes the scaling it uses for Western Australian ATAR courses as average marks scaling, applied after school marks have been moderated and combined with exam marks. The underlying idea is the same as elsewhere: each course’s results are placed on a common scale using information about how its students performed across their courses.

Scaling is recalculated every year

Because scaling depends on the students taking each subject, it is recalculated from scratch each year. Subjects with large, stable cohorts tend to scale in a similar way from year to year, while subjects with small or changing cohorts can move more. Our guide to how scaling changes from year to year explores this.

Special adjustments

In some states, policy adjustments are added to the scaling process. In Victoria, VTAC applies a policy adjustment to the scaling of languages. These adjustments are part of the published rules and apply equally to all students taking the relevant studies.

What scaling does not do

  • It does not reward a subject for difficult content.
  • It does not penalise a subject for being popular.
  • It does not change your order within a subject.
  • It does not add a fixed number of points to every student.
  • It does not depend on which school you attend.

A scaling example in slow motion

Scaling can feel abstract, so here is a simplified illustration of the reasoning behind it. Real scaling uses far more data and more sophisticated methods, but the logic is the same.

Two subjects, overlapping students

Imagine two subjects, Subject A and Subject B. Many students take both, along with several other subjects. In their raw marks, the average in Subject A is 65 and the average in Subject B is 70. On raw marks alone, Subject B looks like the place where students do better.

Looking at the students’ other results

Now look at how each group performs in their other subjects. Suppose the students taking Subject A have, on average, stronger results across their other subjects than the students taking Subject B. That suggests Subject A’s students are a stronger group overall, and that its lower raw average reflects tougher competition or tougher marking, not weaker students.

Placing both subjects on one scale

Scaling uses that information to place both subjects on a common scale. Subject A’s results are moved relatively higher, and Subject B’s relatively lower, so that a scaled result in either subject represents a similar level of performance relative to the whole student population. After scaling, a strong student in Subject A is not disadvantaged for choosing the subject with the lower raw average.

Why it repeats

Every subject is linked to others through the students who take them, so adjusting one subject affects the comparisons for the rest. Iterative methods repeat the adjustment across all subjects until the scaled values settle. That is the idea behind descriptions such as QTAC’s iterative inter-subject scaling.

What stays the same

Throughout this process, the order of students within each subject never changes. A student who finished ahead of another in Subject A remains ahead after scaling. Scaling only changes where each subject’s results sit relative to other subjects.

Why scaling exists

Scaling exists to be fair. Without it, a student who took easier subjects would have an advantage over one who took harder, more competitive subjects. Scaling removes that advantage, so no student is better or worse off simply because of the subjects they chose.

This is why the idea that “hard subjects scale up” is only half true. A subject scales up because the students in it are strong, not because the content is difficult. You can explore any subject’s real numbers with our HSC and VCE scaling calculators.

The practical lesson is freeing: you do not have to chase “high-scaling” subjects. You just have to do well in whatever you take, because scaling rewards your position, not the subject name.

Why fairness requires scaling

Without scaling, the simplest way to raise an aggregate would be to choose subjects where high marks are easier to earn relative to the competition. Students would be pushed towards subjects for strategic reasons rather than interest or strength, and students who chose more competitive subjects would be disadvantaged. Scaling removes that incentive. Its goal is that two students who perform equally well relative to the whole student population end up with similar scaled results, whatever subjects they chose.

The trap of chasing scaling

Because scaling reflects the strength of each subject’s students, a subject that scales well is one where you face stronger competition. If you choose such a subject but finish in the lower part of its cohort, your scaled result may be lower than if you had finished near the top of a subject that scales more modestly. Scaling can help a student who is genuinely strong in a high-scaling subject, but it rarely rescues a weak result.

Using scaling sensibly

Scaling is best used as a tie-breaker. If you are equally strong in and equally interested in two subjects, and both meet your course requirements, the one that tends to scale better at your level of result may be the slightly better choice. Look at several years of data, at the point in the range where you expect to finish. Our guides to the highest scaling subjects and to maths and English scaling show what the data looks like.

Step 2: Your best scores form an aggregate

Once your subjects are scaled, your best scaled results are added together into a number called an aggregate. The exact rule depends on your state. Some states use your best four or five subjects; some add a portion of a fifth or sixth.

The key point is that your strongest subjects are what count. A weak subject often counts for less, or does not count at all, once your better results are in. This is why taking a safety subject you can do well in is a sound strategy.

Because only your best count, dropping a weak subject rarely helps as much as students think. If it was not going to make your top results anyway, it was already doing little to your aggregate.

How each state forms the aggregate

  • NSW: scaled marks for your best 2 units of English plus your best 8 units from your remaining ATAR courses, with no more than 2 units of Category B courses. Scaled marks are out of 50 per unit, so the aggregate is out of 500.
  • Victoria: your best scaled study score from the English group plus your next three best scaled study scores, which form your primary four, plus 10 per cent of your fifth and sixth scaled study scores or an eligible VET increment. The aggregate can range from zero to over 210.
  • Queensland: the sum of your best five scaled results, from five General subjects or four General subjects plus one Applied subject or eligible VET qualification. The maximum is 500.
  • Western Australia: the sum of your best four scaled scores, plus 10 per cent of your best scaled score in a language other than English, 10 per cent of your scaled score in Mathematics Methods and 10 per cent of your scaled score in Mathematics Specialist. The maximum is 430.
  • South Australia and the NT: the scaled scores from your best 90 credits of eligible Stage 2 study, including at least 60 credits from 20-credit Tertiary Admission Subjects or valid pairs, and up to 20 credits of Recognised Studies. The aggregate is out of 90.
  • Tasmania: from 2025, your best eligible Level 3 and Level 4 course scores over up to two years, each worth up to 15 points, totalling 60 to 75 points from four or five courses.
  • ACT: your best three major scaled scores plus 60 per cent of your next major or minor.

Why “best” matters so much

Counting only your best results has two big effects. First, it protects you: a single weak subject does not necessarily drag your aggregate down, provided you have enough stronger results to replace it. Second, it focuses attention on the subjects that do count. If you know which of your subjects are likely to be in your best results, you can prioritise them, while still working steadily in the others in case your results change.

Partial contributions

In some states, results outside your main counted group still contribute a little. In Victoria, your fifth and sixth studies each contribute 10 per cent of their scaled scores. In Western Australia, bonuses for a language other than English and for advanced mathematics are added even if those courses are not among your best four. These partial contributions can make a small but real difference.

Special rules: combinations, limits and bonuses

Beyond the basic “best results” rules, each state has detailed rules that affect how particular combinations of subjects are treated. These rarely matter for most students, but when they do, they can change an aggregate noticeably.

NSW: categories and subject areas

In NSW, no more than 2 units of Category B courses can be included in the ATAR calculation, and eligibility requires at least 8 units of Category A courses. Eligibility also requires courses from at least four subject areas and at least three Board Developed courses of 2 units or more. UAC also has rules about how related courses, such as different levels of mathematics, are counted. NESA notes that English Studies only counts towards the ATAR if the student sits the optional HSC exam.

Victoria: mathematics and the English group

In Victoria, VTAC has rules about combinations of related studies. For example, if a student takes all three VCE mathematics studies, the lowest-scoring one can only contribute as an increment. The English group includes English, English as an Additional Language, English Language and Literature, and the best scaled score from the group counts in the primary four.

Queensland: one Applied subject or VET

In Queensland, only one Applied subject or one eligible VET qualification can be included in the best five. Students also need a C or better in an English subject to be eligible, but English only counts towards the TEA if it is among the best five.

Western Australia: unacceptable combinations

In Western Australia, some combinations of ATAR mathematics courses are subject to unacceptable combination rules, which limit how they can be counted. The bonuses for a language other than English and for Mathematics Methods and Mathematics Specialist apply regardless of whether those courses are in a student’s best four.

South Australia and the NT: credits and attempts

In South Australia and the NT, at least 60 of the 90 credits must come from 20-credit Tertiary Admission Subjects, or valid pairs of 10-credit subjects counted in place of a 20-credit subject. Up to 20 credits of Recognised Studies can be included in the flexible option, and the results used must come from a limited number of attempts.

Check your combination
If your subject combination is unusual, includes several related courses, or includes VET or Recognised Studies, check your admissions centre’s rules or ask your careers adviser how it will be counted.

How VET and other studies are counted

Vocational education and training can contribute to the ATAR in several states, within limits. The rules differ, so it is worth knowing how your state treats VET before you choose a course.

NSW

Some NSW VET courses are Board Developed courses that can count towards the ATAR. For these, students generally need to sit the optional HSC exam for their marks to contribute. Most such VET courses are Category B, so no more than 2 units of Category B courses can be included in the ATAR calculation.

Victoria

Scored VCE VET programs can count as studies in the ATAR calculation. Some VET programs without a study score can contribute an increment, worth 10 per cent of the student’s fourth primary scaled score, in place of a fifth or sixth study.

Queensland

One eligible VET qualification at Certificate III or higher can be one of the five results used to calculate the TEA, alongside four General subjects.

South Australia and the NT

VET can contribute as Recognised Studies, up to 20 credits in the flexible option. SATAC uses a scaled score for VET based on the average of the student’s first 70 credits of Tertiary Admission Subjects.

Western Australia

The Western Australian ATAR is calculated from scaled scores in ATAR courses. Vocational qualifications can provide other routes into some university courses, but they do not form part of the TEA itself.

If you are taking VET, ask your school how it will be treated in your state, and check whether the calculator you use models it correctly.

How common types of subject feed into the calculation

Different kinds of subject interact with the calculation in different ways. Knowing how your own subjects are treated helps you understand where your ATAR will come from.

English courses

English has a special role in most states. In NSW, your best 2 units of English must be included in your aggregate. In Victoria, your best scaled score from the English group must be in your primary four. In Queensland, a C or better in an English subject is required for eligibility, though English only counts if it is among your best five. When states offer several English courses, each is scaled according to its own cohort, so more demanding courses often scale more strongly, but only help students who perform well in them.

Mathematics courses

Mathematics is not compulsory for the ATAR in most states, but it interacts with the calculation in several ways. Advanced mathematics courses often attract strong cohorts and scale well at higher levels of result. Some states have rules about counting several mathematics courses together: in Victoria, if a student takes all three mathematics studies, the lowest can only contribute as an increment, and in Western Australia some mathematics combinations are subject to unacceptable combination rules. Western Australia also adds bonuses of 10 per cent of the scaled scores in Mathematics Methods and Mathematics Specialist to the TEA.

Sciences

Science subjects are scaled according to their cohorts like any other subject. In Queensland, mathematics and science subjects have external assessment worth 50 per cent of the result, compared with 25 per cent in most other General subjects, so the external assessment carries more weight in these subjects.

Humanities and social sciences

History, economics, legal studies, geography and similar subjects are scaled in the same way. Several attract strong cohorts and can contribute well to an aggregate. Their position varies by state and year.

Languages

Languages often have small cohorts, which can make their scaling less predictable from year to year. Some states treat languages specially: VTAC applies a policy adjustment to the scaling of languages in Victoria, and Western Australia adds 10 per cent of the best scaled score in a language other than English to the TEA. Several states offer separate language courses for students with different levels of background in a language.

Creative and performance subjects

Arts, music, drama and design subjects often include practical components, folios or major works alongside written assessment. They are scaled like any other subject, and students who perform very well in them can achieve high ATARs. Their practical components can make results harder to predict.

Extension subjects

In NSW, extension courses are usually 1 unit and reported out of 50. They count as single units within the best 10 units used for the ATAR. Extension courses often attract strong cohorts, but they also add workload.

Applied and vocational subjects

In Queensland, one Applied subject or eligible VET qualification can be included in the best five. In NSW, some VET courses can count if the optional HSC exam is sat, usually as Category B courses, with at most 2 units of Category B courses included. In Victoria, scored VCE VET programs can count as studies, and some unscored VET can contribute an increment. In South Australia and the NT, VET can contribute as Recognised Studies.

The English requirement

In most states, you must have a result in an English subject for it to count towards your ATAR. This does not mean English has to be one of your best subjects. It means at least one English study needs to be part of the mix.

The rules vary. Victoria, for example, requires an English study in the aggregate. Queensland requires you to complete an English subject to be eligible for an ATAR, though it only counts if it is among your best five. Other states have their own version.

Whatever your state, the message is the same: take English seriously, because you cannot avoid it. Aim for the best English result you can, even if it is not your favourite subject.

English in each state, in more detail

  • NSW: your best 2 units of English must be included in your aggregate, even if they are among your lowest units. UAC’s own explanation shows that English counts whether or not it would otherwise be among your best results.
  • Victoria: your best scaled study score from the English group must be one of your primary four.
  • Queensland: you must achieve a C or better in an English subject to be eligible for an ATAR, but English is only included in your TEA if it is among your best five scaled results.
  • Western Australia: English is not compulsory in the TEA, but universities generally require evidence of English competence for admission, which can be met through ATAR English or related courses.
  • South Australia and the NT: the SACE and NTCET include literacy requirements, and your university aggregate is built from your best eligible credits.
  • Tasmania and the ACT: certificate and tertiary entrance requirements apply, and your aggregate is built from your best eligible courses.

Why English gets special treatment

English is treated differently because strong communication skills are relevant to almost every university course. Requiring English in the aggregate, or as a condition of eligibility, ensures that every ATAR reflects some achievement in English.

Choosing your English course

Most states offer more than one English course. More demanding courses often scale more strongly, because they tend to attract students who are strong across all their subjects, but that only helps if you perform well in them. The right choice depends on where you are likely to rank, your interest and the requirements of courses you are considering. In NSW, for example, our guides to English Advanced and English Standard work through the trade-off.

Why English deserves real effort

In NSW and Victoria, a weak English result has a direct cost, because it must count. In Queensland, failing to reach a C in English removes ATAR eligibility altogether. Treat English as one of your most important subjects, and work on essay structure, close reading and timed writing throughout the year.

Five students, five rules in action

These short illustrative cases each show one rule of the calculation at work. The students are invented, and the figures are simplified.

Liam and the English rule

Liam, a NSW student, is strongest in mathematics and sciences, and his English Standard result is his weakest. He hoped English would be left out of his ATAR. Because NSW always includes the best 2 units of English, it counts. Liam’s aggregate would have been higher if he had put more time into English during the year, because every mark he gained there would have counted directly.

Ruby and the Category B limit

Ruby, also in NSW, takes two VET courses that are Category B, both with strong results, and sits the optional HSC exams so they can count. Only 2 units of Category B courses can be included in her ATAR, so one of the two courses cannot count, however strong its result. Her best 8 units outside English include one Category B course and her best Category A courses.

Ethan and three mathematics studies

Ethan, a Victorian student, takes General Mathematics, Mathematical Methods and Specialist Mathematics. Under VTAC’s rules, if a student takes all three, the lowest-scoring one can only contribute as an increment. Ethan’s two higher mathematics scores can be in his primary four, but his third contributes only 10 per cent.

Chloe and the WA bonus

Chloe, a Western Australian student, takes Mathematics Methods, but her best four scaled scores come from other courses. She assumed Methods would not affect her ATAR. In fact, 10 per cent of her Methods scaled score is added to her TEA as a bonus, so her Methods result raised her TEA after all.

Noah and the Queensland English requirement

Noah, a Queensland student, has strong results in five General subjects but struggles in English. Queensland requires a C or better in an English subject for ATAR eligibility, even though English only counts in the TEA if it is among the best five. Noah works hard in English to make sure he meets the requirement, while his other five subjects form his TEA.

What these cases show

Each case turns on a rule that is easy to overlook: compulsory English, limits on certain courses, rules for combinations, bonuses outside the best results and eligibility conditions. Checking your own state’s rules can prevent unpleasant surprises.

Step 3: Your aggregate becomes a rank

The final step turns your aggregate into your ATAR. Every student’s aggregate is placed in order, from highest to lowest. Your position in that order is your percentile rank, and that is your ATAR.

Because it is a rank, your ATAR depends on how everyone else did, not just on your own aggregate. The student with the highest aggregate receives 99.95. The rest are spread down the scale to 0.00, in steps of 0.05.

This is also why your ATAR can only be finalised after every student’s results are in. Until then, no one knows the exact order, so no one can know the exact ranks.

Who you are ranked against

The ATAR is designed to show your position relative to your whole age group in your state, not just relative to students who receive an ATAR. The age group includes young people who left school early or did not complete enough eligible subjects. Admissions centres use information about the age group to make sure each ATAR represents the same position in that population each year.

This has an important consequence. Because students who receive an ATAR tend to be stronger academically than the age group as a whole, the typical ATAR among recipients is well above 50. UAC reported a median ATAR of 70.75 in NSW in 2025, and VTAC reported an average of 69.52 in Victoria in 2024.

Many students share the top rank

The top ATAR of 99.95 is not reserved for a single student. It is the highest band on the scale, and every student whose position falls in that band receives it. In 2025, 53 students received 99.95 in NSW, 42 in Victoria and 37 in Queensland.

Each step is a band

ATARs are reported in steps of 0.05, giving 2,000 possible values from 0.00 to 99.95. Queensland’s admissions centre describes these as 2,000 bands. Each band represents roughly the same share of the age group, but the number of ATAR recipients in each band differs, because recipients are not spread evenly across the age group’s results.

Low ranks are not reported exactly

ATARs at the lower end of the scale are usually not reported as exact values. In Queensland, for example, ATARs of 30 or below are reported as “30.00 or less”. In South Australia, ATARs below 30.00 are not shown on the Tertiary Entrance Statement.

Why the ranking waits for every result

Because each ATAR depends on the whole set of aggregates, the ranking can only be done once every student’s results have been finalised, scaled and combined. That is the main reason ATARs are released a little after subject results in some states, and why no one can calculate an exact ATAR in advance.

How an aggregate becomes an ATAR, in more detail

The final step, turning aggregates into ATARs, is the part students see least of. Here is what happens in more detail.

Ranking the aggregates

Once every ATAR-eligible student’s aggregate has been calculated, the aggregates are placed in order. The task is then to express each student’s position as a rank relative to the whole age group in the state.

Relating the ranking to the age group

Not everyone in the age group receives an ATAR. Admissions centres take this into account so that each ATAR reflects a position in the whole age group, not just among ATAR recipients. That keeps the meaning of each ATAR stable from year to year, even when the share of students receiving an ATAR changes.

Reporting in bands

Positions are reported in bands of 0.05, from 0.00 to 99.95, giving 2,000 possible values. Students whose positions fall in the same band receive the same ATAR. Each band represents roughly the same share of the age group.

Conversion tables

The result of this process can be summarised as a table that shows which aggregates correspond to which ATARs in a particular year. Some admissions centres publish such a table: VTAC releases one on results day, and SATAC publishes a university aggregate to ATAR conversion table. These tables are what ATAR calculators use, from past years, to estimate your rank.

Why the same aggregate can mean different ATARs in different years

Because the conversion depends on the current year’s results, the same aggregate can correspond to a slightly different ATAR in different years. In large states, the differences are usually small, but they are one reason no one can know your exact ATAR before results are released.

Percentiles and the age group, explained

The last step of the calculation expresses your aggregate as a rank against your age group. Two ideas make this step clear.

Percentiles

A percentile describes the share of a group at or below a point. If you are at the 80th percentile, about 80 per cent of the group is at or below your position. The ATAR works in a similar way: an ATAR of 80 means you performed better than about 80 per cent of your age group.

The age group

The group the ATAR compares you with is your age group in your state, including people who do not receive an ATAR. Students who receive an ATAR tend to be stronger academically than the age group as a whole, so the typical ATAR among recipients is around 70 rather than 50. In NSW in 2025, UAC reported a median of 70.75, and about 34.6 per cent of recipients had an ATAR of 80 or above, even though 80 represents the top 20 per cent of the age group.

Why this design?

Anchoring the ATAR to the age group gives it a stable meaning from year to year. If it were based only on recipients, its meaning would change whenever more or fewer students chose an ATAR pathway.

A worked example, step by step

Let us follow one student through all three steps. Say their raw marks are: English 82, Maths 78, Chemistry 85, Economics 80 and Geography 70.

Step 1, scaling. After scaling, Chemistry and Maths rise a little because of strong cohorts, English holds steady, and Geography drops slightly. The scaled results might be English 40, Maths 41, Chemistry 43, Economics 39 and Geography 33 (out of 50).

Step 2, aggregate. The state takes the best of these. Chemistry, Maths, English and Economics do the work; Geography, the weakest, contributes little or nothing once the others are counted.

Step 3, rank. That aggregate is compared with every other student’s. If it sits above 89% of them, the ATAR is about 89.00. The strong, consistent results across four subjects carried the rank — not any single top mark.

What the example leaves out

The worked example above simplifies the process to make the three steps clear. In a real calculation, a few more details apply.

  • Moderation comes first. The raw marks would first be combined with exam results and, where school marks are involved, moderated before scaling.
  • State rules decide what counts. Whether Geography counts depends on the state’s rules and how many subjects or units the student has. In NSW, for example, a 2-unit course outside the best 10 units does not count at all.
  • English may be compulsory. In NSW and Victoria, English would be included even if it were the student’s weakest subject.
  • The rank is relative to the age group. The final step places the aggregate within the student’s age group in their state, rather than simply among students who received an ATAR.

The lesson of the example

The example’s main message holds in every state: strong, consistent results across the subjects that count produce a high ATAR. No single top mark is required, and a weaker subject may count for little or nothing if there are enough stronger results.

How the states differ

The three steps are national, but the details are set by each admissions centre. NSW builds an aggregate from ten units of scaled marks through UAC. Victoria combines an English study with your next best three, plus increments, through VTAC. Queensland uses QTAC and takes your best five scaled results. Why the details differ

Each state’s ATAR calculation grew out of its own senior certificate, with its own subjects, assessment and history. The admissions centres have agreed that the final ATAR should mean the same thing nationally, but the route to it reflects each state’s system. That is why NSW talks about units, Victoria about study scores, Queensland and Western Australia about the TEA, South Australia about credits and Tasmania about the TE Score.

NSW in detail

NSW students complete the HSC, set and examined by NESA, and UAC calculates their ATAR.

From assessment to HSC mark

For each course, your school’s assessment marks are moderated using your school group’s exam results, keeping your order within the group. Your HSC mark is then the average of your moderated assessment mark and your exam mark. Most courses are reported out of 100, and 1-unit extension courses out of 50.

Scaling and the aggregate

UAC scales each course’s marks and reports scaled marks per unit, out of 50. Your aggregate is the sum of the scaled marks for your best 2 units of English and your best 8 units from your remaining ATAR courses, with no more than 2 units of Category B courses. The maximum aggregate is 500.

Eligibility

You need at least 10 units of ATAR courses, including at least 8 units of Category A courses, 2 units of English, three Board Developed courses of 2 units or more and courses from at least four subject areas.

In numbers

In 2025, UAC reported 60,432 ATAR recipients, a median ATAR of 70.75 and 53 students at 99.95. UAC also explains that most HSC courses have a median mark above 75, while students with marks around 70 typically receive ATARs in the 50s and marks around 80 typically lead to an ATAR around 80. Our guide to how the NSW ATAR is calculated goes further.

The Limited ATAR

UAC explains that some institutions allow certain applicants who complete between 5 and 9 units of ATAR courses, including at least 1 unit of English, to use a Limited ATAR. It is calculated from the best 5 units of courses completed rather than the usual 10. Check whether the institutions you are interested in accept it before relying on it.

Estimating in NSW

To estimate a NSW ATAR, you need an estimate of each HSC mark, which means estimating both your moderated assessment mark and your exam mark. Your rank within your school group and your trial exam results are useful guides. Our HSC ATAR calculator and our guide to converting HSC marks to an ATAR can help, and our HSC scaling calculators show how individual courses scale.

Common NSW questions

Students often ask whether extension courses help, whether English Standard or Advanced is better for their ATAR, and whether a VET course will count. Extension courses count as single units within the best 10 and can help if results are strong. The better English course is the one in which you are likely to rank higher, given your interest and course requirements. VET courses count only if they are Board Developed and the optional HSC exam is sat, and usually as Category B courses.

Victoria in detail

Victorian students complete the VCE, administered by the VCAA, and VTAC calculates their ATAR.

From assessment to study score

Each VCE study combines school-assessed coursework and examinations, with school-based assessment statistically moderated using exam performance. VTAC scales each study score, applying a policy adjustment to languages. You need study scores in at least four eligible studies.

VTAC reported 49,310 ATARs for 2025 and 42 students at 99.95, and an average ATAR of 69.52 in 2024. VTAC releases the table converting aggregates to ATARs on results day. Our guide to how the Victorian ATAR is calculated goes further.

VTAC uses your best scaled score from the English group, which includes English, English as an Additional Language, English Language and Literature. Estimating in Victoria

To estimate a Victorian ATAR, you need an estimate of each study score. Queensland students complete the QCE, administered by the QCAA, and QTAC calculates their ATAR.

How the calculation has changed

The ATAR calculation has evolved as states have reformed their senior systems.

The move to a national name

Before the ATAR, states used their own ranks: the UAI in NSW and the ACT, the ENTER in Victoria, and the TER in South Australia, the NT, Western Australia and Tasmania. These were replaced by the ATAR from 2009 and 2010.

Queensland’s change

Queensland used the OP, which placed students in 25 bands, until it moved to the ATAR for students completing Year 12 in 2020. The change came with broader reforms, including external assessment in General subjects and a new approach to scaling by QTAC.

Tasmania’s TE Score from 2025

Tasmania changed its TE Score rules for students from 2025, building the score from the best eligible Level 3 and Level 4 course scores over up to two years.

Ongoing adjustments

States and admissions centres continue to refine their rules, for example in how particular courses are counted. That is why it is important to check current information rather than relying on older advice. Our guide to the ATAR, the OP and the ENTER covers the history in more depth.

How many subjects count towards your ATAR?

Most states build your ATAR from your best four to six subjects. NSW counts ten units, which is usually five subjects. Victoria uses your English study plus your next best three, with a small contribution from a fifth and sixth. Queensland uses your best five.

The practical takeaway is to take at least one more subject than the minimum. That gives you a safety margin, so one bad result does not sink your ATAR.

Minimums and buffers, state by state

  • NSW: you need at least 10 units of ATAR courses for eligibility. Many students take 11 or 12 units, so that their weakest units can be left out if necessary.
  • Victoria: you need at least four study scores, and your aggregate can include up to six results. Taking five or six studies lets you benefit from increments and protects your primary four.
  • Queensland: you need five eligible results. Many students take six General subjects, so that their sixth can replace a weaker result.
  • Western Australia: you need at least four ATAR courses. Taking five or more gives you a buffer for your best four, and taking a language or advanced mathematics can add a bonus.
  • South Australia and the NT: you need at least 90 credits of eligible Stage 2 study. Taking more than the minimum allows SATAC to choose your best combination.
  • Tasmania: four or five eligible Level 3 and Level 4 courses contribute, and courses completed over two years can count.

When a buffer subject is not worth it

An extra subject only helps if you can do reasonably well in it without harming your other results. If it takes so much time that your counted subjects suffer, it can lower your ATAR indirectly. Weigh the protection an extra subject gives against the time it will take.

Dropping a subject

Because only your best results count, dropping a subject is rarely the boost students hope for. It can also remove your safety net. If you are thinking of dropping a subject, check your state’s minimum requirements, consider whether that subject could still end up among your best results, and talk to your teachers first.

Accelerated study, repeats and results from more than one year

Not every student completes all their ATAR subjects in a single year. Some accelerate one or more subjects, some repeat a subject or a year, and some states deliberately count results from two years. Here is how the calculation handles these situations.

Accelerated subjects

Students who complete a final-year subject early can often include that result in their ATAR, subject to their state’s rules. In NSW, UAC’s rules allow courses completed in different years to be included in certain circumstances. In Victoria, Units 3 and 4 of a study completed before the final year can contribute in the usual way.

Tasmania’s two-year design

In Tasmania, the TE Score is designed to include eligible Level 3 and Level 4 courses completed over up to two years, in Years 11, 12 or 13. Results from Year 11 courses can therefore count directly.

Repeating subjects or Year 12

Students who repeat may have results from more than one year. Each state has rules about which results can count and how they are combined. In South Australia and the NT, for example, the results used for the university aggregate must come from a limited number of attempts.

Check before you plan

If you are considering acceleration or repeating, ask your school and admissions centre how your results will be treated, and whether there are time limits on accumulating results.

Why you cannot calculate the exact number yourself

Students often ask for a formula to work out their exact ATAR. There is not one you can run at home, and that is not a flaw — it is how a rank works.

Your ATAR depends on how every other student performs, and on the exact scaling for that year. Both are only known once all results are finalised. That is why even good calculators give an estimate: they apply last year’s scaling to your marks, which is close, but not the final answer. See how accurate ATAR calculators are.

The pieces that are unknown until results day

  • Your final subject results. Your exam results and the final combination with moderated school marks are not known until results are released.
  • This year’s scaling. Scaling is recalculated each year from the current students’ results.
  • This year’s conversion. The relationship between aggregates and ATARs depends on the current year’s results and age group. VTAC, for example, releases its aggregate-to-ATAR table on results day.

How estimates work despite this

A calculator can still give a useful estimate by applying the most recent published scaling and conversion data to the marks you enter. For large, stable subjects in large states, last year’s patterns are usually a reasonable guide. The estimate is most useful when you enter realistic marks and treat the result as a range rather than a single number.

Why a range is more honest

An estimate shown as a single figure with two decimal places suggests a precision that does not exist before results. A range, such as “likely between 82 and 86”, reflects the uncertainty in your marks, in scaling and in the conversion. Plan your preferences across your whole range.

Building your own rough estimate

If you want to understand the calculation better, try building a rough estimate by hand. Find your admissions centre’s latest scaling data, look up the scaled result for each of your subjects at your expected level, combine them under your state’s rules and, where a conversion table is published, convert the total to an ATAR. Compare your answer with a calculator. Our guide on reading a scaling report shows how.

Reading a scaling report, briefly

Admissions centres publish scaling reports after each year’s results. They are long, but you usually only need a few parts.

Find your subjects

Most reports include tables for each subject showing how results at different points convert to scaled results. Look for your subjects and the point in the range closest to your expected result.

Look at more than one point

Because scaling varies along the range, compare the scaled values at several points: near the top, in the middle and lower down. That shows you how the subject’s scaling changes along its range.

Compare years

Look at the same subject in several years’ reports. If its scaled values are similar each year, the subject is stable. If they move noticeably, treat any single year with caution.

Find the conversion table if there is one

Some admissions centres publish a table converting aggregates to ATARs. VTAC releases one on results day, and SATAC publishes a university aggregate to ATAR conversion table. These show how aggregates related to ATARs in a particular year.

Remember what the report cannot tell you

A report describes a past year. It cannot tell you exactly how your subjects will scale this year or what your ATAR will be. Our full guide on how to read a scaling report walks through an example.

What you can actually control

You cannot control scaling or how other students perform. What you can control is your own ranking within each subject. That is what scaling rewards.

So the winning strategy is simple: choose subjects you can do well in, take one or two extra for safety, and aim to finish as high as you can within each. A strong rank in an ordinary-scaling subject beats a weak rank in a high-scaling one, every time.

Five practical ways to lift your rank in each subject

  1. Know the assessment structure. Find out how much each task and the exam contribute in each subject, and plan your effort accordingly.
  2. Use feedback relentlessly. After each task, ask how your response could have earned more marks, and apply that advice to the next one.
  3. Practise full exams. Complete past papers under timed, closed-book conditions and mark them against official guidelines or ask a teacher to mark them.
  4. Focus on your counted subjects. Use a calculator to see which subjects are likely to count and which move your estimate most, while keeping every subject strong in case your results change.
  5. Protect your energy. Consistent sleep, breaks and exercise support concentration and memory across a long year.

Your rank in your school group matters

Because moderation usually preserves your order within your school group, improving your position in your class can feed directly into your moderated school marks. Every assessment task is a chance to move up.

Choose subjects you can rank well in

Your result in each subject depends on where you finish among the students taking it. Choosing subjects you are good at and enjoy is the most reliable way to finish high within them. Scaling should be a tie-breaker, not the main reason for a choice.

Adjustments are within your control too

Your ATAR is set by your results, but your selection rank can be higher if you are eligible for adjustment factors. Check the schemes at each university you are interested in and apply on time for any that require an application.

What-if scenarios: how changes flow through the calculation

Thinking through a few scenarios shows how the calculation responds to changes in your results.

What if one counted subject drops by a few marks?

Your scaled result for that subject falls, so your aggregate falls. If you have a spare result that can take its place, and it is stronger than the reduced result, the spare may replace it and soften the fall. Otherwise, the full drop flows into your aggregate.

What if a subject that does not currently count improves?

Nothing changes until it rises above your weakest counted result. Once it does, it replaces that result, and your aggregate rises by the difference between the two scaled results.

What if English is your weakest subject in NSW or Victoria?

It still counts. In NSW, your 2 units of English are included regardless, and in Victoria your English-group score is in your primary four. Improving English therefore has a direct effect on your aggregate in these states.

What if you take Mathematics Methods in Western Australia but it is not in your best four?

You still receive a bonus of 10 per cent of its scaled score, added to your TEA. Improving your Methods result therefore still raises your TEA, even if Methods never enters your best four.

What if you take a fifth and sixth study in Victoria?

Each contributes 10 per cent of its scaled study score as an increment. Improving them raises your aggregate a little, and if one of them rises above a study in your primary four, it can move into the primary four and count in full.

What if your aggregate sits in the middle of the range?

The middle of the age group’s distribution is crowded, so a small change in your aggregate can move your ATAR more there than near the top, where students are more spread out.

Study strategy that follows from the calculation

Understanding how the ATAR is calculated suggests some practical study strategies.

Prioritise the subjects that count

Identify which subjects are likely to be among your counted results, including compulsory English where it applies. Give them consistent attention, while keeping your other subjects strong in case your results change.

Value every assessment

Because school assessment contributes to your results in most states, and moderation generally preserves your order within your school group, every assessment task is a chance to improve your position.

Prepare seriously for external assessment

External assessment carries substantial weight: half of each HSC mark in NSW, half of each combined score in Western Australia, 30 per cent of each SACE Stage 2 subject, and 25 or 50 per cent of Queensland General subjects. Practise full exams under timed conditions.

Aim for consistency

The ATAR rewards strong results across several subjects more than one outstanding result. Consistency also protects you if one subject goes less well than expected.

Look after yourself

Sleep, regular breaks and exercise support concentration and memory. A sustainable routine usually produces better results across the year than bursts of intense study followed by exhaustion.

Choosing subjects with the calculation in mind

The calculation has several implications for subject choice.

Meet the eligibility rules

Make sure your subjects meet your state’s ATAR requirements: the right number of eligible subjects, units or credits, compulsory English where it applies, and any limits such as NSW’s Category B rule.

Choose subjects you can rank well in

Your scaled result in each subject depends on where you finish among its students. Choosing subjects you are good at and enjoy is the most reliable way to finish high.

Meet course prerequisites

Check the prerequisites and assumed knowledge for courses you might want to study. A missing prerequisite can close a door regardless of your ATAR.

Consider a buffer

Where your state counts only your best results, an extra subject can protect you if one goes badly, provided it does not take too much time from your other subjects.

Use scaling as a tie-breaker

If you are equally strong in and equally interested in two subjects, scaling at your expected level can help you decide. It should not override your strengths and interests. Our guides to scaling subjects in NSW, Victoria, Queensland and Western Australia show what the data looks like.

What teachers see that the calculation does not

The ATAR calculation uses only your results. Your teachers see much more: how you approach problems, how you respond to feedback, which skills you are developing and where you could improve. That knowledge is valuable in two ways. First, it helps you improve the results that feed into the calculation. Second, it helps you make realistic estimates, because teachers know how students with similar work have performed in past years. Use your teachers’ insight alongside any calculator.

What the three show

The ATAR responds differently to changes at different points on the scale, and what matters most depends on your goals. For all three students, the best strategy is the same: strong results in the subjects that count and a preference list built around a realistic range.

The ATAR is a rank, not your marks

A common misunderstanding is that the ATAR is an average of your marks. It is not. The ATAR is a rank that shows how you finished compared with other students in your state.

So an ATAR of 90 does not mean you scored 90 per cent. It means you finished ahead of about 90 per cent of your age group. This is why strong marks in a subject do not translate directly into an ATAR number, and why the two should not be confused.

Why marks and ATARs drift apart

Marks and ATARs are on different scales for three reasons. First, marks are not adjusted for the strength of each subject’s students; scaled results are. Second, the ATAR only uses your counted results, under your state’s rules. Third, the ATAR is a rank against your age group, so it depends on how results are spread across the whole population.

UAC gives a clear illustration for NSW. Most HSC courses have a median mark above 75, while the median ATAR is usually around 70. Students with marks around 70 typically receive ATARs in the 50s, and students with marks around 80 typically receive an ATAR around 80. So a student who averages 72 has not “got a 72”; they are in a crowded part of the distribution where many students have similar marks.

Reading an ATAR correctly

Read your ATAR as a position: the approximate percentage of your age group in your state you performed better than. An ATAR of 76 means better than about 76 per cent. Among students who receive an ATAR, the typical result is around 70, so 76 is above the middle of that group.

Ranks and percentiles

The ATAR works in a similar way to a percentile rank. Because results bunch up in the middle of the age group, a small change in your aggregate can move your ATAR by more in the middle of the scale than near the top, where students are more spread out.

How sensitive the ATAR is at different points

The same change in your aggregate does not move your ATAR by the same amount everywhere on the scale.

In the middle

Many students have aggregates in the middle of the distribution. In that crowded region, a small rise in your aggregate can move you past many students, so your ATAR can rise noticeably. This is why small improvements in your results can matter a great deal for students in the middle of the range.

Near the top

Near the top, students are more spread out, so the same rise in aggregate moves you past fewer students and changes your ATAR by less. However, each step near the top separates a very small group, and competitive courses can have thresholds separated by fractions of a point.

What this means for you

Wherever you are on the scale, improvements in the subjects that count help. In the middle of the range, they may move your ATAR more than you expect. Near the top, they can decide which side of a competitive threshold you land on.

After the calculation: selection ranks and offers

Your ATAR is the end of the calculation, but not the end of the process. When you apply for courses, universities use a selection rank.

From ATAR to selection rank

Your selection rank for a course is your ATAR plus any adjustment factors you are eligible for at that institution. Adjustments may be given for strong results in particular subjects, for regional or location-based reasons, for educational disadvantage, for elite sporting or artistic performance, or through school recommendation schemes. Your ATAR itself does not change. Our guides to selection rank versus ATAR and how selection ranks are calculated explain more.

From selection rank to offer

For most courses, applicants are ranked by selection rank and offers are made from the top down until the places for each round are filled. You receive an offer for the highest preference you are eligible for. The lowest selection rank that receives an offer becomes that round’s published threshold.

Additional criteria

Some courses also use admissions tests, interviews, portfolios, auditions or personal statements, and all courses check prerequisites. For these courses, the ATAR is part of the picture rather than the whole of it.

Adjustment factors, in more detail

Adjustment factors are not part of the ATAR calculation, but they are the next step for almost every applicant, so they are worth understanding.

Main types

  • Subject adjustments for strong results in particular subjects, often relevant to the course.
  • Regional and location adjustments for students who live or study in particular areas.
  • Equity adjustments for students whose education has been affected by circumstances such as hardship, illness or disrupted schooling, through schemes such as UAC’s Educational Access Schemes, VTAC’s Special Entry Access Scheme and QTAC’s Educational Access Scheme.
  • Elite athlete and performer adjustments for students competing or performing at a high level.
  • School recommendation schemes, which consider school recommendations and other criteria, sometimes before ATARs are released.

Caps and conditions

Most institutions cap the total adjustment a student can receive for a course, and some courses do not accept certain adjustments. Some adjustments are applied automatically, while others require an application with supporting documents and a deadline.

Interstate, IB and other qualifications

Interstate applicants

Your ATAR is calculated by the admissions centre in the state where you completed Year 12, and it is used directly by universities in other states. You do not need to convert it. Admissions centres pass ATARs between each other, and they are treated as equivalent.

Students who complete the International Baccalaureate Diploma receive an IB score rather than an ATAR. Admissions centres publish tables converting IB scores into a rank comparable with the ATAR, so IB students can compete on an equivalent basis.

Some schools outside Australia teach an Australian senior curriculum. Students who complete these programs may receive an ATAR, sometimes reported as a notional ATAR, depending on the program and the admissions centre.

Other overseas qualifications

Students with other overseas qualifications are usually assessed by universities using their own entry requirements for each qualification, often alongside English language requirements. They do not receive an ATAR.

Calculation myths

“The ATAR is your average mark”

The ATAR is a rank built from scaled results under your state’s rules. An average raw mark ignores scaling, counting rules and the conversion to a rank.

“Scaling adds a fixed number of points”

Scaling varies along each subject’s range and changes every year. A headline figure for a subject may not apply at your level of result.

“Every subject you take counts”

In most states, only your best results count, with some rules about compulsory subjects. A weaker subject may contribute little or nothing.

“Bonus points are part of the ATAR”

In most states, bonus points are adjustment factors added to your selection rank for particular courses. Western Australia’s language and mathematics bonuses are an exception, because they are built into the TEA used to calculate the ATAR.

“Only one student can get 99.95”

The top ATAR is a band, and every student whose position falls in it receives 99.95. In 2025, 53 NSW students did.

“Your school changes your ATAR”

Your school’s marks are moderated, but no bonus or penalty is applied to your ATAR for the school you attend. Every student in a state is ranked under the same rules.

“You can calculate your exact ATAR in advance”

Scaling and the conversion from aggregate to ATAR are recalculated each year, so any advance calculation is an estimate. Our article on common ATAR myths covers many more.

More misunderstandings about the calculation

“Moderation lowers good students’ marks”

Moderation adjusts a school group’s marks to match how the group performed in the exam. It can move marks up or down. It generally preserves each student’s order within the group, so a strong student near the top of their group remains near the top.

“The exam is all that counts”

In every state, school assessment contributes to subject results, alongside external assessment in most subjects. In South Australia, school assessment is 70 per cent of each Stage 2 subject, and in Queensland internal assessment is 75 per cent of most General subjects.

“A subject’s scaling tells you its effect on your ATAR”

Scaling tells you how results in a subject convert to scaled results. The effect on your ATAR depends on whether the subject counts, your other results and where your aggregate sits in the distribution.

“Higher aggregates always mean much higher ATARs”

The relationship between aggregate and ATAR is not a straight line. In the crowded middle, small aggregate changes move the ATAR more; near the top, they move it less.

“The calculation favours certain subjects”

The calculation applies the same rules to every subject. Scaling reflects each subject’s students, not a preference for particular subjects. Policy adjustments, such as Victoria’s for languages, and Western Australia’s bonuses are published rules that apply equally to every student taking the relevant courses.

Practical mistakes students make about the calculation

Some misunderstandings about the calculation lead to practical mistakes in subject choice, study priorities or planning. Here are the most common.

Miscounting units or credits

In NSW, the calculation is based on units, and in South Australia and the NT on credits. Students sometimes count subjects instead, and discover too late that they are short of the minimum or that a course counts for less than they thought. Check the unit or credit value of every course you take.

Overlooking the Category B limit

In NSW, no more than 2 units of Category B courses can be included in the ATAR. Students who take several Category B courses sometimes expect all of them to count. Only 2 units can.

Forgetting the optional exam

In NSW, some courses, such as English Studies and many VET courses, only count towards the ATAR if the optional HSC exam is sat. Students who skip the exam can find the course does not count.

Assuming VET counts everywhere

VET can contribute in some states but not in others, and always within limits. In Western Australia, the TEA is built from ATAR courses. In Queensland, only one VET qualification at Certificate III or higher can be among the best five.

Neglecting English

In NSW and Victoria, English must count, so neglecting it costs marks directly. In Queensland, failing to reach a C in English removes ATAR eligibility.

Ignoring combination rules

Students taking several related courses, such as multiple mathematics studies in Victoria or particular mathematics combinations in Western Australia, sometimes find that not all of them can count in full.

Dropping a subject without checking

Dropping a subject can leave a student below the minimum requirements or remove a useful buffer. Always check the rules and talk to your school first.

Comparing aggregates across states

Aggregates have different maximums and structures in each state. Only the ATAR is comparable between states.

Explaining the calculation to parents and carers

Parents and carers often want to understand how the ATAR is calculated, especially if they completed school under a different system. A short explanation usually covers what they need.

  • It is a rank. The ATAR shows your child’s position relative to their age group in your state, not a mark out of 100.
  • Results are scaled. Each subject’s results are adjusted onto a common scale, based on how the students in that subject performed across all their subjects.
  • The best results count. Each state counts a set of your child’s best scaled results, with rules about compulsory subjects such as English.
  • The total is ranked. The combined total is ranked against the age group to produce the ATAR.
  • Universities add adjustments. For each course, universities may add adjustment factors to create a selection rank.

Most parents find that once they see the ATAR as a rank built from scaled results, the rest makes sense. The most helpful thing they can do is encourage steady work, sensible subject choices and perspective.

Putting it all together

The ATAR calculation can seem complicated because every state has its own details. Underneath, though, the logic is consistent across Australia, and it can be summarised in a few principles.

Principle 1: Results are made comparable

School marks are moderated so they can be compared across schools, and subject results are scaled so they can be compared across subjects. Both processes aim to ensure that students are judged fairly, whatever school they attend and whatever subjects they take.

Principle 2: Your best results count

Each state counts a set of your best scaled results, with some rules about compulsory subjects such as English, partial contributions such as Victorian increments and the ACT’s 60 per cent rule, and bonuses such as those in Western Australia. Weaker results that fall outside the counted set have little or no effect.

Principle 3: The total becomes a rank

Your counted results form an aggregate, which is ranked against your age group in your state. Your position is reported as your ATAR, from 0.00 to 99.95, so that an ATAR means the same position wherever it was earned.

Principle 4: Universities add the final layer

When you apply, universities add any adjustment factors you are eligible for, creating a selection rank for each course. Offers are then made by selection rank, subject to prerequisites and any other criteria.

What this means in practice

The practical lessons follow directly. Choose subjects you can do well in and that meet your course requirements. Know which subjects will count in your state, and give them consistent effort, including English where it is compulsory. Value both school assessment and external assessment. Treat any estimate as a range. And remember that your selection rank, not your ATAR alone, decides your offers.

Glossary of calculation terms

Aggregate

The total of your counted scaled results, built under your state’s rules.

Category A and Category B

In NSW, the two classifications of HSC courses that can count towards the ATAR. At least 8 units of Category A courses are needed, and at most 2 units of Category B courses can be included.

Combined score

In Western Australia, the average of a moderated school mark and an exam mark for an ATAR course.

Increment

In Victoria, the 10 per cent contribution of your fifth and sixth scaled study scores, or an eligible VET increment.

Moderation

The process that adjusts school marks so that they can be compared across schools.

Primary four

In Victoria, your best English-group scaled score plus your next three best scaled scores.

Scaled mark or scaled score

A result after scaling has placed it on a common scale with other subjects.

Scaling

The process that adjusts results in different subjects based on how each subject’s students performed across all their subjects.

Selection rank

Your ATAR plus any adjustment factors for a particular course at a particular institution.

Study score

In Victoria, a score from 0 to 50 showing your position within a VCE study, with the mean set at 30.

TE Score

In Tasmania, the score built from your best eligible Level 3 and Level 4 course scores, which is converted to an ATAR.

TEA

The Tertiary Entrance Aggregate, used in Queensland and Western Australia.

University aggregate

In South Australia and the NT, the total of scaled scores from your best 90 credits, out of 90.

Common questions

How many subjects are used to calculate an ATAR?

Most states use your best four to six subjects. NSW counts ten units (usually five subjects); Victoria uses an English study plus your next best three, with a small contribution from a fifth and sixth; Queensland uses your best five.

Is English required for the ATAR?

In most states, yes. You generally need a result in an English subject for it to count towards your ATAR, though English does not have to be one of your best subjects.

What is scaling and why does it matter?

Scaling adjusts each subject so the same result means the same thing across subjects, based on how strong the group taking it was. It matters because it decides how much each of your results contributes to your ATAR.

Does the ATAR use my best subjects?

Yes. Your strongest scaled results are combined into your aggregate. Weaker subjects often count for less, or not at all, which is why taking an extra subject for safety helps.

How is the aggregate turned into an ATAR rank?

Every student’s aggregate is placed in order from highest to lowest. Your position in that order is your percentile rank, reported as your ATAR from 0.00 to 99.95.

Can I calculate my exact ATAR myself?

No. Your ATAR depends on how every other student performs and on the exact scaling for that year, which is only known once all results are in. Calculators give a close estimate using last year’s scaling.

What are the three steps of the ATAR calculation?

First, each subject result is scaled to account for the strength of the students taking it. Second, your best scaled results are combined into an aggregate under your state’s rules. Third, that aggregate is ranked and your position relative to your age group in your state is reported as your ATAR, from 0.00 to 99.95.

What happens before scaling?

Before scaling, each subject needs a result that is comparable across schools. Your curriculum authority combines school assessment and external assessment, and in most states school marks are moderated first, so that marks from different schools can be compared fairly.

Who calculates the ATAR?

Admissions centres or equivalent bodies calculate the ATAR: UAC for NSW, VTAC for Victoria, QTAC for Queensland, TISC for Western Australia and SATAC for South Australia and the NT. In Tasmania it is worked out through the University of Tasmania with TASC, and ACT results are scaled through the Board of Senior Secondary Studies’ process.

Does my school calculate my ATAR?

No. Your school provides assessment marks, but your ATAR is calculated at the state level using the same rules for every student. Your school’s marks are moderated, but no bonus or penalty is applied for the school you attend.

What is moderation?

Moderation adjusts school assessment marks so that they can be compared across schools. In NSW, Victoria and Western Australia, it uses each school group’s exam results, generally keeping students’ order within the group while adjusting the size of their marks.

Does moderation change my rank in my class?

Generally not. In the states that moderate school marks using exam results, the order of students within a school group is usually preserved. What changes is the size of the marks and the gaps between them, to match how the group performed in the exam.

How does Queensland check school marks?

Queensland uses endorsement and confirmation. The QCAA endorses assessment instruments before they are used and confirms schools’ marks by reviewing samples of student work. The QCAA has stated that external assessment results do not scale internal assessment results.

How much is the exam worth in NSW?

Each HSC mark is the average of your moderated school assessment mark and your exam mark, so the exam contributes half of each HSC mark.

How much is the exam worth in Queensland?

In Queensland General subjects, external assessment contributes 25 per cent of the result in most subjects and 50 per cent in mathematics and science subjects. Applied subjects have no external assessment.

How much is the exam worth in Western Australia?

For each ATAR course, the combined score is the average of the moderated school mark and the exam mark, so the exam contributes half.

How much is external assessment worth in South Australia?

In South Australia and the NT, Stage 2 subjects combine school assessment, worth 70 per cent, with external assessment, worth 30 per cent.

Does scaling change my order within a subject?

No. Scaling never changes the order of students within a subject. If you finish ahead of another student before scaling, you remain ahead after scaling. Scaling only changes where the subject’s results sit relative to other subjects.

Why does scaling look at my other subjects?

Because the best way to judge how strong a subject’s students are is to see how they perform in their other subjects. Students who take several subjects link those subjects together, allowing admissions centres to compare subjects fairly.

Is scaling the same every year?

No. Scaling is recalculated every year from the current students’ results. Subjects with large, stable cohorts tend to scale similarly from year to year, while small or changing subjects can move more.

Does scaling add a fixed amount to a subject?

No. Scaling transforms results along the whole range of a subject, and its effect can differ at the top, middle and bottom. VTAC notes, for example, that the adjustment for a study differs at each study score.

What is an iterative scaling method?

An iterative method repeats a calculation until the results settle. In inter-subject scaling, each subject’s scaled values are estimated, used to recalculate the strength of every subject’s students, and the process repeats until the values stop changing meaningfully. QTAC describes its scaling as iterative.

What is average marks scaling?

Average marks scaling is the term TISC uses for the scaling of Western Australian ATAR courses, applied after school marks have been moderated and combined with exam marks. Like other methods, it places each course’s results on a common scale.

Does Victoria give languages a scaling bonus?

VTAC applies a policy adjustment to the scaling of languages, which affects the initial scaled average of each language study. It is not a fixed number of points added to the ATAR, and its effect depends on each student’s result and combination.

What is an aggregate?

An aggregate is the total of your counted scaled results, built under your state’s rules. It goes by different names, such as the TEA in Queensland and Western Australia, the university aggregate in South Australia and the NT and the TE Score in Tasmania.

What is the maximum NSW aggregate?

The NSW aggregate is out of 500, because scaled marks are out of 50 per unit and 10 units are counted.

What is the maximum Victorian aggregate?

The Victorian aggregate can range from zero to over 210. It is built from the primary four plus 10 per cent of the fifth and sixth scaled study scores.

What is the maximum Queensland TEA?

The Queensland TEA is out of 500, as the sum of the best five scaled results.

What is the maximum WA TEA?

The maximum Western Australian TEA is 430: four scaled scores of up to 100 each, plus bonuses of up to 10 each for a language other than English, Mathematics Methods and Mathematics Specialist.

What is the maximum SA university aggregate?

The South Australian and NT university aggregate is out of 90, built from scaled scores out of 20 for 20-credit subjects and out of 10 for 10-credit subjects across the best 90 credits.

Can I compare aggregates between states?

No. Each state builds its aggregate differently, with different maximums. Only the ATAR is comparable between states, because each state converts its aggregates into ranks against its own age group.

How does the NSW calculation work?

UAC adds the scaled marks of your best 2 units of English and your best 8 units from your remaining ATAR courses, with no more than 2 units of Category B courses. The aggregate, out of 500, is ranked to produce your ATAR.

How does the Victorian calculation work?

VTAC adds your best English-group scaled study score and your next three best scaled study scores, plus 10 per cent of your fifth and sixth scaled study scores or an eligible VET increment. The aggregate is converted to an ATAR using that year’s table.

How does the Queensland calculation work?

QTAC adds your best five scaled results, from five General subjects or four General subjects plus one Applied subject or eligible VET qualification, to form your TEA. You need a C or better in an English subject to be eligible.

How does the WA calculation work?

TISC adds your best four scaled scores plus 10 per cent of your best scaled score in a language other than English, 10 per cent of your Mathematics Methods scaled score and 10 per cent of your Mathematics Specialist scaled score, then converts the TEA to an ATAR.

How does the SA and NT calculation work?

SATAC builds your university aggregate from the scaled scores of your best 90 credits of eligible Stage 2 study, with at least 60 credits from 20-credit Tertiary Admission Subjects or valid pairs and up to 20 credits of Recognised Studies, then converts it to an ATAR.

How does the Tasmanian calculation work?

From 2025, your TE Score is built from your best eligible Level 3 and Level 4 course scores over up to two years, each worth up to 15 points, totalling 60 to 75 points from four or five courses. It is then converted to an ATAR.

How does the ACT calculation work?

Your ATAR is based on your best three major scaled scores plus 60 per cent of your next major or minor, using scaled scores from the ACT’s own process for comparing results across colleges and courses.

Why is English treated differently?

Strong communication skills are relevant to almost every university course, so most states make sure every ATAR reflects some achievement in English, either by requiring English in the aggregate or as a condition of eligibility.

Does English always count in NSW?

Yes. Your best 2 units of English must be included in your NSW aggregate, even if they are among your lowest units.

Does English always count in Victoria?

Your best scaled score from the English group must be one of your primary four, so an English-group study always counts in full.

Does English count in Queensland?

You need a C or better in an English subject to be eligible for a Queensland ATAR, but English is only included in your TEA if it is among your best five scaled results.

Is English required in Western Australia?

English is not compulsory in the WA TEA, but universities in Western Australia generally require evidence of English competence for admission, which can be met in several ways, including through ATAR English or related courses.

What is in the Victorian English group?

The English group includes English, English as an Additional Language, English Language and Literature. Your best scaled score from the group counts in your primary four.

What is a Category B course?

In NSW, Category B courses are ATAR courses, often vocational, that can contribute only in a limited way. No more than 2 units of Category B courses can be included in the ATAR calculation, and eligibility requires at least 8 units of Category A courses.

Does English Studies count towards a NSW ATAR?

NESA notes that English Studies only counts towards the ATAR if the student sits the optional HSC exam. Students taking it who want an ATAR should check this and their other eligibility requirements.

Do NSW VET courses count?

Some do. Board Developed VET courses can count if the optional HSC exam is sat, and most are Category B, so no more than 2 units of Category B courses can be included in the ATAR.

What happens if I take all three VCE maths studies?

Under VTAC’s rules, if a student takes all three VCE mathematics studies, the lowest-scoring one can only contribute as an increment, not as one of the primary four.

What are unacceptable combinations in WA?

In Western Australia, some combinations of ATAR mathematics courses are subject to unacceptable combination rules, which limit how they can be counted in the TEA. Check TISC’s current rules if you take several mathematics courses.

Can a Certificate III count in Queensland?

Yes. One eligible VET qualification at Certificate III or higher can be one of the five results used to calculate the Queensland TEA, alongside four General subjects.

How is VET scaled in South Australia?

SATAC uses a scaled score for VET based on the average of the student’s first 70 credits of Tertiary Admission Subjects. VET can contribute as Recognised Studies, up to 20 credits in the flexible option.

Can VET count in Victoria?

Yes. Scored VCE VET programs can count as studies, and some VET programs without a study score can contribute an increment worth 10 per cent of the student’s fourth primary scaled score.

Does VET count in the WA TEA?

The WA TEA is calculated from scaled scores in ATAR courses. Vocational qualifications can provide other routes into some university courses but do not form part of the TEA itself.

How many attempts can count in South Australia?

SATAC’s rules require the results used for the university aggregate to come from a limited number of attempts. Check SATAC’s Tertiary Entrance booklet for the current rule.

What is a valid pair in South Australia?

A valid pair is two 10-credit subjects that SATAC allows to be counted in place of a 20-credit Tertiary Admission Subject towards the 60 credits that must come from 20-credit subjects.

What are Recognised Studies?

In South Australia and the NT, Recognised Studies are studies other than Tertiary Admission Subjects, such as some VET, that can contribute up to 20 credits towards the university aggregate.

How is my aggregate ranked?

Every ATAR-eligible student’s aggregate is placed in order. Your position is then expressed relative to your whole age group in your state, including people who do not receive an ATAR, and reported in bands of 0.05 from 0.00 to 99.95.

Why is the ATAR relative to the age group?

Anchoring the ATAR to the age group gives it a stable meaning from year to year. If it were based only on students who receive an ATAR, its meaning would change whenever more or fewer students chose an ATAR pathway.

Why is the typical ATAR around 70?

Because the ATAR compares you with your whole age group, and students who receive an ATAR tend to be stronger academically than the age group as a whole. UAC reported a median of 70.75 in NSW in 2025, and VTAC reported an average of 69.52 in Victoria in 2024.

How many ATAR values are there?

There are 2,000 possible values, from 0.00 to 99.95 in steps of 0.05. Queensland’s admissions centre describes these as 2,000 bands, each representing roughly the same share of the age group.

Can more than one student get 99.95?

Yes. 99.95 is the top band, and every student whose position falls in it receives that ATAR. In 2025, 53 students received 99.95 in NSW, 42 in Victoria and 37 in Queensland.

Are low ATARs reported?

Usually not as exact values. In Queensland, ATARs of 30 or below are reported as 30.00 or less, and in South Australia ATARs below 30.00 are not shown on the Tertiary Entrance Statement.

What is a conversion table?

A conversion table shows which aggregates correspond to which ATARs in a particular year. VTAC releases one on results day, and SATAC publishes a university aggregate to ATAR conversion table. Calculators use past tables to estimate your ATAR.

Why can the same aggregate give a different ATAR in different years?

Because the conversion from aggregate to ATAR depends on the current year’s results and age group. In large states, the differences are usually small, but they mean no one can know an exact ATAR before results are released.

Why does my ATAR depend on other students?

Because it is a rank. Your position depends on how your aggregate compares with everyone else’s in your age group. Scaling also depends on how all students performed. That is why the ATAR can only be finalised once every result is in.

Can I work out my ATAR from my subject results?

You can estimate it by applying recent scaling and conversion data to your results, ideally with a calculator built for your state. But your exact ATAR depends on the current year’s scaling and conversion, which are only known on results day.

How accurate are ATAR estimates?

A well-built calculator with realistic inputs usually gives a useful estimate, but the official ATAR often differs by a few points. Accuracy depends most on how realistic your inputs are. Treat any estimate as a range.

What data do ATAR calculators use?

Good calculators use the most recent official scaling reports and conversion data from your state’s admissions centre, and apply your state’s counting rules. They cannot use the current year’s data until it is published.

Why does my ATAR differ from my average mark?

Your ATAR is a rank built from scaled results under your state’s rules, while an average mark is unscaled and includes every subject. In NSW, UAC explains that marks around 70 typically lead to ATARs in the 50s, while marks around 80 typically lead to ATARs around 80.

Is a higher raw mark always better for my ATAR?

Within a subject, yes: a higher result means a higher scaled result. Across subjects, the same raw mark can produce different scaled results because each subject’s students differ. And only your counted results affect your aggregate.

Does a weak subject lower my ATAR?

Only if it is counted. In most states, only your best results count, with rules about compulsory subjects such as English. A weak subject that is not among your counted results has little or no effect.

Should I take more than the minimum number of subjects?

Often it helps, because an extra subject can replace a weaker result if one goes badly. But it only helps if you can do reasonably well in it without harming your other results. Check your state’s minimum requirements first.

Does dropping a subject improve my ATAR?

Rarely. Because only your best results count, a weak subject often contributes little anyway. Dropping it can remove a useful buffer or leave you below minimum requirements. Check your state’s rules and talk to your teachers first.

Do extension courses count as separate subjects in NSW?

Extension courses are usually 1 unit and are reported out of 50. They count as single units within the best 10 units used for the NSW ATAR.

Can a fifth or sixth study help in Victoria?

Yes. Each contributes 10 per cent of its scaled study score as an increment, and if one rises above a study in your primary four, it can move into the primary four and count in full.

Can a sixth subject help in Queensland?

Yes. Only your best five scaled results count, so a sixth subject can replace a weaker result if it scales higher.

Can a fifth course help in Western Australia?

Yes. Only your best four scaled scores count, so a fifth course can replace a weaker result. A language or advanced mathematics course also adds a bonus to your TEA even if it is not in your best four.

Do Year 11 results count?

In most states, the ATAR is based on final-year results. In Tasmania, eligible Level 3 and Level 4 courses completed over two years can count towards the TE Score. Accelerated subjects completed early can count in some states under their rules.

Can accelerated subjects count?

Often, yes, under each state’s rules. In NSW, UAC’s rules allow courses completed in different years to be included in certain circumstances, and in Victoria Units 3 and 4 of a study completed before the final year can contribute in the usual way.

How are repeated subjects treated?

Each state has rules about which results can count when a student repeats a subject or year. In South Australia and the NT, for example, the results used for the university aggregate must come from a limited number of attempts. Check your state’s rules.

How are IB results converted?

IB students receive an IB score rather than an ATAR. Admissions centres publish tables converting IB scores into a rank comparable with the ATAR, so IB students can compete on an equivalent basis.

Is my ATAR converted if I apply interstate?

No. ATARs are comparable between states and are used directly by universities in other states. Admissions centres pass them to each other.

When is the ATAR calculated?

After final exams are marked and subject results are finalised, admissions centres calculate scaling, build aggregates and rank them. ATARs are released in December; in 2026, between 10 December in Victoria and 20 December in Western Australia.

Why does the ATAR take time after exams?

Exams must be marked and checked, school marks moderated or confirmed and combined with exam results, scaling calculated from every student’s results, and aggregates built and ranked. Each step depends on all students’ final results.

When are scaling reports published?

Scaling reports and related statistics are published by admissions centres in the period after results are released. The timing differs between states.

What is a selection rank?

Your selection rank for a course is your ATAR plus any adjustment factors you are eligible for at that institution. Universities use selection ranks to make offers, and course thresholds are usually lowest selection ranks.

Are adjustment factors part of the ATAR calculation?

No. In most states, adjustment factors are added after the ATAR is calculated, to create selection ranks for particular courses. Western Australia’s language and mathematics bonuses are different, because they are built into the TEA used to calculate the ATAR.

What types of adjustment factors are there?

Common types include subject adjustments, regional and location adjustments, equity adjustments through schemes such as UAC’s Educational Access Schemes, VTAC’s Special Entry Access Scheme and QTAC’s Educational Access Scheme, elite athlete and performer adjustments, and school recommendation schemes.

Is there a cap on adjustments?

Most institutions cap the total adjustment a student can receive for a course, and some courses do not accept certain adjustments. The rules differ between institutions.

Do I have to apply for adjustments?

Some adjustments are applied automatically, while others, especially equity schemes, require an application with supporting documents and a deadline. Check each scheme you might be eligible for well before results day.

How are offers made?

For most courses, applicants are ranked by selection rank and offers are made from the top down until places are filled. You receive an offer for the highest preference you are eligible for. Some courses also use interviews, tests, portfolios or auditions.

What is a lowest selection rank?

The lowest selection rank is the lowest rank to which an offer was made for a course in a particular round. It is often called the cut-off and includes adjustment factors.

Where can I check the official rules?

Your state’s admissions centre publishes the rules: UAC for NSW and the ACT, VTAC for Victoria, QTAC for Queensland, TISC for Western Australia and SATAC for South Australia and the NT. In Tasmania, TASC and the University of Tasmania publish information on the TE Score.

Where can I find scaling data?

Admissions centres publish scaling data in their annual reports, such as UAC’s report on the scaling of the HSC, VTAC’s scaling report, QTAC’s reports and TISC’s tertiary entrance statistics. SATAC does not publish scaled scores for each subject.

Does SATAC publish an aggregate to ATAR table?

Yes. SATAC publishes a SACE and NTCET university aggregate to ATAR conversion table, which shows how aggregates related to ATARs in a particular year.

Does VTAC publish an aggregate to ATAR table?

Yes. VTAC releases a table converting aggregates to ATARs on results day each year.

What does UAC publish?

UAC publishes an annual report on the scaling of the NSW HSC, data on ATAR distributions and explanations of ATAR eligibility and calculation, including how HSC marks relate to ATARs.

How has the calculation changed over time?

The ATAR replaced the UAI, ENTER and TER from 2009 and 2010, and the Queensland OP for students completing Year 12 in 2020, alongside reforms including external assessment in General subjects. Tasmania changed its TE Score rules from 2025.

What was different about the Queensland OP?

The OP placed Queensland students in 25 bands, with 1 the highest, rather than giving a percentile-style rank. Queensland moved to the ATAR for students completing Year 12 in 2020.

What changed in Tasmania in 2025?

From 2025, the Tasmanian TE Score is built from a student’s best eligible Level 3 and Level 4 course scores over up to two years, with each course worth up to 15 points and a total of 60 to 75 points from four or five courses.

Why does a small mark change move my ATAR more in the middle?

Because many students have aggregates in the middle of the distribution. A small rise in your aggregate moves you past many of them, so your ATAR can change noticeably. Near the top, students are more spread out, so the same change usually moves your ATAR by less.

Do I need to understand the maths of scaling?

No. It helps to understand the idea: scaling places results from different subjects on a common scale based on how each subject’s students performed across all their subjects. Doing well within each subject is what matters most.

Can scaling lower my result?

Yes. Scaling can place a subject’s results higher or lower on the common scale, depending on the strength of its students. In some subjects, scaled results sit above raw results at a given point; in others, they sit below.

Is it better to be top of a low-scaling subject or middle of a high-scaling one?

It depends on the subjects and the year, but finishing near the top of a subject often produces a stronger scaled result than finishing in the middle or lower part of a subject that scales well. Your position within the subject matters a great deal.

Does the difficulty of the exam affect scaling?

Scaling depends on the strength of each subject’s students, not on how hard the exam was. A difficult exam may lower raw marks, but moderation and scaling are designed to place results on a comparable footing.

Why do some subjects scale up every year?

Subjects that consistently attract students who are strong across all their subjects tend to scale relatively high year after year. The exact figures still change each year, and the effect varies along the range.

Can a popular subject scale well?

Yes. Popularity alone does not determine scaling. A popular subject can scale well if its students are strong across their other subjects, or modestly if its cohort is broad.

Why are small subjects less predictable?

With few students, a handful of unusually strong or weak results can shift a subject’s overall pattern, so its scaling can move more from year to year than a large subject’s.

Do I get extra credit for taking more subjects?

Not directly. In Victoria, a fifth and sixth study each add 10 per cent of their scaled scores, and in Western Australia language and advanced mathematics courses add bonuses. Otherwise, extra subjects help only by replacing weaker results.

What does 60 per cent of the next major mean in the ACT?

In the ACT, the ATAR is based on the best three major scaled scores plus 60 per cent of the next best major or minor. That fourth result contributes, but at a reduced weight.

What does 10 per cent of the fifth and sixth mean in Victoria?

Your fifth and sixth scaled study scores each contribute one-tenth of their value to your aggregate. For example, a fifth study with a scaled score of 30 would add 3.0.

How do WA bonuses work?

TISC adds 10 per cent of your best scaled score in a language other than English, 10 per cent of your Mathematics Methods scaled score and 10 per cent of your Mathematics Specialist scaled score to your best four scaled scores, whether or not those courses are in your best four.

Why is the WA maximum 430 rather than 400?

Because the three bonuses for a language other than English, Mathematics Methods and Mathematics Specialist can each add up to 10 points to the best four scaled scores, which are out of 100 each.

How many points is a Tasmanian course worth?

From 2025, each full eligible Level 3 or Level 4 course is worth up to 15 points in the TE Score, which totals 60 to 75 points from four or five courses.

What does a TE Score mean?

The TE Score is Tasmania’s aggregate, built from your best eligible course scores over up to two years. The University of Tasmania explains that an ATAR of 80 means a TE Score at or above that of 80 per cent of Tasmanian Year 12 school leavers.

Can the Research Project count in South Australia?

The university aggregate is built from the best 90 credits under SATAC’s rules, which allow a flexible option of 30 credits alongside 60 credits from 20-credit subjects. Check SATAC’s current rules for exactly how the Research Project can be used.

Does SATAC publish subject scaling?

No. SATAC converts subject results into scaled scores but does not publish scaled scores for each subject, and it advises students not to choose subjects based on historical scaling.

Why are SA estimates less precise?

Because SATAC does not publish scaled scores for each subject, calculators have to approximate the scaling step. Treat SA and NT estimates as approximate ranges.

What is the NTCET?

The Northern Territory Certificate of Education and Training is the NT’s senior credential. It uses the SACE framework, and SATAC calculates NT students’ ATARs using the same rules as South Australia.

What is the Limited ATAR in NSW?

UAC explains that some institutions allow certain applicants who complete between 5 and 9 units of ATAR courses, including at least 1 unit of English, to use a Limited ATAR, calculated from their best 5 units.

What are the NSW eligibility requirements?

You must satisfactorily complete at least 10 units of ATAR courses, including at least 8 units of Category A courses, 2 units of English, three Board Developed courses of 2 units or more and courses from at least four subject areas.

What are the Queensland eligibility requirements?

You need five General subjects, or four General subjects plus one Applied subject or eligible VET qualification at Certificate III or higher, and a C or better in an English subject.

What are the Victorian requirements?

You need study scores in at least four eligible studies, including one from the English group, and you must meet VTAC’s other requirements. Your aggregate can include up to six results.

What are the WA requirements?

Your ATAR is calculated from scaled scores in ATAR courses, and a minimum of four ATAR courses is needed. Universities also generally require the WACE and evidence of English competence.

What are the Tasmanian requirements?

You need the TCE and a Satisfactory Achievement or better in at least four eligible Level 3 or Level 4 courses.

What are the SA requirements?

You must complete the SACE or NTCET and at least 90 credits of eligible Stage 2 study under SATAC’s rules, with at least 60 credits from 20-credit Tertiary Admission Subjects or valid pairs and up to 20 credits of Recognised Studies, within a limited number of attempts.

Is the calculation the same for every student in a state?

Yes. Within a state, every student’s results are moderated, scaled, combined and ranked using the same published rules. Differences between students’ ATARs come from their results and subject combinations.

Does it matter which school I attend?

No bonus or penalty is applied to your ATAR for your school. School marks are moderated so that they can be compared fairly, and moderation generally preserves your order within your school group.

Does private tutoring affect the calculation?

No. The calculation uses only your results. Tutoring may help some students improve their results, but it has no direct role in the calculation.

Is the calculation published?

Yes. Admissions centres publish their rules for eligibility, scaling and aggregates, along with reports and statistics. Curriculum authorities publish how school assessment and exams combine into subject results.

Can I appeal my ATAR?

Your ATAR is the output of a fixed calculation. If you believe an underlying subject result is wrong, each state has a process for checking or reviewing results, with deadlines. If a result changes, your ATAR is recalculated.

What if a subject is missing from my results?

Contact your school and curriculum authority promptly. A missing subject can affect your aggregate, and review processes have deadlines.

Does illness during exams change the calculation?

If an illness or misadventure application is successful, the affected result may be determined using other evidence, and that result flows into the calculation in the usual way. The ATAR itself is not adjusted separately.

Do disability provisions change the calculation?

No. Provisions such as extra time or rest breaks give students fair access to exams. They do not change how results are moderated, scaled or combined.

How do I estimate my ATAR from the calculation?

Estimate each subject result realistically, apply recent scaling at your level of result, combine your counted results under your state’s rules, and use a recent conversion table if one is published. A calculator built for your state does this for you.

Why do estimates use last year’s data?

Because the current year’s scaling and conversion are calculated from the current year’s results, which are only available on results day. Last year’s data is the best guide available beforehand.

Does a calculator know my state’s special rules?

A well-built calculator should apply your state’s rules, such as compulsory English, the NSW Category B limit, Victorian increments and WA bonuses. Check by changing your inputs and seeing whether the calculator responds as the rules say it should.

Is the ATAR calculated differently for boys and girls?

No. The same rules apply to every student. Published statistics sometimes report results by gender, but the calculation itself does not differ.

Is the ATAR calculated differently for regional students?

No. Regional students’ results are moderated, scaled, combined and ranked under the same rules. Some universities offer regional adjustment factors, which affect selection ranks rather than the ATAR.

Are distance education students calculated differently?

No. Students studying through distance education or a virtual school are ranked under the same rules as everyone else in their state.

Does the ATAR consider extracurricular activities?

No. The ATAR is calculated only from eligible academic results. Some universities consider activities, leadership or other criteria in particular schemes or courses, separately from the ATAR.

What happens to my results after the ATAR is calculated?

Your ATAR and relevant subject results are made available to the admissions system, where universities use your selection rank and subject results to make offers. Your results and ATAR remain on record.

Is the calculation fair?

It is designed to be fair by placing results from different subjects on a common scale and applying the same published rules to everyone in a state. Like any single measure, it has limits, which is why universities also use adjustments and other pathways.

What is the most important thing to know about the calculation?

That the ATAR is a rank built from scaled results, and only your counted results matter. Doing well within the subjects that count, including English where it is compulsory, is what lifts your ATAR.

How is an HSC mark calculated?

Each HSC mark is the average of your moderated school assessment mark and your exam mark. For example, a moderated assessment mark of 82 and an exam mark of 78 give an HSC mark of 80.

How is a Queensland subject result calculated?

In General subjects, confirmed internal assessment results are added to the external assessment result to give a result out of 100. Internal assessment contributes 75 marks in most subjects and 50 in mathematics and science subjects, with external assessment making up the rest.

How is a WA combined score calculated?

Your school mark is statistically moderated using the course’s exam marks, then averaged with your exam mark. For example, a moderated school mark of 70 and an exam mark of 66 give a combined score of 68.

How is a SACE subject result formed?

Stage 2 subjects combine school assessment, worth 70 per cent and moderated, with external assessment, worth 30 per cent. Results are reported as grades from A+ to E-, and SATAC converts them to scaled scores.

How is a VCE study score formed?

A study score combines school-based assessment, which is statistically moderated using exam performance, with examination results. It is reported from 0 to 50 and shows your position within the study, with the mean set at 30.

Are internal marks scaled by external results in Queensland?

No. The QCAA has stated that external assessment results do not scale internal assessment results. Internal results are quality assured through endorsement and confirmation instead.

Is the school mark or the exam more important?

It depends on the state and subject. In NSW and Western Australia they are weighted equally. In Queensland, external assessment is 25 per cent of most General subjects and 50 per cent of mathematics and science subjects. In South Australia, it is 30 per cent.

What should Year 10 students know about the calculation?

Check your state’s eligibility rules before choosing subjects, choose subjects you can do well in and enjoy, meet the prerequisites for courses you might want, consider a buffer subject and use scaling only as a tie-breaker.

What should Year 12 students focus on?

Focus on the subjects likely to count, including compulsory English where it applies, value every assessment, prepare seriously for external assessment and use a state-specific calculator to estimate a realistic range.

What should parents know about the calculation?

The ATAR is a rank built from scaled results, only counted results matter, and adjustment factors affect selection ranks rather than the ATAR. Encouraging steady work, sensible subject choices and perspective helps most.

Does my old ATAR still count if I return to study?

Yes. Your ATAR does not expire. Universities may also consider more recent study or experience, and may use whichever basis gives you the strongest rank.

Can the order of my subjects change after scaling?

Your order within each subject does not change, but the order of your own subjects by scaled result can differ from their order by raw mark. A subject with a lower raw mark may produce a higher scaled result than one with a higher raw mark.

Which subject counts if two are tied?

If two results are equal after scaling, either can count without changing the aggregate. The rules simply select the combination of counted results that gives the highest permitted aggregate.

Does the calculation pick the best combination automatically?

Yes. Admissions centres apply the rules to find the combination of your results that gives the highest aggregate permitted by your state’s rules, including compulsory subjects and limits.

Can a subject count partly?

In some states, yes. In Victoria, the fifth and sixth studies count at 10 per cent. In the ACT, the fourth major or minor counts at 60 per cent. In Western Australia, bonuses add 10 per cent of certain scaled scores.

Can I see how my aggregate was built?

Some states report scaled scores or related information with the ATAR, which lets you see which subjects contributed. Others report only the ATAR and subject results. Check your admissions centre’s results service.

Does the same ATAR mean the same thing in every year?

Broadly, yes. Because the ATAR is anchored to the age group, an ATAR of 80 represents roughly the same position in the age group each year, even though the aggregate needed to reach it can change slightly.

Why might my aggregate be high but my ATAR lower than expected?

Aggregates and ATARs are on different scales. If your aggregate sits in a part of the distribution where many students cluster, or if conversion data differ from your expectations, your ATAR may not match what you assumed from the aggregate alone.

Is the ATAR rounded?

The ATAR is reported in bands of 0.05. Your position is placed in the appropriate band, which is why ATARs end in .00, .05, .10 and so on.

How is 99.95 decided?

99.95 is the top band. Every student whose position falls in the top 0.05 per cent of the age group receives it.

Do universities see my aggregate?

Universities mainly use your ATAR, selection rank and subject results. The aggregate is an intermediate step in the calculation and is generally not what universities use to select students.

Can my ATAR be higher than my selection rank?

No. Your selection rank is your ATAR plus any adjustment factors, so it is equal to or higher than your ATAR for each course.

Why do some courses not accept certain adjustments?

Each institution decides which adjustments apply to each course and how they are capped. Some courses, especially those with additional criteria, may limit or exclude certain adjustments.

What is an equity adjustment?

An equity adjustment is an adjustment factor for students whose education has been affected by circumstances such as hardship, illness, disability or disrupted schooling, usually through an application to a scheme run by an admissions centre.

What is a regional adjustment?

A regional adjustment is an adjustment factor some universities offer to students who live or study in regional or remote areas. It raises the selection rank for particular courses, not the ATAR.

What is a subject adjustment?

A subject adjustment is an adjustment factor some institutions add for strong results in particular subjects, often relevant to the course. It raises the selection rank, not the ATAR.

What are school recommendation schemes?

These are schemes in which universities consider school recommendations and other criteria, such as Year 11 results, and may make offers before ATARs are released.

How do early offers relate to the calculation?

Early offers usually use criteria other than the ATAR, such as Year 11 results or school recommendations. Some are conditional on achieving a minimum ATAR or meeting a prerequisite once the ATAR is calculated.

Do universities use subject results as well as the ATAR?

Yes. Universities check subject results for prerequisites and assumed knowledge, and some apply subject-based adjustments. Some courses also consider particular subject results in selection.

Can I improve my ATAR after results?

Your ATAR is fixed unless an underlying result is corrected. You can improve your basis for admission through enabling programs, diplomas, university study or, in some states, repeating subjects.

How do I know which of my subjects count?

Apply your state’s rules: for example, your 2 units of English and best 8 other units in NSW, your English-group study and next three best in Victoria, or your best five in Queensland. A good calculator shows which results are counted.

Does taking a subject at a higher level help?

Only if you perform well at that level. Higher-level courses often scale well because of their strong cohorts, but your scaled result depends on where you finish among those students.

Should I sit an optional HSC exam?

If you want a course such as English Studies or a Board Developed VET course to count towards your NSW ATAR, you generally need to sit the optional HSC exam. Check the rules for your course.

Can I count two English courses?

In NSW, your best 2 units of English count, and further English units can count among your best 8 other units under UAC’s rules. In Victoria, your best English-group study is in the primary four, and a second English-group study can count among your other studies, subject to VTAC’s rules.

Do I need maths to get an ATAR?

Mathematics is not compulsory for the ATAR in most states, although some certificates have numeracy requirements. Many university courses require or assume mathematics, so check course requirements.

Is the calculation the same in the NT as in South Australia?

Yes. NT students complete the NTCET, which uses the SACE framework, and SATAC calculates their ATAR using the same rules as South Australia.

Who handles ACT applications?

UAC handles university applications for ACT students. ACT results are scaled through the Board of Senior Secondary Studies’ process, and the ATAR is based on the best three major scaled scores plus 60 per cent of the next major or minor.

Why does Tasmania count two years?

Tasmania’s TE Score is designed to include eligible Level 3 and Level 4 courses completed over up to two years, reflecting how senior study is structured in the state. From 2025, the best eligible course scores over that period form the TE Score.

A December 2025 Mitchell Institute report estimated that around 64 per cent of Year 12 students nationally receive an ATAR. The share differs between states.

Does moderation lower good students’ marks?

Not as a rule. Moderation adjusts a school group’s marks to match how the group performed in the exam, which can move marks up or down. It generally preserves each student’s order within the group.

Is the exam all that counts?

No. School assessment contributes to subject results in every state. In South Australia it is 70 per cent of each Stage 2 subject, and in Queensland internal assessment is 75 per cent of most General subjects.

Is the relationship between aggregate and ATAR a straight line?

No. Because results bunch in the middle of the distribution, small aggregate changes move the ATAR more in the middle and less near the top.

Does the calculation favour certain subjects?

The same rules apply to every subject. Scaling reflects each subject’s students. Published policies, such as Victoria’s language adjustment and Western Australia’s bonuses, apply equally to all students taking the relevant courses.

What should I do before results day?

Check your eligibility, identify which subjects are likely to count, estimate a realistic range, estimate your selection rank for each course and build a preference list across your range with your genuine first choice first.

What should I do after results day?

Check your subject results, note your ATAR, calculate your selection rank for each course, compare with thresholds and adjust your preferences before the next deadline. Contact your school or curriculum authority promptly if a result seems wrong.

Can teachers help me understand my likely ATAR?

Yes. Teachers know how you perform and how students with similar work have done in past years, which helps you make realistic estimates and improve the results that feed into the calculation.

Why did my friend get a higher ATAR with similar marks?

Their subjects may have scaled differently at their level, their results may have combined more favourably under the counting rules, or their weakest results may not have counted. Similar raw marks can produce different aggregates.

Do all states use exams?

Most states combine school assessment with external exams or assessments. The ACT does not use statewide external exams in the same way as other states and has its own process for comparing results across colleges.

How does the ACT compare results across colleges?

The ACT’s Board of Senior Secondary Studies uses its own scaling process to compare results across colleges and courses, producing scaled scores that are used to calculate the ATAR.

What is the Board of Senior Secondary Studies?

The BSSS is the ACT authority responsible for senior secondary certification. It oversees the ACT Senior Secondary Certificate and the processes that produce the scaled scores used for ACT students’ ATARs.

What is SCSA?

SCSA, the School Curriculum and Standards Authority, sets the WACE courses and runs the exams in Western Australia. TISC then calculates scaled scores and ATARs.

What is NESA?

NESA, the NSW Education Standards Authority, sets the HSC courses, runs the HSC exams and issues HSC results. UAC calculates the ATAR from scaled HSC marks.

What is the VCAA?

The VCAA, the Victorian Curriculum and Assessment Authority, administers the VCE, runs the exams and issues study scores. VTAC calculates the ATAR.

What is the QCAA?

The QCAA, the Queensland Curriculum and Assessment Authority, administers the QCE, sets external assessment and issues subject results. QTAC calculates the ATAR.

What is the SACE Board?

The SACE Board of South Australia administers the SACE, including external assessment and the moderation of school assessment. SATAC calculates the university aggregate and ATAR.

What is TASC?

TASC, the Office of Tasmanian Assessment, Standards and Certification, administers the TCE in Tasmania. The ATAR is worked out through the University of Tasmania with TASC.

What is UAC?

UAC, the Universities Admissions Centre, calculates the ATAR for NSW students and processes university applications for many institutions in NSW and the ACT.

What is VTAC?

VTAC, the Victorian Tertiary Admissions Centre, calculates the ATAR for Victorian students and processes applications for many Victorian institutions.

What is QTAC?

QTAC, the Queensland Tertiary Admissions Centre, calculates the ATAR for Queensland students and processes applications for many Queensland institutions.

What is TISC?

TISC, the Tertiary Institutions Service Centre, calculates scaled scores and ATARs for Western Australian students and manages much of the undergraduate admission process for WA Year 12s.

What is SATAC?

SATAC, the South Australian Tertiary Admissions Centre, calculates the university aggregate and ATAR for students completing the SACE or NTCET and processes applications for many institutions in South Australia and the NT.

Do all admissions centres calculate the ATAR the same way?

They all produce an ATAR on the same scale, relative to the age group, but each builds the aggregate using its own state’s rules for which results count and how.

Admissions centres and universities coordinate so that the ATAR means the same thing nationally, but each state’s admissions centre or equivalent body calculates the ATAR for its own students.

Some vocational or other studies completed through TAFE can contribute in some states, within limits, such as VET in Queensland or Recognised Studies in South Australia. Check your state’s rules.

Not necessarily. Scaling can change the order of your own studies, because each study is scaled according to its own cohort. A study with a slightly lower study score may produce a higher scaled score.

What do the 2,000 bands mean?

Why are ATAR steps 0.05?

Reporting in steps of 0.05 allows fine distinctions near course thresholds while keeping the scale manageable. Every student whose position falls within the same step receives the same ATAR.

Why is the ATAR released after subject results in some states?

Not directly. The ATAR is calculated from results. Effort matters because it shapes those results, but the calculation itself uses only marks and scores.

Should I worry about scaling if I enjoy a subject?

Usually not. If you enjoy a subject and are likely to do well in it, it is often a good choice regardless of its scaling, provided it meets your course requirements.

Not usually. Your ATAR and selection ranks are what matter for applications. Understanding the aggregate helps you see how your results combined.

An aggregate is the total of your counted scaled results, used to calculate your ATAR. A selection rank is your ATAR plus any adjustment factors for a particular course.

Within the same state and year, students with the same aggregate receive the same ATAR. Across different years or states, the same aggregate number can correspond to different ATARs.

Why is it called an aggregate?

Because it aggregates, or adds together, your counted scaled results into a single total that can be ranked.
