ATAR vs OP vs ENTER

If your parents talk about their “OP” or “ENTER score”, they are describing the old versions of the ATAR. This guide explains each one, why they existed, and how they compare.

The OP, ENTER, UAI and TER were all older Year 12 ranking systems, one per state. They did the same job as today’s ATAR: rank students for university entry. Most states replaced them with the national ATAR around 2009 and 2010, and Queensland moved from the OP to the ATAR in 2020. They are all ranks, not marks, so the core idea has never changed.

Key takeaways

  • The OP, ENTER, UAI and TER were all older, state-based Year 12 ranks.
  • They did the same job as the ATAR — ranking students for university.
  • The ATAR replaced them nationally around 2009–2010.
  • Queensland kept the OP until 2020, the last state to switch.
  • The OP ran 1 to 25 (1 was best); the ATAR runs 0.00 to 99.95 (higher is better).
  • They are all ranks, not marks, so the underlying idea never changed.
  • You cannot exactly convert an old score to an ATAR, only estimate.
  • The OP was introduced in 1992 and the last OPs went to the class of 2019; the first Queensland ATARs were released on 19 December 2020.
  • NSW and the ACT received the first ATARs in December 2009; Victoria, SA, NT, WA and Tasmania followed in December 2010.
  • The ENTER, UAI and TER were already treated as equivalent ranks across borders before the ATAR name existed.
  • An OP never expires, and the QCAA can still issue an ATAR statement for anyone awarded an OP from 1997 to 2019.

The one idea every system shared: a rank, not a mark

Before we walk through each old system one by one, it is worth slowing down on the single idea that ties them all together. The OP, the ENTER, the UAI, the TER and today’s ATAR look different on paper. One ran from 1 to 25, others ran up to 99.95 or 100, and each had its own rules about which subjects counted. But every single one of them was a rank, not a mark. If you hold on to that one idea, the rest of this guide becomes much easier to follow.

What a mark tells you

A mark tells you how much of a test you got right. If you score 78 out of 100 on a chemistry exam, that number describes your performance on that paper. It says nothing directly about how anyone else went. Every student in the room could have scored 78, or you could have been the only one above 50. A mark is an absolute measure of one piece of work.

Marks are useful for teachers and students, because they show what you know and what you still need to learn. But marks are not very useful for choosing between thousands of applicants for a limited number of university places. Different subjects are marked on different scales, different exams vary in difficulty from year to year, and different schools assess in different ways. A 78 in one subject is not automatically worth the same as a 78 in another.

What a rank tells you

A rank tells you where you stand compared with other people. If you are ranked in the top 10 per cent, that means 90 per cent of the comparison group finished behind you. A rank does not care whether the test was easy or hard, or whether the subject was marked generously or harshly. It only cares about order: who finished ahead of whom.

That is exactly the information universities need. When a course has 300 places and 1,200 applicants, the university does not need to know what percentage of a chemistry paper each applicant got right. It needs a fair way to put the applicants in order, so it can offer places from the top of the list downwards until the places are filled. Every one of the old systems, and the ATAR today, exists to produce that order.

Why every system needed scaling

Because students take different subjects, a rank cannot simply be built by adding up raw marks. If one student takes subjects that are marked harshly and another takes subjects that are marked generously, their raw totals would not be comparable. So every system on this page used some form of scaling: a statistical adjustment that puts results from different subjects onto a common scale before they are combined.

The methods differed. Queensland’s OP used a common statewide test, the Queensland Core Skills (QCS) Test, to scale results within and between schools. Victoria, NSW, South Australia and Western Australia scaled each subject based on how the students in it performed across their other subjects. Today’s Queensland ATAR uses inter-subject scaling too. But the purpose was always the same: make results comparable, then rank. You can read more about how this works today in our guide to ATAR scaling explained.

Why this matters when comparing old and new scores

Once you see every system as a rank, comparing them becomes a question of position rather than points. An OP 1 and an ATAR of 99 are not the same kind of number, but both say roughly the same thing: this student finished in the top few per cent of their group. A UAI of 90 and an ATAR of 90 are very close in meaning, because both describe a position near the top tenth of the age group.

The details still matter, and later sections explain where the old ranks differ from the ATAR. The OP ranked students against other OP-eligible students in Queensland, while the ATAR ranks students against their whole age group. The UAI’s top value was 100, while the ATAR’s is 99.95. Those differences shift the numbers slightly. But the underlying logic, putting students in order after scaling their results, never changed.

The simple test
Whenever you meet an unfamiliar Year 12 score, ask one question: does a bigger number mean better, or does a smaller number mean better? For the ENTER, UAI, TER and ATAR, bigger is better. For the OP, smaller was better, because it described which of 25 bands you landed in, counting down from the top.

A rank describes the group as much as the student

One more consequence of ranking is easy to miss. A rank only means something in relation to the group it is calculated against. If you change the group, the same performance can produce a different rank. This is why the old systems are not perfectly interchangeable. The OP compared each student with OP-eligible Queensland students. The NSW TER of the 1990s compared students with the HSC cohort, and the UAI then compared them with the wider Year 10 group from two years earlier. The ATAR in NSW now refers to the whole age cohort that started high school together.

So when someone says “I got a 90 in my day”, the honest answer is: a 90 against which group? That question explains almost every difference you will see in the conversion tables later on this page. Keep it in mind as we look at each system in turn, and also when you read our guide to what an ATAR is.

Why states used to have different systems

For decades, education in Australia was run state by state. Each state built its own Year 12 certificate, its own exams, and its own way of ranking students. So each state ended up with its own rank, under its own name.

This worked within a state, but it caused problems across borders. A university in one state could not easily compare a student from another, because the ranks were on different scales. A national rank was the obvious fix, and eventually every state adopted one.

Education has always been run by the states

To understand why Australia ended up with so many different Year 12 ranks, you have to start with who runs schools. Under Australia’s federal system, school education has traditionally been the responsibility of the states and territories rather than the Commonwealth. Each state built its own education department, its own curriculum, its own senior certificate and its own exam board. The Commonwealth has influence through funding and national agreements, but the day-to-day design of Year 11 and 12 has always sat with the states.

That arrangement produced a different senior certificate in almost every jurisdiction. New South Wales has the Higher School Certificate (HSC). Victoria has the Victorian Certificate of Education (VCE). Queensland had the Senior Certificate and now has the Queensland Certificate of Education (QCE). South Australia has the South Australian Certificate of Education (SACE), which the Northern Territory also uses as the NTCET. Western Australia has the Western Australian Certificate of Education (WACE). Tasmania has the Tasmanian Certificate of Education (TCE), and the ACT has its own Year 12 certificate run by the Board of Senior Secondary Studies (BSSS).

When the certificates are different, the results are different too. Some states relied heavily on external exams, while Queensland relied on school-based assessment for decades. Some reported marks out of 100, others reported study scores out of 50 or grades from A to E. With that much variety in the raw ingredients, it was natural for each state to build its own way of turning results into a single number for university entry.

Each state also built its own admissions centre

Alongside the exam boards, each state developed a central body to process university applications. These tertiary admissions centres still exist today. The Universities Admissions Centre (UAC) handles NSW and the ACT. The Victorian Tertiary Admissions Centre (VTAC) handles Victoria. The Queensland Tertiary Admissions Centre (QTAC) handles Queensland. The South Australian Tertiary Admissions Centre (SATAC) handles South Australia and the Northern Territory. The Tertiary Institutions Service Centre (TISC) handles Western Australia, and in Tasmania the University of Tasmania manages admissions itself.

VTAC is a good example of how old these arrangements are. In 2026 it marks 60 years since representatives of Victoria’s universities first came together in 1966 to coordinate admissions. Before centralised admissions, students in many states had to apply to each university separately. Each centre then developed or adopted its own rank, so the names multiplied. If you want to understand the modern role of each centre, see our guides to UAC, VTAC, QTAC, SATAC, TISC and UTAS admissions.

Why universities needed a single number at all

It was not always necessary to rank school leavers. In the early era of Australian universities, students who performed well enough in the final school examination were said to have “matriculated”, and matriculation was usually enough to enrol in a course. There was no need to add marks together and rank everyone, except perhaps to award scholarships.

That changed when demand outgrew supply. As more students finished school and wanted to go to university, popular courses could not take everyone who was qualified. Universities introduced quotas, starting with courses such as medicine, and once a course has more qualified applicants than places, it needs a fair way to decide who gets in. Ranking students by an aggregate of their scaled results became the standard answer. UAC’s technical report on the ATAR describes this shift from a pre-quota era to a post-quota era as the origin of the modern NSW rank.

The border problem

Separate state systems worked well enough inside each state. A university in Brisbane could compare Queensland applicants with each other using the OP, and a university in Melbourne could compare Victorian applicants using the ENTER. The trouble started when students crossed borders.

Imagine a Queensland student with an OP of 4 applying for a law course in Sydney in 2005. The Sydney course had a UAI cut-off of, say, 98. Is an OP 4 above or below a UAI of 98? Neither number is on the other’s scale. Or imagine a Victorian student with an ENTER of 92 applying to a Brisbane university that listed its cut-offs as OPs. Universities and admissions centres needed a translation layer for every combination of states.

The problem became more pressing as more students applied interstate, as families moved for work, and as universities began to recruit nationally. A national rank was the obvious fix. As the next sections show, the states first solved this quietly by making their ranks equivalent behind the scenes, and later made it official by giving the shared rank one name: the ATAR.

Why the states did not simply merge their systems

If a single rank was so obviously useful, why did it take until 2009 to get a single name, and until 2020 for every state to use it? The short answer is that the rank sits at the end of a long chain. To change the rank properly, a state had to think about its senior certificate, its assessment model, its scaling methods and its admissions processes. Changing the label is easy; changing the machinery behind it is not.

Queensland is the clearest example. Its OP was tied to a whole model of school-based assessment and to the QCS Test. Moving to the ATAR meant redesigning senior assessment, introducing external exams in every General subject, building a new scaling method and moving the calculation from the curriculum authority to QTAC. That is why Queensland’s switch took years of review and planning, which we cover in detail further down.

The early era: scores, then ranks

The early part of the timeline shows a gradual move from scores to ranks. NSW’s first post-quota aggregate was a Tertiary Entrance Score, which was later expressed as a Tertiary Entrance Rank. Queensland’s TE Score, introduced in 1974, was also a fine-grained number, and it was used for nearly two decades. These early systems already did the core job of the ATAR: combine results, adjust them so subjects were comparable, and produce an order of merit.

The 1990s: Queensland goes its own way, the rest converge

The 1990s were the decade in which Queensland’s path split from the rest of the country. In 1992 Queensland abolished the TE Score and introduced the OP, a deliberately coarser system of 25 bands, backed by the QCS Test and Field Positions. At almost the same time, education ministers across Australia were working on ways to make the other states’ ranks equivalent, so a student’s rank would mean roughly the same thing wherever they applied.

By the end of the decade, NSW had introduced the UAI and Victoria the ENTER. Despite the different names, these ranks, and the TERs used elsewhere, were designed to be equivalent. Queensland’s OP sat outside that common scale, but conversion tables were published so it could be compared.

2009 and 2010: one name for the shared rank

The ATAR did not invent the idea of an equivalent national rank. What it did was give that rank one name and, in some states, tidy up the details. The decision followed agreement among the tertiary admissions centres, and the federal Education Minister announced the change in June 2009. NSW and the ACT issued ATARs to their Year 12 class of 2009. Victoria, South Australia, the Northern Territory, Western Australia and Tasmania followed with the class of 2010.

2020: the last piece falls into place

Queensland was the final state to adopt the ATAR. After a major independent review by the Australian Council for Educational Research (ACER) and several years of preparation, students who started Year 11 in 2019 became the first to study under the new QCE system. They received Queensland’s first ATARs, calculated by QTAC, on 19 December 2020. Since then, every Year 12 student in Australia who is eligible for a rank receives an ATAR.

Three quick examples

A student who finished in Sydney in 2008 received a UAI; a student who finished in Sydney in 2009 received an ATAR. A student who finished in Melbourne in 2009 received an ENTER, even though NSW had already moved to the ATAR that year. And a student who finished in Brisbane in 2019 received an OP, while their younger sibling finishing in 2020 received an ATAR. In each pair, only one year separates two different names.

Why the year boundaries matter

The boundaries matter most for Queensland. Anyone who finished Year 12 in Queensland between 1992 and 2019 has an OP (if they were OP-eligible) and needs a conversion to compare it with an ATAR. Anyone who finished in 2020 or later has an ATAR. For every other state, the boundary is mostly about the label: an ENTER, UAI or TER from the late 1990s or 2000s already sits on, or very close to, today’s scale.

The OP: Queensland’s old system

Queensland used the Overall Position, or OP. It was a band from 1 to 25, where 1 was the best and 25 the lowest. This is the reverse of the ATAR, which trips people up: a low OP was a strong result.

The OP grouped students into 25 broad bands rather than a fine rank. An OP of 1 put you in the top few per cent of the state. Queensland ran the OP for many years before switching to the ATAR in 2020, the last state to make the change. See how ATAR is calculated in QLD today.

Before the OP: Queensland’s TE Score

The OP did not appear from nowhere. From 1974 to 1991, Queensland ranked school leavers with a Tertiary Entrance (TE) Score. The TE Score was a fine-grained number, and many Queenslanders who finished school in the 1970s and 1980s still remember theirs as a three-digit figure. Today, QUT explains that a TE Score awarded between 1974 and 1991 can be turned into a selection rank by dividing it by 10, so a TE Score of 885 corresponds to a selection rank of 88.50.

The TE Score had a long life, but by the late 1980s it had lost public confidence. A 1987 review, known as the Pitman Report, proposed changes that were not acted on at the time. In 1990 the Review of Tertiary Entrance in Queensland, led by Professor Nancy Viviani and known as the Viviani Report, was commissioned after the decision had been made to abolish the TE Score from 1992. One of the concerns was that universities treated the TE Score as the main selection tool even though it had been intended as one criterion among several, and that employers had started using it in ways it was never designed for.

Another concern was precision. The TE Score separated students by very small amounts, but not every small difference reflected a real difference in achievement. Later QCAA material describes the thinking this way: the TE Score had many points of discrimination, but not all of them represented meaningful differences between students. The OP was designed to be more honest about that uncertainty by grouping students into bands rather than splitting them by tiny margins.

What “Overall Position” actually meant

The name describes exactly what the number was: your overall position in a statewide order of merit. QCAA’s description of the OP says it showed a student’s position in a statewide rank compared with all other OP-eligible students in Queensland. Note the last part of that sentence. The OP did not compare you with every 17- or 18-year-old in the state. It compared you with the students who had chosen, and qualified, to be OP-eligible.

The 25 bands ran from OP 1 at the top to OP 25 at the bottom. That is why a low OP was good. You can think of the OP as answering the question “which of 25 groups did you land in, counting from the top?” rather than “what percentage of people did you beat?”

Who could get an OP

Not every Queensland Year 12 student received an OP. To be eligible, a student generally had to:

  • complete at least 20 semester units of Authority subjects across Years 11 and 12,
  • study at least three of those subjects for all four semesters, and
  • sit the QCS Test in Year 12.

Authority subjects were the academic subjects with syllabuses approved by the state curriculum authority, such as English, Mathematics B, Chemistry and Modern History. Authority-registered subjects and many vocational courses did not count towards the OP. A typical OP-eligible student studied five or six Authority subjects over two years, which comfortably met the 20-semester requirement.

Over time, OP eligibility became less universal. By 2018, around half of Queensland’s Year 12 students were OP-eligible, according to figures summarised from QCAA data. The rest took pathways built around vocational qualifications, Authority-registered subjects or a mix of both. This is one of the reasons Queensland’s later reforms aimed for a rank that could include a broader range of students, for example through the “four General subjects plus one Applied subject or VET qualification” rule used for the ATAR today.

Step one: Levels of Achievement and SAIs

Queensland’s senior system was built on school-based assessment. For each Authority subject, schools assessed students against statewide standards and awarded one of five Levels of Achievement: Very High Achievement (VHA), High Achievement (HA), Sound Achievement (SA), Limited Achievement (LA) and Very Low Achievement (VLA). Within each level, students could be placed on finer “rungs” to show where they sat inside the level. A strong statewide moderation process checked that schools were applying the standards consistently.

For the OP, teachers then did something more fine-grained. In each subject group, they assigned every OP-eligible student a Subject Achievement Indicator (SAI) on a scale from 200 to 400. The top student in the school’s subject group received 400 and the lowest received 200, with everyone else placed in between according to the size of the gaps between them. If the top two students were almost identical, their SAIs would be close; if the top student was far ahead, there would be a big gap. SAIs were used in this standard way for subject groups with 14 or more OP-eligible students.

Step two: the QCS Test

SAIs told the QCAA how students compared within a single subject at a single school. But a 350 in one school’s Physics class was not automatically worth the same as a 350 in another school’s Physics class, or in the same school’s Music class. To make SAIs comparable, the system needed a common reference point that every OP-eligible student took. That reference point was the Queensland Core Skills (QCS) Test.

The QCS Test was a statewide test sat by all OP-eligible students under the same conditions. QCAA described it as an achievement test rather than an intelligence or aptitude test, built around common curriculum elements of the Queensland senior curriculum. Each student received an individual grade from A to E on their Senior Statement, but the main purpose of the test was to provide group information for scaling. For the OP calculations, raw QCS scores were transformed onto a consistent scale with a mean of 175 and a mean difference of 25.

The key word is group. Your own QCS result did not directly set your OP. Instead, the QCS results of the students in your subject group and your school were used to scale everyone’s SAIs. A strong individual QCS performance helped your classmates as much as it helped you, and a weak one pulled the group parameters down slightly for everyone. That is why schools put considerable effort into QCS preparation.

The final QCS Test was held on 3 and 4 September 2019, for the last OP cohort. It no longer exists.

Step three: two stages of scaling

The OP calculation used two stages of scaling, each based on QCS results.

  1. Within-school scaling. For each subject group in a school, the SAIs were scaled so that their mean and spread matched the QCS results of the students in that subject group. If a school’s Chemistry class had strong QCS results, its SAIs were scaled up relative to a class with weaker QCS results. The order of students inside the subject did not change; only the positioning of the whole group did.
  2. Between-school scaling. Each student’s best results were combined into an Overall Achievement Indicator (OAI). Then the OAIs for each school were scaled to match the mean and spread of that school’s QCS results. This put students from different schools onto one statewide scale.

The OAI itself was based on a student’s best 20 semester units of Authority subjects, averaged, with subjects weighted equally. After both stages, every OP-eligible student had a scaled OAI that could be compared with every other OP-eligible student in Queensland.

Step four: from scaled OAIs to 25 OP bands

Finally, the scaled OAIs were arranged in a statewide order and cut into 25 bands, from OP 1 to OP 25. QCAA’s 2019 data summary makes an important point about these bands: there was no fixed quota of students in each band. The proportion of students in OP 1 could change from year to year because the bands were set to keep standards comparable across years, not to hand out a fixed percentage of each OP.

That is a real difference from the ATAR. An ATAR is, by definition, tied to percentages of an age cohort. The OP’s bands were designed with reference to standards over time, so the share of OP 1s could drift. QCAA figures show the share of OP-eligible students receiving an OP 1 starting at 2.0 per cent in 1992, rising to about 2.5 per cent in later reports, and sitting at around 2.8 per cent in the final years of the system.

Special rules for small schools and small classes

A statistical system built on group QCS results has an obvious weakness: small groups. If a school only had four students in a subject, their QCS results could not reliably represent the group. The QCAA had specific procedures for these cases:

  • Small subject groups with fewer than 10 OP-eligible students had their scaled SAIs estimated from Levels of Achievement and rung placements, using data from larger groups elsewhere.
  • Intermediate subject groups of 10 to 13 students used a weighted mix of the small-group method and the standard method.
  • Small schools with fewer than 16 OP-eligible students had no second-stage scaling.
  • Intermediate schools with 16 to 19 OP-eligible students used a weighted average of raw and scaled OAIs.

These rules show how much engineering sat behind a single number from 1 to 25. They also explain why the OP was hard to explain at a dinner table: the answer to “how is my OP worked out?” depended on the size of your school and your classes.

Quality checks and anomaly detection

Because so much depended on group data, the QCAA ran extensive checks. A tool called BonSAI compared each school’s SAI distributions against moderation data, and the QCAA negotiated with schools when patterns looked inconsistent. Students whose QCS performance was strikingly out of line with their school results could be down-weighted in the group calculations, so that one unusual result did not distort everyone else’s scaling. A Scaling Anomalies Committee, including external members, reviewed group-level anomalies.

At the individual level, each student’s OP was compared before release with the OPs of students who took similar subject combinations and achieved similar results. Where an OP looked anomalously low, it was reviewed by a committee and typically adjusted by one position. Students could also apply for a review of their OP after release.

Field Positions: the OP’s lesser-known companion

Alongside the OP, OP-eligible students could receive up to five Field Positions (FPs). Each FP ranked a student in one area of skill, on a scale from FP 1 (highest) to FP 10 (lowest). The five fields were:

  • Field A: extended written expression involving complex analysis and synthesis of ideas.
  • Field B: short written communication involving reading, comprehension and expression in English or a foreign language.
  • Field C: basic numeracy involving simple calculations and graphical and tabular interpretation.
  • Field D: solving complex problems involving mathematical symbols and abstractions.
  • Field E: substantial practical performance involving physical or creative arts or expressive skills.

Each subject contributed a different weighting to each field. English contributed heavily to Field A, for example, while Mathematics C contributed heavily to Field D. A student only received an FP in a field if their subject combination carried enough weight in it. FPs were mainly used as tie-breakers when many applicants for a course shared the same OP. They disappeared along with the OP and have no equivalent in today’s ATAR.

Students who were not OP-eligible

Students who did not qualify for an OP were not shut out of tertiary study. QTAC calculated a separate selection rank for OP-ineligible students based on their Senior Statement results, and vocational qualifications could also be converted to ranks. These ranks were used for admission within Queensland. Students on student visas who would otherwise have met the requirements could receive an equivalent OP.

The split between “OP” and “OP-ineligible rank” is one reason the system felt complicated. Two Queensland students could leave the same school with two completely different kinds of tertiary entrance number. The ATAR’s design for Queensland, with one rank for General subjects and set rules for including an Applied subject or a VET qualification, was meant to bring more students under a single measure.

How OPs were reported to students

OPs were not released as a standalone number. Each Queensland Year 12 student received a Senior Education Profile from the state’s studies authority. Depending on the student, it could contain a Senior Certificate (and later a QCE) listing their subject results, and, for OP-eligible students, a Tertiary Entrance Statement showing their OP and any Field Positions. OP-eligible students received this statement in mid-December, while QTAC released ranks for OP-ineligible students later in the month.

Because the OP and FPs were printed on an official statement, many Queenslanders still have the document today. If you are trying to confirm an old OP, that Tertiary Entrance Statement is the best place to look before contacting the QCAA.

An illustration of how an OP came together

The following example is simplified and uses invented figures. It is designed to show the logic of the OP rather than to reproduce the exact QCAA mathematics.

Imagine a student called Mia at a large Brisbane school in 2012. She studies English, Mathematics B, Chemistry, Biology and Modern History for all four semesters, which gives her 20 semester units, and she sits the QCS Test.

  1. Her teachers award Levels of Achievement: VHA in Chemistry and Biology, HA in English, Mathematics B and Modern History.
  2. Within each subject group, her teachers place her on the 200–400 SAI scale. She is near the top in Chemistry with an SAI of 380, mid-upper in English with 320, and so on.
  3. The QCAA scales each of her subject groups using that group’s QCS results. Her school’s Chemistry students had strong QCS results, so Chemistry SAIs are scaled to a high position. Her Modern History group had more modest QCS results, so that group is scaled to a lower position.
  4. Her scaled SAIs are averaged across her best 20 semesters to form her OAI.
  5. Her school’s OAIs are then scaled to match the school’s overall QCS results, putting Mia on the statewide scale.
  6. Her scaled OAI lands in the band labelled OP 3. She also receives FPs, for example FP 2 in Field D thanks to her mathematics and science, and FP 3 in Field A.

Notice what Mia could and could not control. She controlled her own work, which drove her SAIs and Levels of Achievement. She contributed to, but did not control, her groups’ QCS results. And she had no control over how many other students landed near her. That mix of personal and group factors was central to how the OP worked.

Strengths of the OP system

It is easy to criticise a system after it has been replaced, so it is worth being fair. The OP had genuine strengths:

  • It respected school-based assessment. Queensland’s teachers designed and marked the work that counted, under a moderation system that many educators admired.
  • It avoided false precision. Twenty-five bands did not pretend that tiny differences between students were meaningful.
  • It used every subject equally. Subjects were weighted equally in the OAI, and scaling was based on the actual students in each group.
  • It had layered quality checks. Anomaly detection, review committees and individual review processes were built in.
  • Field Positions gave extra information for distinguishing between applicants with the same OP.

Criticisms that led to its replacement

By the 2010s, though, the OP had attracted sustained criticism, and the 2014 ACER review concluded that Queensland’s senior assessment and tertiary entrance arrangements needed attention. Common concerns included:

  • Coarse bands. With only 25 bands, thousands of students shared each OP. Universities with competitive courses often needed tie-breakers, and the difference between the top and bottom of a single band could be significant.
  • Dependence on the QCS Test. Because group QCS results drove scaling, some students felt their OP depended partly on how classmates performed on a single test.
  • Complexity. Few students or parents could explain how an OP was calculated, which made it hard to trust.
  • Falling eligibility. With only about half of Year 12 students OP-eligible, the OP no longer described the whole cohort.
  • Interstate translation. Queensland students needed their OP converted to an ATAR for interstate applications, and interstate students needed their ATARs converted for Queensland applications.

Those concerns, together with the review’s recommendations, led to the decision to replace the OP with the ATAR and to redesign senior assessment around a mix of school-based and external assessment. We look at that change in its own section below, and you can see how the modern system works in our QCE scaling guide and our QCE to ATAR conversion guide.

The ENTER: Victoria’s old system

Victoria used the Equivalent National Tertiary Entrance Rank, or ENTER. Despite the name, it worked much like today’s ATAR: a rank from 0 to 99.95, where higher was better.

The ENTER was calculated from VCE study scores, in almost the same way the ATAR is now. When the national ATAR arrived, Victoria’s change was mostly a change of name and scale details. A student who understood the ENTER already understood the ATAR. See how ATAR is calculated in VIC.

What the name “ENTER” really meant

The full name, Equivalent National Tertiary Entrance Rank, is a clue to its purpose. By the late 1990s, education ministers and admissions centres had agreed that Year 12 ranks across most of Australia should be equivalent, so that a rank of 90 in one state meant much the same as a rank of 90 in another. Victoria chose a name that said so out loud: its rank was the equivalent national rank, calculated in Victoria.

The term ENTER was only ever used in Victoria. The same kind of rank was called the UAI in NSW and the ACT and the TER in South Australia, the Northern Territory, Western Australia and Tasmania. The calculations behind them differed because the certificates differed, but the ranks were coordinated so they could be treated as equivalent for admissions. Victoria used the ENTER from 1998 to 2009, and before that it used its own TER.

Victoria before the ENTER

Victoria’s admissions history goes back a long way. VTAC traces its origins to 1966, when Victoria’s universities began to coordinate their admissions, and the organisation grew from there. In the late twentieth century, Victoria moved from the old Higher School Certificate to the Victorian Certificate of Education, and a statewide Tertiary Entrance Rank was used to rank students for university.

When the ENTER arrived in 1998, it coincided with other modernising steps at VTAC, including the launch of its website and online applications. For students, the change was mostly in the name and the national framing. The idea of combining scaled VCE results into an aggregate and turning that aggregate into a rank was already established.

Step one: the VCE study score

The ENTER started with the study score, which is still the building block of the Victorian ATAR. The Victorian Curriculum and Assessment Authority (VCAA) calculates a study score for each VCE study a student completes at Units 3 and 4, combining school-assessed coursework or tasks with external examinations. Study scores run from 0 to 50 and are designed so that, in a typical study, the average is around 30.

A study score is already a ranking inside one study. A score of 40 places a student well above the middle of that study’s cohort, and scores of 45 and above are achieved by only a small fraction of students. What a study score cannot tell you is how competitive a study’s cohort was compared with other studies. That is where VTAC’s scaling comes in.

Step two: VTAC scaling and “ENTER subject scores”

VTAC scaled each study score to adjust for the strength of the cohort taking that study. The question scaling asks is roughly this: how well did the students in this study perform across all their other studies? If the students in a study tended to perform strongly everywhere, the study’s scores were scaled up. If they tended to perform less strongly elsewhere, the scores were scaled down.

In the ENTER era, the scaled scores were often called ENTER subject scores. Today they are simply called scaled study scores. The principle has not changed, and you can read the modern version in our VCE scaling guide. One point is worth repeating because it is so often misunderstood: scaling reflects the strength of a study’s cohort, not how “hard” the study feels.

Step three: the aggregate

Once each study had a scaled score, VTAC built an aggregate from a fixed pattern:

  • the scaled score in one English study (English, English Language, Literature or English as a Second Language, as it was then called),
  • the student’s next three best scaled scores, which together with English formed the “primary four”, and
  • 10 per cent of the fifth and sixth best scaled scores, known as increments.

This is exactly the same pattern used for the Victorian ATAR today. If you have ever heard a teacher talk about the “primary four plus 10 per cent of the fifth and sixth”, they are describing a formula that has been used in Victoria since well before the ATAR existed.

Step four: from aggregate to ENTER

VTAC then ranked the aggregates and expressed each student’s position as a percentile rank from 0.00 to 99.95, in steps of 0.05. An ENTER of 80.00 meant the student’s position was higher than 80 per cent of the comparison group. The highest possible ENTER was 99.95, the same top value used by the ATAR today.

Because the ENTER was designed to be equivalent to ranks in other states, the conversion from aggregate to rank was coordinated with those states rather than worked out in isolation. The ATAR now makes the reference group explicit everywhere: it describes a student’s position in their age group, not just among the students who finished Year 12. That is why an ATAR of 80 does not mean you beat 80 per cent of your Year 12 classmates; it means you finished in the top 20 per cent of the wider age group.

Small rules that changed over time

Like any system that ran for more than a decade, the ENTER evolved. One example from VTAC’s own records is the treatment of repeated studies. For a time, the ENTER calculation included a penalty for repeating a study. VTAC’s research found that the penalty was no longer serving a useful purpose, since students mostly repeated studies to strengthen folio work for art and design courses rather than to boost their ENTER. VTAC removed the penalty with effect from the 2003/04 selection round.

The way results reached students changed too. VTAC’s history notes that ENTER and VCE results were sent by SMS for the first time during the ENTER era, and in 2004 VTAC phased out paper applications and launched SEAS, the Special Entry Access Scheme that still supports students facing disadvantage today. If that scheme is relevant to you, our guide to educational access schemes explains how they work now.

Notional ENTERs for other qualifications

Not every applicant to a Victorian university had completed the VCE. Students with the International Baccalaureate Diploma, for example, needed a rank on the same scale so they could compete for places. VTAC calculated notional ENTERs for these applicants, and its records show it reviewed the IB methodology in the early 2000s after a South Australian study found that states were converting IB scores in different ways. From 2004, admissions centres moved towards a combined national approach to IB conversions, which is still used in most states today.

How the ENTER became the ATAR

When the national ATAR was announced in 2009, Victoria had very little to change. The ENTER already ran from 0.00 to 99.95 in steps of 0.05, it was already treated as equivalent to other states’ ranks, and the aggregate formula did not need to be touched. VTAC’s history records 2010 as the year the ATAR replaced the ENTER in Victoria, with the Year 12 class of 2010 receiving the first Victorian ATARs.

That is why the original version of this page said a student who understood the ENTER already understood the ATAR. In Victoria, the switch was mainly a renaming exercise that aligned Victoria with the national label. Study scores, scaling, the primary four, the increments and the 99.95 top rank all carried straight across. For today’s version, see our VCE to ATAR conversion guide or try the VCE ATAR calculator.

The UAI: NSW and the ACT

New South Wales and the ACT used the Universities Admission Index, or UAI. Like the ENTER, it was a rank where higher was better, calculated from scaled HSC marks.

The UAI ran up to 100, in fine steps, and served exactly the role the ATAR does now. NSW and the ACT moved to the ATAR around 2009. See how ATAR is calculated in NSW today.

Before the UAI: the NSW TES and TER

NSW has been ranking school leavers for university since the late 1960s. UAC’s technical report on the ATAR describes the period from 1967 to 1998 as the era of the Tertiary Entrance Score (TES) and then the Tertiary Entrance Rank (TER). These early measures were built from HSC results, scaled so that different courses could be compared, and combined into an aggregate that could be used to put applicants in order.

The NSW TER expressed a student’s position within the HSC cohort, that is, among the students who actually sat the HSC. That sounds natural, but it created a problem once the states began trying to make their ranks equivalent. Different states had very different Year 12 completion rates, so being in the top 10 per cent of one state’s Year 12 group did not mean the same thing as being in the top 10 per cent of another’s. A national rank needed a common reference group.

1998: the UAI arrives

The NSW Board of Studies’ guide to the 1998 HSC announced the introduction of the Universities Admission Index (UAI), replacing the TER, as a rank on a scale from 0 to 100. A report by the Technical Committee on Scaling later explained the main change: the rank was now referenced to the Year 10 cohort from two years earlier, rather than to the HSC cohort alone, and the name changed to the UAI. This was a response to the national push to make ranks equivalent across states.

Referencing the Year 10 cohort meant the UAI described where a student would have sat had everyone in their Year 10 group gone on to complete Year 12 and receive a rank. Because some students leave school after Year 10, and those students tend on average to have lower results, the same HSC performance produced a higher number on the UAI than it would have on the old HSC-only TER. As an illustration, a student at the 90th percentile of the HSC group might find themselves at around the mid-90s on a scale that includes the whole Year 10 group.

The UAI was used in NSW for the HSC classes of 1998 to 2008. Its calculation was the responsibility of the Technical Committee on Scaling, acting on behalf of the NSW Vice-Chancellors’ Committee, with UAC publishing an annual report on the scaling of the HSC.

How the UAI was calculated

The UAI followed a structure that will be familiar to any NSW student today:

  1. HSC marks. The NSW Board of Studies (now NESA) reported an HSC mark for each course, combining school assessment and the HSC exam.
  2. Scaling. Each course’s marks were scaled to adjust for the strength of the students taking it, based on how those students performed across their other courses. Scaled marks were expressed so that each unit contributed up to 50 marks.
  3. Aggregate. The student’s best two units of English were added to their best eight other units, for a total of ten units and an aggregate out of 500.
  4. Rank. The aggregates were ranked and converted to a UAI against the reference group.

The ATAR in NSW uses the same structure: the best 2 units of English plus the best 8 units from the remaining courses, scaled, aggregated, and converted to a rank. You can see the current method in our guide to how the ATAR is calculated in NSW, our HSC scaling explainer and our HSC to ATAR conversion guide.

Accumulating the HSC over time

The UAI era also saw more flexibility in how students could complete the HSC. Under the HSC “Pathways” arrangements of the late 1990s, students could accumulate courses over more than one year, and the NSW Board of Studies’ statistics for 1998 note that students who added courses could have their UAI recalculated, with the new results available for inclusion in their best ten units. The same principle continues today: UAC calculates the NSW ATAR from the best ten units of scaled marks regardless of the year in which each course was completed, and students can repeat courses to try to improve their result.

Why nobody got 500

One of the curious facts about the NSW system is that the aggregate is out of 500 but no one gets 500. UAC’s material on the change to the ATAR noted that, over 30 years, no NSW student had ever received an aggregate of 500. The students who received the top rank were those with the highest aggregates in their year, not those with a perfect score. This is a good reminder that the rank depends on position: the top rank goes to the best performers in a cohort, whatever their exact total.

The 2001 HSC reforms

The UAI era also spanned a major change to the HSC itself. From 2001, the “new HSC” introduced standards-referenced reporting, with performance bands describing what students at each level typically know and can do. Band 6 became the familiar label for the top band in 2-unit courses. UAC’s technical report includes a section on the consequences of those reforms for the calculation of the rank.

For students, the main effect was that HSC marks became tied to published standards rather than simply to a student’s position in a course. The UAI still depended on scaled marks, so scaling continued to do the work of making courses comparable. The reforms show that the rank can stay stable even when the certificate underneath it changes. If you want to know how bands and scaled marks interact today, see our guides to a good HSC score and HSC subject scaling calculators.

Why the top UAI was 100 and the top ATAR is 99.95

The UAI ran up to 100.00, while the ATAR tops out at 99.95. This small difference has a simple explanation. A percentile rank of 100 would literally mean finishing ahead of 100 per cent of the group, including yourself, which is impossible. The 99.95 cap represents the top band of 0.05 per cent. Other states’ ranks, including Victoria’s ENTER, already used 99.95 as their top value.

The NSW Technical Committee on Scaling recommended in its 2006 report that the highest UAI should become 99.95 rather than 100.00. When the ATAR was introduced in NSW in 2009, the 99.95 ceiling came with it, bringing NSW into line with the rest of the country. UAC’s own summary of the change lists this as one of the visible differences between the two measures.

What changed when the UAI became the ATAR

UAC’s explanation of the move from the UAI to the ATAR in 2009 highlighted a few points:

  • The top value became 99.95 instead of 100.
  • The reference group changed. The ATAR in NSW indicates a student’s position relative to the age cohort they began high school with in Year 7, rather than the Year 10 group used for the UAI. NSW was also raising its minimum school leaving age around this time, which made Year 10 a less useful reference point.
  • The numbers shifted slightly. Apart from the top score, the same position produced an ATAR a little higher than the old UAI, and the gap widened lower down the scale.
  • Nothing else changed. UAC stressed that the scaling process, the order of students and the applicants selected for each course stayed the same. Because course cut-offs moved by the same amount as the ranks, no one was advantaged or disadvantaged.

That last point is the one to remember. A shift in the numbers is not a change in who gets in. If every applicant’s rank moves up by about the same amount, and every course’s cut-off moves up by the same amount, the result of the selection process does not change.

The UAI in the ACT

The ACT has its own senior system run by the Board of Senior Secondary Studies (BSSS), with school-based assessment and a common scaling test, the ACT Scaling Test (AST). Before it adopted the UAI, the ACT used its own Tertiary Entrance Rank, documented in BSSS booklets from the early 1990s. The ACT later aligned with NSW by adopting the UAI, since ACT applicants and NSW applicants were processed together through UAC.

When the ATAR was introduced, the ACT moved with NSW, issuing ATARs to its Year 12 class of 2009. ACT students today receive an ATAR calculated from their best three major courses plus 60 per cent of the next best major or minor, using AST-based scaling. Because UAC handles both jurisdictions, ACT and NSW ATARs are designed to be directly comparable. See our guides to how the ATAR is calculated in the ACT and UAC in the ACT.

What the UAI means for families today

If a parent or relative finished the HSC between 1998 and 2008, they received a UAI. Their number is very close to an ATAR, but not identical. UAC’s published conversion table, covered in detail further down this page, shows how to translate. For most purposes, you can treat a UAI as being within one or two points of the equivalent ATAR, with the ATAR slightly higher except at the very top.

If they finished before 1998, they received a TER or a TES, which described their position in a different reference group. Those numbers are harder to compare directly. In that case, the most useful comparison is often in words rather than numbers: “you were in roughly the top tenth of your year” translates much more reliably across systems than any raw number does.

The TER: South Australia, WA, NT and Tasmania

Several states used the Tertiary Entrance Rank, or TER, including South Australia, Western Australia, the Northern Territory and Tasmania. As the name suggests, it was another rank for tertiary entry.

The TER worked on similar principles to the ENTER and UAI: scale each subject, combine the best results, and rank students against their age group. These states also moved to the ATAR around 2009 and 2010.

One name, several different calculations

The Tertiary Entrance Rank was the most widely used name of all the old systems. At various times it was used in South Australia, the Northern Territory, Western Australia, Tasmania, the ACT, Victoria and NSW. That breadth is exactly why “TER” can be confusing: a TER from Perth in 2003 and a TER from Adelaide in 2003 shared a name and a scale, but they were built from different certificates through different calculations.

What the TERs had in common was their output. By the 2000s, the TERs in South Australia, the Northern Territory, Western Australia and Tasmania all reported a percentile rank with a top value of 99.95, and they were treated as equivalent to the ENTER and UAI. So, for a university in another state, a TER of 85 from any of these places was read as roughly the same as an ENTER of 85 or a UAI of 85.

The TER in South Australia and the Northern Territory

South Australia and the Northern Territory have shared a senior certificate and an admissions centre for many years. Students complete the South Australian Certificate of Education (SACE), known in the Northern Territory as the NTCET, and SATAC handles admissions for both. In the TER era, the curriculum and assessment body was the Senior Secondary Assessment Board of South Australia (SSABSA), which later became the SACE Board.

The TER calculation in the 2000s worked in four broad steps:

  1. Subject scores. SSABSA reported each student’s achievement in each tertiary entrance subject as a score out of 20.
  2. Scaling. SATAC scaled each subject’s scores according to the performance of the students taking it, so that each subject could contribute fairly to the rank.
  3. University aggregate. SATAC added together the scaled scores for the student’s best four subjects and half of the scaled score for the fifth best subject, giving a university aggregate out of 90.
  4. Rank. The aggregates were placed in order and the TER was assigned as a percentile rank from 0.00 to 99.95, in steps of 0.05.

The university aggregate out of 90 has survived into the ATAR era. Today, SACE students’ university aggregates are calculated from 90 credits of Stage 2 study, which may include up to 20 credits of Recognised Studies, and SATAC converts the aggregate to an ATAR. The aggregate appears on the student’s Tertiary Entrance Statement alongside their ATAR. For the modern version, see our guides to how the ATAR is calculated in SA, SACE to ATAR conversion and SATAC in the Northern Territory.

The TER in Western Australia: the TEE and the TES

Western Australia’s TER grew out of the Tertiary Entrance Examination (TEE), the main final-year exam system for university-bound students from 1986 to 2009. Western Australia had two numbers in this era that people often mix up:

  • the Tertiary Entrance Score (TES), a score calculated from a student’s scaled TEE results, and
  • the Tertiary Entrance Rank (TER), the percentile rank derived from the TES.

For many years, the TES was based on a student’s best average scaled score across four or five TEE subjects, with at least one subject from each of two lists, multiplied by 5.1. That produced a score out of 510. In 2008 the list rule was removed and the TES was calculated from a student’s best four TEE subjects, with candidates still required to complete English or English Literature. In 2009, the last year of the TEE, the TES was reported out of 400.

TISC then converted the TES into a TER between 0 and 99.95, showing each student’s rank position. Applicants from other states, New Zealand or the International Baccalaureate who applied to WA universities could also be given a TER that was treated as equivalent to the WA TER.

From 2010, Western Australia introduced the new WACE examinations, replacing the TEE, and the TER was replaced by the ATAR. Today TISC scales each ATAR course using a method called average marks scaling, adds a student’s best four scaled scores into a Tertiary Entrance Aggregate (TEA), applies any bonuses for languages and higher-level mathematics, and converts the TEA to an ATAR. You can explore this in our guides to how the ATAR is calculated in WA, WACE to ATAR conversion and TISC explained.

The TER in Tasmania

In Tasmania, the TER was calculated for students who completed the Tasmanian Certificate of Education (TCE) and met the tertiary entrance requirements. The calculation was based on an aggregate of the student’s best pre-tertiary subject results, which could include a limited number of results from Year 11, and the aggregates were then ranked and converted to a TER on the same principles as elsewhere.

Tasmania is a special case in one other respect. Because the University of Tasmania is the state’s only university, it effectively acts as the state’s admissions centre. Tasmania moved from the TER to the ATAR with the class of 2010. For the modern system, see our guides to how the ATAR is calculated in Tasmania, TCE to ATAR conversion and TASC and UTAS explained.

The TER in the ACT, Victoria and NSW

Three other jurisdictions also used a TER at some stage before switching to their later names:

  • The ACT used its own TER, calculated by the BSSS, before adopting the UAI alongside NSW.
  • Victoria used a statewide TER in the 1990s before introducing the ENTER in 1998.
  • NSW used a TER, building on the earlier Tertiary Entrance Score, until the UAI replaced it in 1998.

So if a relative from any of these places says they received a “TER”, the year matters a great deal. An NSW TER from 1995 described a position among HSC students, while a South Australian TER from 2005 was part of the national equivalent scale. The same three letters can describe quite different reference groups.

Other acronyms that look like the TER

Tertiary entrance has a crowded alphabet, and several acronyms are easy to confuse with the TER:

  • TES can mean Tertiary Entrance Score (as in WA and early NSW) or Tertiary Entrance Statement (the document SATAC issues today, and the name of the statement Queensland’s OP-eligible students received).
  • TE Score was Queensland’s Tertiary Entrance Score, used from 1974 to 1991.
  • TEE was Western Australia’s Tertiary Entrance Examination.
  • TEA is the Tertiary Entrance Aggregate, used in WA and Queensland today as the total from which the ATAR is calculated.

When reading old documents, check which of these is meant before comparing numbers. A TES of 300 in WA and a TER of 30 are not related in the way the digits might suggest.

Why the TER states changed so easily

For South Australia, the Northern Territory, Western Australia and Tasmania, the move from the TER to the ATAR in 2010 was mostly a change of label. Their TERs already ran to 99.95 in steps of 0.05, and they were already part of the national equivalence arrangement. Western Australia’s change coincided with a bigger shift in its senior curriculum, from the TEE to the new WACE courses and exams, but the rank itself kept the same meaning.

That smooth handover contrasts sharply with Queensland’s experience a decade later. The TER states were already on the common scale; Queensland had to build a new pathway to reach it. How to read this table

Three columns do most of the work when you are trying to make sense of an old score.

“Where and when” tells you which system someone actually received. This matters more than the name. A “TER” from NSW in the 1990s and a “TER” from South Australia in the 2000s are different measures with different reference groups.

“Scale” tells you which direction is good. The OP and FPs are the only measures where a lower number is better. Everything else puts the high numbers at the top.

“Built from” tells you what the rank was measuring. The OP depended partly on group results from the QCS Test; the other systems depended on scaling each subject against the students taking it. Those design differences are why direct one-to-one conversions do not exist, even when the scales look similar.

How the old ranks were made equivalent across borders

One of the least-known parts of this story is that most of the old ranks were already equivalent before the ATAR existed. The ATAR is often described as the moment Australia got a national rank. It is more accurate to say it was the moment the existing national rank got a single name.

The ministers’ push in the 1990s

In the mid-1990s, Australia’s state and federal education ministers met through a body called the Ministerial Council on Education, Employment, Training and Youth Affairs (MCEETYA). Records of their meetings from 1994 and 1995 show work on several tertiary entrance issues:

  • making rank equivalences across states available for tertiary intakes in 1997,
  • agreeing to a “home state rule”, under which interstate applicants would be assessed using the university entrance rank from their home state,
  • endorsing a Common University Entrance (CUE) Index approach for admissions centres to use when determining interstate equivalences, and
  • designing research into a methodology for calculating interstate equivalences.

The practical outcome was that, from the late 1990s, the ranks used outside Queensland were designed to line up with each other. UAC’s admissions information reflects this, noting that since the late 1990s all states and territories except Queensland used a common measure to rank Year 12 students, even though it went by different names.

The common index and its many names

Government data standards from the 2000s show how this worked in practice. When universities reported a student’s entrance score to the federal government, they were asked to report the value of the common index agreed by the ministers. The standards listed the different local names for that one index: the UAI in NSW, the ENTER in Victoria, the TER in South Australia, the Northern Territory, Western Australia and Tasmania, and, once the change happened, the ATAR in NSW and the ACT.

For Queensland, the common index went by another name: the Interstate Transfer Index (ITI). Because the OP was not on the common scale, Queensland students needed a separate number to compete interstate. The QCAA calculated a rank on the common scale for OP-eligible students so they could be considered for courses in other states. Later, that rank was simply reported as an ATAR. It also helps explain why the QCAA’s ATAR statement service covers students who received an OP from 1997 onwards, the period after the national equivalence arrangements came into effect.

Equivalence beyond Australia

The same thinking extends to qualifications from outside the Australian state systems. Admissions centres convert International Baccalaureate (IB) Diploma scores to an ATAR-equivalent “combined rank”. According to ACTAC material, separate methods were used before 2004, after which a combined table was introduced for most jurisdictions. Today the combined rank table is used in NSW, the ACT, Queensland, Victoria and Western Australia, while South Australia, the Northern Territory and Tasmania use a South Australian table.

New Zealand’s NCEA results are also converted to an ATAR-equivalent rank for New Zealand students applying to Australian universities, using a methodology overseen by the Australasian Conference of Tertiary Admission Centres. New Zealand’s qualifications authority still describes this rank as the ATAR, “also known as the Interstate Transfer Index”. The old name lives on in the places where translation between systems is still needed.

Why equivalence is not the same as identical calculation

Equivalence means the ranks are designed to mean the same thing, not that they are calculated the same way. CourseSeeker, the national course search site, puts it plainly: ATARs are calculated in each state to reflect a student’s rank against other students in that state, all ATARs are treated as equivalent between the states, and there are differences between the states in how the ATAR is calculated.

That was true of the ENTER, UAI and TER, and it remains true of the ATAR. A Victorian ATAR is built from a primary four and increments, an NSW ATAR from ten units, a South Australian ATAR from a 90-credit aggregate, and so on. Each state then aligns its ranks to the shared scale. If you want to see how the states differ today, our guide to the HSC vs other states and our guide to the VCE vs other states lay out the main differences.

How scaling differed between the old systems

Every system on this page used scaling, but not in the same way. Understanding the differences explains a lot about why the systems produced the results they did, and why the OP in particular felt so different to students.

Approach 1: a common test as the yardstick (Queensland’s OP)

Queensland’s OP relied on a common test, the QCS Test, to compare groups. The logic was that if every OP-eligible student sat the same test, the test results of a subject group or school could show how strong that group was compared with others. Subject groups and schools with stronger QCS results had their school-assigned results scaled to higher positions.

The strength of this approach is that it gave Queensland a single, independent reference point in a system that otherwise relied on school-based assessment. Its weakness, in the eyes of critics, was that a general test could only partly reflect achievement in specific subjects, and that students’ results depended partly on how their classmates performed on the test. This is one reason schools invested so heavily in preparing whole cohorts for the QCS Test.

Approach 2: scaling by performance in other subjects (VCE, HSC, SACE and today’s QCE)

Victoria, NSW and South Australia, and now Queensland, scale each subject by looking at how the students who take it perform across their other subjects. This is often called inter-subject scaling. If the students in a subject tend to do well in all their subjects, their results in that subject are scaled to reflect that strong cohort. If they tend to do less well elsewhere, their results are scaled to a lower position.

The advantage is that no separate test is needed: the students’ own results provide the comparison. The method also responds automatically to changes in who takes each subject from year to year. The trade-off is that it is a statistical process that many students find counter-intuitive, which is why myths about “subjects that scale well” are so persistent. Our guide to reading a scaling report shows how to interpret the published results for your state.

Approach 3: average marks scaling (Western Australia)

Western Australia’s TISC uses a method it calls average marks scaling, applied to the combined school and exam marks for each ATAR course. In broad terms it serves the same purpose as inter-subject scaling elsewhere: putting courses on a common scale based on the overall performance of the students taking them, so that the best four scaled scores can be added fairly. The TEE era used scaling for the same reason before the TES and TER were calculated.

Approach 4: a scaling test for the territory (ACT)

The ACT, with its school-based system, uses the ACT Scaling Test (AST) to help place results from different colleges and courses on a common scale. In that respect the ACT’s approach has something in common with Queensland’s old use of the QCS Test, even though the ACT has always used a finer-grained rank rather than bands. The ACT’s results are then aligned with NSW through UAC so that ACT and NSW ATARs are comparable.

What all four approaches have in common

Different as they are, all four approaches answer the same question: how can results from different subjects, schools or courses be combined fairly? None of them rewards a subject for being difficult, and none of them guarantees that a particular subject will always scale up or down. Each year’s scaling depends on that year’s students. That is as true of today’s ATAR as it was of the OP, ENTER, UAI and TER, and it is the most useful lesson the history of these systems offers to students choosing subjects today.

When the ATAR took over

The move to a single national rank happened in stages. Most states adopted the ATAR around 2009 and 2010, replacing the ENTER, UAI and TER. Queensland held on to the OP the longest, switching only in 2020.

The reason was fairness across borders. With one national rank, a student in Perth and a student in Sydney could be compared for the same course on the same scale. The ATAR made university admissions consistent across the country.

Who decided, and when

The ATAR was the result of agreement among the tertiary admissions centres, which work together through the Australasian Conference of Tertiary Admission Centres (ACTAC). UAC’s technical report explains that the decision to adopt the new name followed a 2008 decision by ACTAC. In June 2009, the federal Education Minister announced that the state-level university entrance scores would be removed and replaced with a national Australian Tertiary Admission Rank. The ACT confirmed it would adopt the national rank in the same month.

The rollout was staged by Year 12 class rather than by calendar year:

  • NSW and the ACT issued ATARs to the Year 12 class of 2009, replacing the UAI immediately.
  • Victoria, South Australia, the Northern Territory, Western Australia and Tasmania issued ATARs to the Year 12 class of 2010, replacing the ENTER and the TERs.
  • Queensland kept the OP for another decade and issued its first ATARs to the class of 2020.

So if someone finished Year 12 in Melbourne in 2009, they received an ENTER, while a friend who finished in Sydney the same year received an ATAR. Both ranks were on the same national scale, which is why the staggered rollout caused little trouble for admissions.

What actually changed in 2009 and 2010

Because the ranks outside Queensland were already equivalent, the changes were small and mostly technical:

  • A single name. Course guides, offer notices and results statements all began to say “ATAR”.
  • A common top value. NSW and the ACT moved from a top UAI of 100.00 to a top ATAR of 99.95, matching the other states.
  • Clearer reference groups. In NSW, the ATAR was referenced to the age cohort that started high school together, rather than the Year 10 group used for the UAI.
  • Reporting details stayed local. Each state kept its own rules for things like how low ranks are displayed and how results are released, so small differences remain today. Queensland, for example, reports ATARs at or below 30 as “30.00 or less”.

What did not change

The more important list is what stayed the same. The certificates did not change because of the ATAR. HSC marks, VCE study scores, SACE grades and WACE exam results continued as before. Scaling methods did not change because of the ATAR. Each admissions centre kept scaling subjects in its established way. The order of students did not change either. UAC made this point directly when the UAI became the ATAR: the scaling process, the rank order of students and the applicants selected for each course all remained the same.

In other words, the 2009 and 2010 change was a relabelling of an existing national scale, not a new way of judging students. That is why most students and teachers outside Queensland barely noticed it.

Course cut-offs moved with the ranks

One practical effect was on course cut-offs. If the ATAR for a given position came out slightly higher than the old UAI, then the cut-off for a course that had been filled at that position would also come out slightly higher. UAC noted at the time that any small change in an ATAR compared with a UAI would see an equivalent change in the course cut-off.

This is worth remembering if a parent compares the cut-off for a course “in their day” with the cut-off today. Some of the difference may simply be the change of scale, not a change in how competitive the course has become. Competitiveness also changes over time with demand and the number of places offered, so cut-offs are a moving target in any case. For current figures, see our NSW ATAR cut-offs, VIC ATAR cut-offs and QLD ATAR cut-offs pages.

The ATAR and the selection rank

The ATAR era also brought clearer language around adjustments. Your ATAR is your rank from Year 12 results. When you apply for a course, admissions centres may add adjustment factors, previously called “bonus points”, for things like subject results, living in a regional area or experiencing educational disadvantage. The total is your selection rank for that course. Adjustments do not change your ATAR; they change your selection rank.

The old systems had similar mechanisms, but they were often described in different ways in each state. Today the terms ATAR, adjustment factors and selection rank are used consistently across Australia. Our guides to selection rank vs ATAR, how the selection rank is calculated and ATAR bonus points explain the current rules in detail.

Queensland’s move from OP to ATAR, step by step

Queensland’s switch was the biggest change in this whole story, because it was not just a new name. It involved a new senior certificate, a new assessment model, a new scaling method and a new organisation responsible for the rank. Here is how it unfolded.

Step 1: the ACER review (2013–2014)

In June 2013, the Queensland Government commissioned the Australian Council for Educational Research (ACER) to conduct a major independent review of the state’s senior assessment and tertiary entrance processes. The review was led by Dr Gabrielle Matters and Professor Geoff Masters. Its report, Redesigning the secondary–tertiary interface, was submitted in 2014.

The review examined how senior subjects were assessed, how comparable results were across schools, how the OP was calculated, and how well the system supported tertiary selection. It concluded that although the existing arrangements had served Queensland well, senior assessment and tertiary entrance needed attention. Among its central recommendations were the introduction of common external assessment alongside school-based assessment and a move away from the OP towards a finer-grained rank.

Step 2: the government decision (2015)

In 2015, the Queensland Government announced that the OP would be replaced by the ATAR, bringing Queensland into line with the other states. The new model would combine school-based assessment with common external assessment. The original plan named a slightly earlier start; the final timeline made students entering Year 11 in 2019 the first to study under the new system, and the Year 12 class of 2019 the last to receive OPs.

Step 3: a new senior certificate

The new QCE system reorganised senior subjects into categories. The two that matter most for the ATAR are:

  • General subjects, the academic subjects that lead most directly to tertiary study, such as English, Mathematical Methods, Chemistry and Modern History. Each General subject has three internal assessments set and marked by schools and one external assessment set and marked by the QCAA.
  • Applied subjects, which emphasise practical skills and pathways into work and further training. Applied subjects are assessed internally with four assessments, and in Essential English and Essential Mathematics one of those is a common internal assessment set by the QCAA.

The weighting of the external assessment depends on the subject. According to the QCAA, in most General subjects the external assessment contributes 25 per cent of the final result, while in mathematics and science subjects it contributes 50 per cent. External assessments are held at the same time across Queensland in Term 4 of Year 12.

Step 4: the QCS Test is retired

The QCS Test had been the backbone of OP scaling for nearly three decades. Under the new system it was no longer needed, because external assessment and inter-subject scaling took over its role of making results comparable. The final QCS Test was held on 3 and 4 September 2019, for the last OP cohort.

Step 5: QTAC takes over the rank

Under the OP, the curriculum authority calculated the rank. Under the ATAR, QTAC does. QTAC receives final subject results from the QCAA, scales them using inter-subject scaling, calculates each eligible student’s Tertiary Entrance Aggregate and converts it to an ATAR. The ATAR is a number from 0.00 to 99.95 in steps of 0.05, and in Queensland ATARs at or below 30 are reported as “30.00 or less”.

QTAC’s explanation of the allocation step shows how fine the new rank is. If the Queensland Year 12 population is roughly 60,000, then about 30 students fall into each ATAR band of 0.05. The top 30 receive 99.95, the next 30 receive 99.90, and so on. Compare that with the OP, where each of 25 bands held hundreds or thousands of students.

Step 6: new eligibility rules

To receive a Queensland ATAR, a student must meet three conditions:

  1. English. Satisfactorily complete an English subject, meaning a C or better at Units 3 and 4 in English, English as an Additional Language, Literature, English and Literature Extension or Essential English. English only counts in the calculation if it is one of the student’s best results, but passing it is compulsory for eligibility.
  2. A valid subject combination. Complete five General subjects; or four General subjects plus one Applied subject; or four General subjects plus a completed VET qualification at Certificate III or above.
  3. A time limit. Accumulate the results within a five-year period.

There are also rules about combinations. For example, only General English subjects or Applied English subjects can be included, not both, and the same applies to mathematics. These rules stop students from counting two versions of the same subject.

These rules were a deliberate break from OP eligibility. The OP required 20 semester units of Authority subjects and the QCS Test. The ATAR allows a VET qualification or an Applied subject to fill one of the five places, bringing more students into the same rank. Data from QTAC’s 2020 report, as summarised by ATAR Notes, showed most students qualified through five General subjects (about 72 per cent), with about 16 per cent qualifying through four General subjects plus an Applied subject.

Step 7: the first Queensland ATARs (December 2020)

The class of 2020 had an extraordinary final year. They were the first full cohort to have started Prep, the first to begin high school in Year 7, the first to complete the new QCE, and they did all of this during the COVID-19 pandemic. The QCAA reduced the number of internal assessments for Units 3 and 4 that year, but QTAC confirmed that the ATAR calculation itself would not change.

The first Queensland ATARs were released online at 9am on Saturday 19 December 2020, at the same time as QCAA results. The Queensland Government’s statement that day congratulated more than 49,600 graduates of the class of 2020. The week before, QTAC had to correct an error during internal testing that sent incorrect messages to about 24,000 students; QTAC contacted the affected students and the official release went ahead as planned.

Step 8: the system today

Every Queensland Year 12 student who meets the eligibility rules now receives an ATAR on the same national scale as students in every other state. There is no OP, no QCS Test and no Field Position. Students who finished Year 12 in an earlier year can also qualify for a new ATAR by completing additional subjects; QTAC sets the minimum as three General subjects, or two General subjects plus one Applied subject or VET qualification.

Direction: why one counts down and the other counts up

The OP counted down because it described bands from the top: band 1 was the highest group, band 2 the next, and so on to band 25. The ATAR counts up because it is a percentile: it describes what percentage of the age group you finished ahead of, so a bigger number is better. Neither direction is more correct; they are simply answering slightly different questions.

The practical effect is that Queensland families who knew the OP well had to flip their instincts. “Low is good” became “high is good”. That sounds trivial, but it caused real confusion in the first few years, especially when parents read course requirements written as minimum ATARs and instinctively looked for a small number.

Granularity: 25 bands versus 2,000 steps

This is the most important practical difference. With only 25 bands, the OP grouped together large numbers of students who had achieved slightly different results. In 2019, for example, QTAC’s conversion table showed that the lowest ATAR awarded within OP 1 was 98.85. An OP 1 student could therefore have been anywhere from about 98.85 to 99.95 on the ATAR scale. An OP 10 student could have been anywhere from about 77.85 to just under 80.70.

The ATAR splits the scale into steps of 0.05. That means there are 2,000 possible values from 0.00 to 99.95, although Queensland groups everything at or below 30 together. For selection, this greatly reduces ties. Under the OP, universities often had to use Field Positions or other information to choose between applicants who shared the same OP. Under the ATAR, ties at exactly the same rank are much less common.

QCAA’s own description of the change sums it up: the ATAR is a finer-grained rank order of students than the OP. Whether finer is always better is a fair question. The designers of the OP had argued that very fine distinctions could suggest more precision than the underlying results justified. But for course selection, especially for competitive courses, the finer scale is much easier to work with.

The comparison group

QCAA describes the OP as a student’s position compared with all other OP-eligible students in Queensland. QTAC describes the ATAR as a student’s position against the whole Queensland Year 12 age cohort, expressed on the national scale. Those are different groups. The OP group only included students who had chosen and qualified for the OP pathway, which by the end was about half of Year 12. The ATAR is placed against the whole age group, which includes students who did not seek a rank at all.

This difference is one reason why an OP cannot simply be converted into an ATAR by formula. The two numbers place the same student against different crowds. It is also why the share of students in a given OP band and the share of students above a given ATAR are not the same thing.

Which results count

The OP was based on a student’s best 20 semester units of Authority subjects. For most students, that meant five subjects across four semesters of Year 11 and Year 12. Results from Year 11 therefore contributed to the OP, through the semester structure.

The Queensland ATAR is based on a student’s best five eligible results at Units 3 and 4, which are studied in Year 12. Units 1 and 2, usually studied in Year 11, can count towards the QCE but are not used in the ATAR calculation. Students can include one Applied subject or one completed VET qualification at Certificate III or above in place of a fifth General subject. This makes the ATAR more flexible about pathways but more focused on Year 12 performance.

Assessment and external exams

Under the OP, all assessment was school-based. Teachers designed and marked assessment against statewide syllabuses, and a strong moderation system checked that standards were applied consistently. There were no subject-specific external exams; the QCS Test was the only common statewide assessment, and it was not tied to any one subject.

Under the ATAR system, General subjects combine three internal assessments with one external assessment set and marked by the QCAA. The external assessment is worth 25 per cent in most subjects and 50 per cent in mathematics and science. This gives every General subject a common statewide element, which supports comparability without relying on a separate test like the QCS.

Scaling: QCS group data versus inter-subject scaling

The OP’s scaling relied on the QCS Test. Subject groups and schools were scaled according to how their students performed on the QCS, which acted as a common yardstick. Your QCS result mattered mostly through its effect on your groups.

The ATAR uses inter-subject scaling. In simple terms, QTAC compares how students in each subject perform across all their other subjects, and uses that information to place every subject on a common scale. There is no separate test. The principle is similar to the scaling used in Victoria, NSW, South Australia and Western Australia, though each state’s method has its own technical details. Our ATAR scaling guide and scaling myths guide go through the logic step by step.

Who does the calculating

Under the OP, the curriculum authority, the QCAA and its predecessors, calculated the rank as part of its certification role. Under the ATAR, QTAC calculates the rank, in the same way that admissions centres do in other states. This separation reflects a common national pattern: the curriculum authority certifies subject results, and the admissions centre turns those results into a rank for tertiary selection.

Field Positions disappeared

Field Positions had no direct equivalent in the new system. They were useful as tie-breakers in a 25-band world, but the ATAR’s fine scale makes them less necessary. Courses that care about particular skills now usually express that through subject prerequisites or adjustment factors for relevant subjects rather than through a separate field rank.

So is the ATAR harder than the OP?

Not harder, but different. The ATAR does not require students to know more or perform better than the OP did. It simply measures and reports their position more finely, against a different reference group, using a different assessment and scaling model. A student who would have been a strong OP candidate is likely to be a strong ATAR candidate too.

What has changed is the experience. External assessment adds exam pressure at the end of Year 12 that OP students did not face in the same way. On the other hand, there is no QCS Test, and students have clearer rules about which subjects count. Different students will find different parts of each system easier or harder.

Was an OP 1 the same as an ATAR of 99.95?

No. An OP 1 was a band, and in 2019 it covered students whose ATARs started at about 98.85 and ran up to 99.95. Only the very top students in that band would have had an ATAR of 99.95. UAC’s 2018 comparison table makes the same point from the other direction: ATARs of 99.95, 99.50 and 99.00 all corresponded to an OP 1 in 2018. If someone tells you their OP 1 was “the same as a 99.95”, the more accurate statement is that it placed them in roughly the top one to one and a half per cent of the comparison group.

Bands or fine ranks: the argument about precision

The OP and the ATAR represent two different answers to a genuine question in educational measurement: how precisely should we report a student’s position? It is worth understanding both sides, because the question has not gone away.

The case for bands

Every assessment contains some uncertainty. A student who sits an exam on a slightly better day, writes on a slightly more familiar topic or is marked by a slightly more generous marker could finish a little higher than they otherwise would. If a system reports results in very fine steps, some of the differences between students will reflect that kind of noise rather than real differences in achievement.

This was a central argument when Queensland replaced the TE Score with the OP. Reviews at the time accepted that the TE Score had many points of discrimination, but not all of them represented meaningful differences between students. Grouping students into 25 bands was a way of being honest about that uncertainty. Two students in the same band were treated as having performed at essentially the same level.

The case for fine ranks

The problem with bands is what happens at the edges and in selection. With 25 bands, each band held many students. If a course could only take half of the OP 5 applicants, it needed another way to decide between them, which is where Field Positions and other tie-breakers came in. Students just below a band boundary also had very different outcomes from students just above it, even though the gap between them could be tiny.

Fine ranks reduce these problems. With steps of 0.05, ties are much rarer, and the move from one step to the next reflects only a small difference in position. QTAC’s description of Queensland’s allocation, with roughly 30 students in each 0.05 step if the Year 12 population is about 60,000, shows how fine the modern scale is.

Where the debate lands today

The national system has settled on fine ranks, because they work better for selection across thousands of courses and hundreds of thousands of applicants. But the concern behind bands remains valid. A difference of 0.05 or 0.10 in an ATAR is not a meaningful difference in ability. It is a difference in position that happens to matter for selection when a course cut-off falls at that point.

For students, the practical lesson is to treat your ATAR as a precise position for selection purposes, but not as a precise measure of what you know or can do. A student with an ATAR of 84.95 and one with 85.10 have performed at essentially the same level, even if a cut-off of 85.00 separates them for one round of offers. That is also why adjustment factors, alternative pathways and later offer rounds exist: to soften the effect of very small differences at the margin.

What the shift did not do

It is tempting to read the table as “the ATAR made everyone’s result better”. It did not. A student who would have been offered a place with a UAI of 85 would still have been offered it with the equivalent ATAR of 86.25, because the cut-off moved too.

TER vs ATAR in SA, NT, WA and Tasmania

For the TER states, the TER-to-ATAR change in 2010 sits between the Victorian and NSW experiences. The scale did not need to change: South Australian, Northern Territory, Western Australian and Tasmanian TERs already ran to 99.95. The ranks were already treated as equivalent to the ENTER and UAI. So a TER from the late 2000s can be read as roughly equivalent to an ATAR of the same value.

What did change in some states was the certificate underneath. Western Australia’s move from the TEE to the new WACE examinations happened at the same time as the move to the ATAR, so a WA student in 2010 experienced a bigger change than a South Australian student did. South Australia kept its 90-point university aggregate, which still sits behind the SACE ATAR today, although the SACE itself was later reformed. Tasmania continued with the TCE, with the ATAR managed through the University of Tasmania.

If you need to compare an older TER from before the national equivalence arrangements of the late 1990s, be more cautious. Those TERs were calculated against different reference groups in different states, and there is no single conversion table that covers them all. In that case, ask the relevant admissions centre or university how it treats the result.

State by state: the old rank and today’s ATAR

The sections above explain each old system in depth. This section pulls the threads together state by state, so you can see at a glance what a student in each place received in the past and what they receive now.

New South Wales

Then: NSW ranked school leavers with a Tertiary Entrance Score and then a Tertiary Entrance Rank from the late 1960s until 1997. The UAI took over for the HSC class of 1998 and ran until the class of 2008. The UAI was calculated from the best ten units of scaled HSC marks, including two units of English, and its top value was 100.

Now: NSW students have received an ATAR since the class of 2009. The calculation still uses the best 2 units of English plus the best 8 other units of scaled HSC marks, but the top value is 99.95 and the reference group is the age cohort that started high school together. UAC calculates the ATAR for NSW and ACT students. Read more in how the ATAR is calculated in NSW or use the HSC ATAR calculator.

Australian Capital Territory

Then: The ACT used its own Tertiary Entrance Rank, calculated through the BSSS, before adopting the UAI alongside NSW.

Now: ACT students have received an ATAR since the class of 2009, at the same time as NSW. The ATAR is calculated from the best three major courses plus 60 per cent of the next major or minor, with scaling informed by the ACT Scaling Test. See the ACT calculation guide and the ACT ATAR calculator.

Victoria

Then: Victoria used a statewide TER in the 1990s, then the ENTER for the classes of 1998 to 2009. The ENTER was calculated by VTAC from the primary four scaled study scores plus 10 per cent of the fifth and sixth, on a scale from 0.00 to 99.95.

Now: Victorian students have received an ATAR since the class of 2010. The formula and scale are unchanged from the ENTER. See the Victorian calculation guide and the VIC ATAR calculator.

Queensland

Then: Queensland used the TE Score from 1974 to 1991 and the OP from 1992 to 2019. The OP was calculated by the curriculum authority from teacher-assigned SAIs scaled using QCS Test group results, and reported in 25 bands with OP 1 at the top. OP-eligible students also received Field Positions.

Now: Queensland students have received an ATAR since the class of 2020. QTAC calculates it from the best five eligible subject results, after inter-subject scaling, with a C or better in English required. General subjects include external assessment, and there is no QCS Test. See the Queensland calculation guide and the QLD ATAR calculator.

South Australia

Then: South Australia used a TER until the class of 2009. SATAC scaled subject scores reported by SSABSA and combined them into a university aggregate out of 90, which was converted to a TER on the 0.00–99.95 scale.

Now: South Australian students have received an ATAR since the class of 2010. The university aggregate out of 90 survives, calculated from 90 credits of Stage 2 SACE study, and SATAC converts it to an ATAR. See the SA calculation guide and the SA ATAR calculator.

Northern Territory

Then: The Northern Territory shared South Australia’s approach, with students completing the SACE-based NTCET and receiving a TER through SATAC.

Now: NT students have received an ATAR since the class of 2010, still through SATAC and the same SACE-based calculation. See the NT calculation guide and the NT ATAR calculator.

Western Australia

Then: Western Australia ran the Tertiary Entrance Examination from 1986 to 2009. Scaled TEE results produced a Tertiary Entrance Score, which TISC converted into a TER on the 0–99.95 scale.

Now: WA students have received an ATAR since the class of 2010, when the new WACE examinations replaced the TEE. TISC scales each ATAR course, adds the best four scaled scores and any bonuses into a TEA, and converts it to an ATAR. See the WA calculation guide and the WA ATAR calculator.

Tasmania

Then: Tasmania used a TER calculated for students who completed the TCE and met the tertiary entrance requirements, based on an aggregate of their best pre-tertiary results.

Now: Tasmanian students have received an ATAR since the class of 2010, with the University of Tasmania central to the process because it is the state’s only university. See the Tasmanian calculation guide and the TAS ATAR calculator.

What the state-by-state view shows

Lay the eight jurisdictions side by side and a clear pattern appears. In seven of them, the move to the ATAR was a change of name on top of an existing, equivalent rank, sometimes with a small technical adjustment such as NSW’s change of top value and reference group. The underlying certificate, scaling and aggregate stayed recognisable. Only Queensland rebuilt its system from the ground up. That is why the OP dominates most conversations about old ranks: it is the one old system that really was different.

It also explains why today’s ATAR calculations still differ from state to state. The ATAR did not replace each state’s machinery; it sat on top of it. When you compare your ATAR with a friend’s in another state, you are comparing two numbers designed to mean the same thing, produced by two different engines. If you want to see those engines side by side, our comparisons of the QCE vs other states and the HSC vs other states are the next step.

Can you convert an old OP or ENTER to an ATAR?

Roughly, but not exactly. Because the OP used broad bands and the ATAR uses a fine rank, there is no perfect one-to-one match. Official conversion tables existed to map an OP band to an ATAR range, so an OP of 1 mapped to a high ATAR, and so on.

The ENTER, UAI and TER are easier, because they were already fine ranks close to the ATAR’s scale. But any conversion is a guide, not an exact figure. If you need to convert an old score for an application, check with the relevant admissions centre for the official mapping.

Why an exact conversion is impossible

It is natural to want a formula that turns an OP into an ATAR, or an old score into a modern one. The admissions centres are clear that no such formula exists. QTAC’s guidance states that there is no possible direct translation of OP to ATAR, because the two are derived from different sets of information and by different statistical methods. There are three separate reasons for this.

  1. Bands versus fine ranks. An OP is a band containing many students with different underlying results. You cannot recover a precise position from a band, only a range.
  2. Different comparison groups. The OP compared students with other OP-eligible students; the ATAR compares students with the age cohort. The same performance places a student differently against different groups.
  3. Different methods. The OP used QCS-based scaling within and between schools; the ATAR uses inter-subject scaling and external assessment. Even with the same students, the two methods would not produce exactly matching orders.

The same logic applies, more mildly, to the ENTER, UAI and TER. They were on the national scale, so conversions are closer, but small differences in reference groups and years still mean any conversion is a guide rather than an exact figure.

How Queensland universities treat an OP today

If you have an OP and want to apply to study, you do not need to convert it yourself. QCAA states that OPs do not expire and can still be used for university entrance, and QTAC confirms that Year 12 results, whether an OP, ATAR or selection rank, do not expire after you leave school. When you apply through QTAC, your OP is converted to a selection rank using QTAC’s tables for the year you received it.

QUT, for example, publishes indicative tables showing how OPs from different years translate into selection ranks for its courses. In its tables for 2015 to 2019, an OP 1 converts to a selection rank of 99.00 and an OP 2 to 97.00, while an OP 3 converts to 96.00 for 2017 to 2019 and 95.00 for 2015 and 2016. These are selection ranks for admission, not ATARs, but they show how universities place older results on today’s scale.

How to get an official ATAR for an old OP

If you need an actual ATAR figure, for example to apply interstate or for your own records, the QCAA can issue an ATAR statement. Its rules are straightforward:

  • You must have been issued an OP between 1997 and 2019. The QCAA can only calculate ATARs for those years.
  • You apply using the QCAA’s ATAR application form, with a certified copy of identification.
  • The QCAA calculates ATARs for OP holders free of charge.
  • If you are simply applying for tertiary study, you may not need the statement at all. The QCAA provides tertiary entrance data to QTAC, and admissions centres can obtain your ATAR directly.
  • If you did not receive an OP, the QCAA cannot give you an ATAR. In that case, you would apply to QTAC for a selection rank based on your other results.

Converting a UAI, ENTER or TER

For the other old ranks, the job is easier:

  • UAI (NSW and ACT, 1998–2008). Use UAC’s official UAI to ATAR conversion table. The selection of values in the UAI vs ATAR section above gives you the general pattern; UAC’s complete table gives every value.
  • ENTER (Victoria, 1998–2009). The ENTER was already on the 0.00–99.95 national scale with the same formula as the Victorian ATAR. For most purposes, an ENTER can be read as roughly equal to an ATAR of the same value.
  • TER (SA, NT, WA, Tas, late 1990s to 2009). These TERs were on the national scale and can generally be read as roughly equal to an ATAR of the same value.
  • Older ranks. For an NSW TER before 1998, a Victorian TER from the early 1990s, a Queensland TE Score or any other older measure, ask the relevant admissions centre or university. QUT, for instance, converts a Queensland TE Score to a selection rank by dividing it by 10.

A step-by-step method for any old score

  1. Identify the system and the year. Write down exactly what the score was called, which state issued it and which Year 12 class it belonged to.
  2. Check the direction. Confirm whether high or low was good. Only the OP and FPs counted down.
  3. Find the official table for that year. Admissions centres and universities publish conversion tables; use the one closest to the year in question.
  4. Think in ranges, not single numbers. For an OP, describe the band as a range of ATARs. For a UAI, ENTER or TER, allow for a point or two of difference.
  5. Ask the admissions centre if it matters. If the conversion affects an application, the admissions centre’s own assessment is the only one that counts.
  6. Remember other pathways. For adults returning to study, recent study, work experience or an admissions test may matter more than an old Year 12 rank.

Worked examples: translating old scores

Here are some realistic situations, with a careful translation for each. The people are invented, but the conversions use the official tables and rules described above.

Example 1: a parent with an OP 2 from 2001

Sarah’s mother finished Year 12 in Brisbane in 2001 with an OP 2. Sarah wants to know how that compares with her own predicted ATAR of 96.

Using the 2019 table as a rough guide, OP 2 covered ATARs from about 97.30 to 98.80. The 2001 relationship would not have been identical, but it is unlikely to have been very different. So Sarah’s mother was probably in roughly the 97 to 99 range in ATAR terms, a little above Sarah’s prediction. Because her OP falls within 1997 to 2019, she could request an official ATAR statement from the QCAA, free of charge, if she wanted the exact figure.

Example 2: a parent with a UAI of 92.50 from 2004

James’s father received a UAI of 92.50 in Sydney in 2004. UAC’s table shows a UAI of 92.00 matching an ATAR of 92.65, and a UAI of 93.00 matching 93.55. A UAI of 92.50 sits halfway, so its ATAR equivalent is about 93.10. UAC’s full table would give the precise value. In practice, James can tell his father that his UAI was equivalent to an ATAR of a little over 93.

Example 3: a relative with an ENTER of 85.40 from 2006

Priya’s aunt received an ENTER of 85.40 in Melbourne in 2006. Because the ENTER used the same scale and formula as today’s Victorian ATAR, the simplest accurate translation is that she finished in a position similar to an ATAR of about 85.40 today. The bigger difference between Priya and her aunt is likely to be which VCE studies were offered and how competitive particular courses are now.

Example 4: a WA relative who remembers a TES

Tom’s uncle says he “got about 300” in the WA TEE in 2003. That number is almost certainly his Tertiary Entrance Score (TES), which was out of 510 at the time, not his TER. The TES was converted to a TER, which was the actual rank. If Tom’s uncle can find his TER, it can be read as roughly equivalent to an ATAR of the same value. If he only remembers the TES, the safest translation is in words rather than numbers, because the TES-to-TER relationship changed from year to year.

Example 5: a grandparent with a Queensland TE Score

Mia’s grandfather finished school in Toowoomba in 1983 with a TE Score of 905. The TE Score was not on the national scale, and there is no official ATAR for it. For admissions purposes, QUT’s method would treat a TE Score of 905 as a selection rank of 90.50. That is a useful indication that he was a strong student, but it is a selection rank for one university’s purposes, not a formal ATAR.

Example 6: a 2019 graduate with an OP 8 returning to study

Jake finished Year 12 in Cairns in 2019 with an OP 8 and has been working since. In 2027 he wants to apply for a nursing degree. His OP does not expire. When he applies through QTAC, it will be converted to a selection rank. As a guide, a 2019 OP 8 corresponded to ATARs from about 83.50 to 86.20. He could also add adjustment factors if he is eligible, and any study or qualifications completed since school may give him an additional selection rank. Queensland institutions generally consider applicants on their best available rank, so it is worth listing everything when he applies. Our nursing ATAR guide shows typical entry requirements to compare against.

Example 7: siblings from different systems

Ella received an ATAR of 91.20 in Queensland in 2025. Her older brother received an OP 6 in 2016 and insists he did better. Using the 2019 table as a guide, OP 6 ran from about 88.75 to 91.10. So Ella’s result sits at or slightly above the top of where her brother’s band would land in ATAR terms. Allowing for year-to-year variation, the fair conclusion is that they performed at a very similar level. The family debate can end in a draw.

The golden rule for conversions
Translate old scores into ranges and words, not exact numbers. “Roughly the top five per cent” or “low 90s in today’s terms” is more accurate than a single converted figure, and it is all most conversations need.

Questions to ask when someone quotes an old score

Most confusion about old ranks comes from comparing two numbers without first checking what each one measures. Before you compare an old score with a modern ATAR, run through these questions. They take a minute and prevent most mistakes.

  1. Which state issued it? A TER from Perth and a TER from Sydney were different measures, even with the same name.
  2. Which Year 12 class was it? The year tells you which system applied. An NSW student from 1997 had a TER; one from 1998 had a UAI; one from 2009 had an ATAR.
  3. Which direction was good? If it was an OP or an FP, lower was better. For everything else, higher was better.
  4. Was it a rank or a score? In Western Australia, the TES was a score that fed into the TER. In Queensland before 1992, the TE Score was the main measure. Make sure you are comparing the rank, not an intermediate score.
  5. Was it before the national equivalence arrangements? Ranks from the late 1990s onwards outside Queensland were designed to be equivalent. Earlier ranks were referenced to different groups and need more caution.
  6. Was it a personal result or a course cut-off? “I needed a 90” often means the course cut-off, not the person’s own rank. The two are easy to mix up in memory.
  7. Did it include adjustments? A remembered number might be a selection rank that included bonus points, not the underlying rank.
  8. Is there an official table? For an OP from 1997 to 2019, the QCAA can issue an ATAR. For a UAI, UAC publishes a conversion table. For others, the admissions centre can advise.

Once you have the answers, use the tables on this page to translate into a range of ATARs, and describe the result in words as well as numbers. “Roughly the top seven per cent of the age group” is a translation everyone can understand, whichever system they grew up with.

What this means for you today

If you are in Year 12 now, you will receive an ATAR, wherever you study. The old systems are gone. The only time they come up is when an older relative compares their result to yours, and now you can translate.

The reassuring part is that the core idea has never changed. Every one of these systems was a rank, not a mark. Understand that, and you understand all of them. Try our ATAR calculators to see where your results land on today’s scale.

If you are in Year 12 now

Wherever you are in Australia, if you meet your state’s eligibility rules you will receive an ATAR. You do not need to know anything about the OP, ENTER, UAI or TER to get the most out of your final year. What you do need is a clear understanding of how your own state turns your results into an ATAR, because the rules are different in each place:

  • NSW: your best 2 units of English plus your best 8 other units of scaled HSC marks. See how the ATAR is calculated in NSW.
  • ACT: your best three majors plus 60 per cent of the next major or minor, scaled with the AST. See the ACT guide.
  • Victoria: English plus your best three scaled study scores, plus 10 per cent of the fifth and sixth. See the Victorian guide.
  • Queensland: your best five eligible results, with a C or better in English required. See the Queensland guide.
  • Western Australia: your best four scaled scores plus any bonuses, combined into a TEA. See the WA guide.
  • South Australia: a university aggregate out of 90 from 90 credits of Stage 2 study. See the SA guide.
  • Northern Territory: the same SACE-based approach through SATAC. See the NT guide.
  • Tasmania: an aggregate of your best TCE results, converted through the University of Tasmania’s process. See the Tasmanian guide.

Knowing your state’s rule helps with the decisions that actually matter in Year 12: where to put your effort, how to treat your weakest subject, and how much a fifth or sixth subject really adds. When results day approaches, our ATAR release dates guide lists when each state publishes results.

If you are in Year 10 or Year 11

For younger students, the history on this page is mainly useful as reassurance. The core logic of tertiary entrance has been stable for decades: perform as well as you can in subjects you can sustain for two years, and let scaling do its job of making subjects comparable. No system, old or new, has rewarded students for choosing subjects purely to chase scaling.

If you are planning subjects, focus on three questions. Which subjects are you good at and interested in? Which subjects are prerequisites for courses you might want? And which combination can you sustain under pressure in Year 12? Our guides to predicting your ATAR in Year 11 and common ATAR misconceptions are good places to start.

If you are applying interstate

One of the biggest practical benefits of the ATAR is that you can use it directly in another state. A Western Australian student applying to a course in Melbourne, or a Queensland student applying in Sydney, uses the same ATAR they received at home. Some admissions centres convert interstate ATARs into their own selection ranks for processing, but the student does not need to do anything special. Before 2020, Queensland students applying interstate needed a separate ATAR calculated from their OP; today their ATAR travels with them like everyone else’s.

What still differs between states is the detail of course requirements. Subject prerequisites, adjustment factors and application deadlines are set by each admissions centre and university, so always check the requirements in the state you are applying to. Interstate subject equivalences are usually published by the admissions centres to help with prerequisites.

If you are an adult with an old score

Your old Year 12 result does not expire, whether it was an OP, ENTER, UAI, TER or ATAR. Admissions centres can still use it to calculate a selection rank. But for many adults it will not be the strongest part of an application. Universities typically consider a range of evidence for applicants who are not recent school leavers, including:

  • completed tertiary study, even a few units,
  • vocational qualifications such as a Certificate IV or Diploma,
  • admissions tests such as the Special Tertiary Admissions Test (STAT), and
  • work and life experience, in some cases.

Institutions often assess you on your best available rank. So if your old OP or UAI is modest, it may be worth looking at whether more recent study would give you a stronger selection rank. Each admissions centre publishes its rules for non-school-leaver applicants.

If you studied the IB or overseas

International Baccalaureate Diploma results are converted to an ATAR-equivalent rank by the admissions centres, using the combined rank table in most states and the South Australian table in South Australia, the Northern Territory and Tasmania. New Zealand NCEA results are also converted to an ATAR-equivalent rank for New Zealand applicants. These conversions follow the same principle as the old interstate equivalence arrangements: translate a different qualification onto the shared national scale.

If you are a teacher or careers adviser

For staff who remember the older systems, the main update is in language. Students today will hear “ATAR”, “selection rank” and “adjustment factors” from every admissions centre. Older terms such as OP, ENTER, UAI, TER and “bonus points” still appear in family conversations and older resources, so a quick translation can save a lot of confusion. Sharing this page with families is one way to do that.

Why one national rank is fairer

The old state systems were not wrong, but they created an awkward gap. A student in Queensland with an OP of 3 and a student in Victoria with an ENTER of 94 might be equally strong, yet their scores looked nothing alike. Universities had to translate between systems, and students moving interstate faced confusion.

A single national rank fixed this. With the ATAR, the same number means the same thing everywhere, so a course can compare every applicant on one scale. That fairness across borders is the main reason every state eventually adopted it.

One number, one meaning, everywhere

The heart of the fairness argument is consistency. With a single national rank, an ATAR of 85 in Hobart is designed to mean the same thing as an ATAR of 85 in Darwin, Perth or Sydney. CourseSeeker, the national course information site, describes it this way: ATARs are calculated in each state to reflect a student’s rank against other students in that state, and all ATARs are treated as equivalent between the states, which makes applying interstate easier.

That consistency matters most for students who move. A family that relocates from Brisbane to Adelaide in the middle of high school no longer has to worry that the student will finish with a rank universities in other states struggle to read. A student in a border town who is choosing between universities on either side of a state line can compare cut-offs directly.

Fairness for universities, too

Universities also benefit. When applicants from every state arrive with the same kind of rank, a university can put them all in one order of merit without maintaining separate conversion rules for each system. Before 2020, that work was still needed for Queensland applicants, with OPs converted to ATARs or selection ranks and interstate ATARs converted for Queensland courses. The single rank simplifies a process that affects hundreds of thousands of applications every year.

Fairness for students in different subjects

The national rank is only fair if the results feeding into it are comparable. That is the job of scaling in each state. Scaling adjusts results so that a strong performance in a subject with a highly competitive cohort is recognised, and a strong performance in a subject with a less competitive cohort is not over-rewarded. The aim is that no student is disadvantaged simply by choosing one subject over another. Our scaling explainer shows how this works in practice.

What a national rank does not do

It is worth being honest about the limits. A national rank does not mean a national calculation. Each state still has its own certificate, its own assessment model and its own scaling method. Two students with the same ATAR in different states have reached it by different routes. The equivalence is designed and checked, but it is not a guarantee that every detail lines up perfectly.

A national rank also does not mean the ATAR is the only route to university. Many students are admitted through other pathways, including vocational qualifications, prior higher education study, alternative entry schemes and portfolio or interview-based selection. The ATAR is the main tool for recent school leavers, but it is one tool among several. Our guide to what counts as a good ATAR puts the number in that wider context.

Why Queensland’s switch completed the picture

Until 2020, the national picture had one gap. Queensland’s OP sat outside the common scale, so Queensland students and universities needed an extra translation step. Queensland’s move to the ATAR closed that gap. Since the class of 2020, every eligible Year 12 student in Australia receives the same kind of rank, reported on the same scale, under the same name. That is the end point the states had been working towards since the equivalence agreements of the 1990s.

The ongoing debate about a single rank

The ATAR has its critics as well as its supporters, and the arguments echo those made about the old systems. Critics argue that reducing years of schooling to one number puts too much pressure on students, encourages subject choices driven by perceived scaling rather than interest, and says little about qualities such as creativity, persistence or practical skill. Some point out that a growing share of students reach university through other pathways, which raises questions about how central the ATAR should be.

Supporters reply that a single, transparent, nationally consistent rank is the fairest practical way to allocate limited places among large numbers of school leavers. It treats students from different schools and subjects on the same basis, it is published and explained by independent admissions centres, and it is backed by scaling methods that are reviewed every year. They also note that universities can, and do, add other criteria where a course needs them.

Both sides agree on one thing that the history on this page makes clear: whatever it is called, the rank is a selection tool. It was never designed to measure a student’s worth, and it works best when students and families treat it that way.

What parents and older siblings should know

If someone in your family finished school before about 2010, they almost certainly received an OP, ENTER, UAI or TER, not an ATAR. Their advice about “what score you need” may be framed in the old system, so it helps to translate before comparing.

The good news is that the thinking still transfers. All these systems ranked students for university, rewarded consistent strong results, and used scaling to compare subjects. A parent who understood their ENTER already understands most of how your ATAR works.

Translate the number before you translate the advice

When a parent says “you need a 4 for that course” or “I got a 92 and that was enough”, the first job is to work out which system they are talking about. An OP 4 and an ATAR of 92 can describe a similar position, but they look completely different. A UAI of 92 and an ATAR of 92 look identical but differ slightly. Before you weigh the advice itself, translate the number using the tables on this page.

The second job is to separate the number from the year. A course cut-off from 2005 tells you how competitive that course was in 2005, against that year’s applicants, using that year’s scale. It does not tell you what the course needs today. Demand for courses rises and falls, universities change the number of places they offer, and in NSW the scale itself shifted when the UAI became the ATAR. For any current decision, look up current figures rather than relying on memory.

Entry pathways have widened

Parents who went through the system in the 1990s or 2000s may remember a world where the rank was almost everything. Today, students have more ways to strengthen an application or reach the same course:

  • Adjustment factors for certain subjects, for living or studying in regional areas, for elite athletes and for students who have experienced educational disadvantage. See our bonus points guide and maximum bonus points guide.
  • Early offer schemes run by many universities, which may consider school recommendations or Year 11 results before final ATARs are released.
  • Alternative entry through diplomas, enabling programs, vocational qualifications or prior tertiary study.
  • Course-specific selection such as interviews, portfolios, auditions or admissions tests, especially in medicine, the creative arts and some health courses.

This does not make the ATAR unimportant. For most school leavers it remains the central part of an application. But a modest result is far less final than it might have seemed a generation ago.

What has not changed

Much of the wisdom parents gained under the old systems still holds:

  • Consistency matters. Every system rewarded strong results across a set of subjects, not a single brilliant one.
  • English matters. Across these systems, most states have required English in some form, either inside the rank calculation itself or as a separate condition for eligibility or university entry.
  • Scaling is not a trick. Choosing subjects only because they “scale well” was risky under the old systems and is still risky now. A strong result in a subject you enjoy usually beats a weak result in a subject you chose for its scaling.
  • The rank measures position, not worth. A rank is a selection tool for one purpose. It has never been a measure of a person’s intelligence or future.

How to talk about scores at home

Year 12 conversations can become stressful, and old scores sometimes make them worse. A few habits help:

  1. Compare positions, not numbers. “You were in the top 10 per cent, and I’m aiming for something similar” is a fairer comparison than “you got 92 and I’m predicted 89”.
  2. Talk about the course, not the score. Focus on what the student wants to study, what it requires now, and the backup pathways.
  3. Use current tools. Our ATAR calculators, ATAR predictor and ATAR target calculator use modern scaling data rather than memories of older systems.
  4. Keep perspective. Many parents can say from experience that their own rank mattered intensely for a few weeks and very little after the first year of university.

Results day, then and now

The way students received their ranks has changed almost as much as the ranks themselves. Older relatives’ memories of results day can sound very different from today’s experience, so here is a brief history.

Letters, newspapers and phone lines

For much of the twentieth century, students received their results by post, and many states published exam results and university offers in newspapers. VTAC’s history notes the moment course offers were first published in The Age, and families across the country remember checking printed lists for names. Applications were made on paper forms, often with a thick printed guide listing every course and its codes. VTAC’s guide, for example, was a printed publication for decades before the final printed edition appeared in the mid-2010s.

Telephone services came next. VTAC describes its automated phone line, the VTAC Infoline, as the first step away from paper-based applications, and it later ran a telephone application service before moving fully online.

SMS and the early internet

The ENTER era coincided with the early internet. VTAC launched its website in 1998, the same year the ENTER began, and online applications started that year. VTAC later sent ENTER and VCE results by SMS for the first time, and in 2004 phased out paper applications entirely. Across the country, the late 1990s and 2000s saw admissions move steadily from paper and phone to the web.

Online release and nationally consistent timing

Today, ATARs are released online. When Queensland released its first ATARs in December 2020, QTAC made them available online only, with an option to print a results notice, at the same time as the QCAA released subject results. Students needed to create an ATAR account in advance to see their result.

Release timing has also become more coordinated. Education ministers agreed that ATAR results would be released in a nationally consistent window of dates from 2020, although each state still sets its own exact day. Under the OP, Queensland students received their OPs in mid-December and OP-ineligible students received QTAC ranks later in December; today all eligible Queensland students receive their ATAR on the same release day. Our state release date pages, such as QLD ATAR release date, NSW ATAR release date and VIC ATAR release date, list the current schedule.

What has stayed the same

For all the changes in technology, results day itself has a familiar shape. Results arrive in December, offer rounds follow shortly afterwards, and students have a window to change their preferences once they know their rank. Advice from older relatives about staying calm, reviewing preferences carefully and remembering that there are many pathways is as relevant now as it was in the era of newspaper lists.

Myths about the old systems

Because the old systems are half-remembered by many people, myths about them circulate freely. Here are the most common ones, and what the official sources actually show.

Myth 1: “A low OP was a bad result”

The opposite is true. OP 1 was the best possible result and OP 25 the lowest. A student with an OP 2 was in roughly the top few per cent of OP-eligible students. This is the single most common confusion when Queenslanders compare notes with friends from other states.

Myth 2: “The ATAR is a percentage mark”

It is not. The ATAR is a percentile rank. An ATAR of 75 does not mean you got 75 per cent of anything right. It means you finished ahead of about 75 per cent of your age group. The same was true of the ENTER, UAI and TER. Our misconceptions guide covers this in more depth.

Myth 3: “An OP 1 equals an ATAR of 99.95”

An OP 1 was a band, not a single position. In 2019, it covered ATARs from about 98.85 to 99.95. Only the very top students in the band were at 99.95. Most OP 1 students would have had ATARs in the high 98s or low-to-mid 99s.

Myth 4: “The ATAR made it harder to get into university”

Outside Queensland, the ATAR was a relabelling of an existing equivalent rank. UAC confirmed that the change from the UAI did not alter the order of students or who was selected for each course. In Queensland, the change was more substantial, but it changed how students were assessed and ranked, not how many places were available. Course competitiveness depends on demand and places, not on the name of the rank.

Myth 5: “Old scores expire after a few years”

They do not. The QCAA states that OPs do not expire, and QTAC confirms that Year 12 results do not expire once you leave school. The same applies to UAIs, ENTERs, TERs and ATARs. What changes over time is how much weight a university gives an old result compared with more recent study.

Myth 6: “You can convert any old score to an exact ATAR”

Only some can be converted officially, and none by a simple formula. QTAC explicitly says there is no direct translation from OP to ATAR. The QCAA can calculate an ATAR for students who received an OP from 1997 to 2019, and UAC publishes a UAI-to-ATAR table. For older ranks, you will usually need the admissions centre to assess the result.

Myth 7: “The ENTER, UAI and TER were completely different systems”

They were calculated differently, but from the late 1990s they were designed to be equivalent. Government data standards treated them as local names for a single common index agreed by education ministers. That is why the change to the ATAR in 2009 and 2010 was so smooth outside Queensland.

Myth 8: “Your QCS score decided your OP”

Not directly. Your individual QCS grade appeared on your Senior Statement, but the main role of the QCS Test was to provide group data for scaling. Your own OP depended on your SAIs in each subject, scaled using the QCS results of your subject groups and your school. Your QCS performance helped or hindered your groups more than it set your individual result.

Myth 9: “A UAI of 100 meant perfect marks”

No NSW student ever received a perfect aggregate of 500. The top UAI went to the students with the highest aggregates in their year, whatever their exact total. A UAI of 100 described a position at the very top of the rank order, not a perfect score.

Myth 10: “The ATAR compares you with your Year 12 classmates”

The ATAR places you against your age group, not just against the students in Year 12. That is why the average ATAR among students who actually receive one is well above 50: students who leave school early or do not seek a rank are part of the comparison group but do not appear among ATAR recipients.

Myth 11: “Hard subjects automatically scale up”

Scaling reflects the strength of the students who take a subject, measured by their performance across their other subjects. It does not measure how difficult the subject feels. This was true of the old systems and remains true today. See our ATAR scaling myths page for examples.

Myth 12: “Queensland students were disadvantaged interstate under the OP”

Queensland students could still apply interstate. The QCAA calculated a rank on the national scale for OP-eligible students, later reported as an ATAR, so they could be considered for interstate courses. The process was less convenient than having one rank for everyone, but it was designed to treat Queensland applicants fairly.

Frequently confused pairs

Some terms from old and new systems are so similar, or so often used together, that people mix them up. Here are the pairs that cause the most trouble.

OP and QCE

The OP was a rank; the QCE is a certificate. Under the old system, Queensland students could receive a QCE (or earlier a Senior Certificate) whether or not they were OP-eligible. Under the new system, students can receive a QCE whether or not they are ATAR-eligible. The certificate shows that a student completed senior schooling to the required standard; the rank is only for tertiary selection.

QCS and QCE

The QCS Test was the Queensland Core Skills Test used for OP scaling, last held in 2019. The QCE is the Queensland Certificate of Education. They share two letters and a state, but they are completely different things, and only the QCE still exists.

TES and TER

In Western Australia, the TES was the Tertiary Entrance Score, calculated from scaled TEE results; the TER was the Tertiary Entrance Rank derived from it. Elsewhere, TES can also mean Tertiary Entrance Statement, the document that records a student’s aggregate and rank in South Australia and that recorded Queensland students’ OPs and FPs. Always check which TES is meant.

ATAR and selection rank

Your ATAR is your rank based on Year 12 results. Your selection rank is your ATAR plus any adjustment factors for a particular course. Two students with the same ATAR can have different selection ranks for the same course, and one student can have different selection ranks for different courses.

Study score and ENTER (or ATAR)

In Victoria, a study score is a result out of 50 in one VCE study. The ENTER, and now the ATAR, is a rank built from scaled study scores across several studies. A study score of 40 and an ATAR of 40 mean entirely different things: the first is a strong result in one study, the second is a position in the lower half of the age group.

HSC mark, band and UAI (or ATAR)

In NSW, an HSC mark is a result out of 100 in one course, and a band (Band 1 to Band 6) describes the standard that mark represents. The UAI, and now the ATAR, is a rank built from scaled marks across ten units. A Band 6 in a course says nothing on its own about a student’s ATAR, although a set of Band 6 results usually goes with a high rank.

OP band and HSC band

Both systems used the word “band”, which adds to the confusion. An OP band was one of 25 groups in Queensland’s overall rank, with band 1 at the top. An HSC band is one of six standards in a single NSW course, with Band 6 at the top. They measure completely different things and run in opposite directions.

Raw score and scaled score

Every system distinguished between a student’s raw result in a subject and the scaled result used in the rank. In the OP system, the raw input was the SAI; in Victoria, the study score; in NSW, the HSC mark; in South Australia, the subject score; in Western Australia, the combined school and exam mark. The scaled version is the one that counts towards the rank, which is why two students with the same raw result in different subjects can contribute different amounts to their ranks.

Glossary: old and new terms explained

Tertiary entrance has more acronyms than almost any other part of education. This glossary covers the terms you are most likely to meet when comparing old and new systems.

Ranks and scores

ATAR (Australian Tertiary Admission Rank). The national rank used for university entry since 2009 in NSW and the ACT, 2010 in most other states and 2020 in Queensland. A percentile rank from 0.00 to 99.95 in steps of 0.05.

ENTER (Equivalent National Tertiary Entrance Rank). Victoria’s rank from 1998 to 2009, calculated by VTAC from scaled VCE study scores. Same scale and formula as today’s Victorian ATAR.

UAI (Universities Admission Index). The rank used in NSW from 1998 to 2008 and in the ACT, calculated from scaled HSC marks. Its top value was 100.

TER (Tertiary Entrance Rank). A rank name used at different times in South Australia, the Northern Territory, Western Australia, Tasmania, the ACT, Victoria and NSW. The TERs in use in the 2000s were on the national 0–99.95 scale.

OP (Overall Position). Queensland’s rank from 1992 to 2019. Twenty-five bands, with OP 1 the highest and OP 25 the lowest. Compared students with other OP-eligible Queensland students.

FP (Field Position). Queensland’s supplementary ranks in up to five fields of study, from FP 1 (highest) to FP 10 (lowest). Used mainly as tie-breakers. Abolished with the OP.

TE Score (Tertiary Entrance Score, Queensland). Queensland’s fine-grained entrance score from 1974 to 1991, replaced by the OP.

TES (Tertiary Entrance Score). In Western Australia, the score calculated from scaled TEE results and converted into a TER. Early NSW systems also used a Tertiary Entrance Score.

ITI (Interstate Transfer Index). A name for the common national index, used notably in Queensland and still used in describing ATAR-equivalent ranks for New Zealand applicants.

CUE Index (Common University Entrance Index). An approach endorsed by education ministers in the 1990s for working out interstate equivalences.

Selection rank. Your ATAR (or other rank) plus any adjustment factors for a particular course. It is the number actually used to decide offers.

Notional ATAR / combined rank. An ATAR-equivalent rank calculated for students with other qualifications, such as the IB Diploma.

Calculation terms

Scaling. The statistical process that adjusts results from different subjects onto a common scale before they are combined, based on the strength of each subject’s cohort.

Aggregate. The total of a student’s scaled results under their state’s rules, which is then converted to a rank. Examples include the NSW aggregate out of 500, the Victorian aggregate, the SA university aggregate out of 90 and the TEA in WA and Queensland.

TEA (Tertiary Entrance Aggregate). The aggregate used in Western Australia and Queensland today, from which the ATAR is calculated.

Primary four. In Victoria, the English study plus the three next-best scaled study scores, all counted in full.

Increments. In Victoria, 10 per cent of the fifth and sixth best scaled study scores.

Study score. A VCE result out of 50 for each study, ranking students within that study.

SAI (Subject Achievement Indicator). Under the OP, a teacher-assigned placement from 200 to 400 showing each OP-eligible student’s position within a school subject group.

OAI (Overall Achievement Indicator). Under the OP, the average of a student’s best 20 semester units of scaled SAIs, before being scaled between schools and banded.

LOA (Level of Achievement). Queensland’s five-level subject result under the old system, from Very High Achievement to Very Low Achievement.

QCS Test (Queensland Core Skills Test). The statewide test that OP-eligible students sat in Year 12, used mainly for group scaling. Last held in September 2019.

AST (ACT Scaling Test). The common test used in scaling ACT results.

Percentile rank. A number showing the percentage of a reference group that a student finished ahead of.

Age cohort. The full group of young people of the relevant age, including those who do not finish Year 12 or seek a rank. The ATAR’s reference group.

Certificates and exams

HSC. The Higher School Certificate in NSW, issued by NESA.

VCE. The Victorian Certificate of Education, issued by the VCAA.

QCE. The Queensland Certificate of Education, issued by the QCAA. The new QCE system began with students entering Year 11 in 2019.

SACE and NTCET. The South Australian Certificate of Education and its Northern Territory equivalent.

WACE. The Western Australian Certificate of Education.

TEE. Western Australia’s Tertiary Entrance Examination, used from 1986 to 2009.

TCE. The Tasmanian Certificate of Education.

General, Applied and Authority subjects. Queensland subject categories. Authority subjects counted for the OP; General subjects, with one Applied subject or VET option, count for the ATAR.

Organisations

UAC. Universities Admissions Centre, for NSW and the ACT.

VTAC. Victorian Tertiary Admissions Centre.

QTAC. Queensland Tertiary Admissions Centre, which calculates the Queensland ATAR.

SATAC. South Australian Tertiary Admissions Centre, for South Australia and the Northern Territory.

TISC. Tertiary Institutions Service Centre, for Western Australia.

QCAA (formerly QSA). Queensland Curriculum and Assessment Authority, which calculated OPs and now certifies QCE results.

BSSS. ACT Board of Senior Secondary Studies.

SSABSA. Senior Secondary Assessment Board of South Australia, the predecessor to the SACE Board.

ACTAC. Australasian Conference of Tertiary Admission Centres, the body through which admissions centres coordinate national matters such as the ATAR.

MCEETYA. The former Ministerial Council on Education, Employment, Training and Youth Affairs, which worked on interstate rank equivalence in the 1990s.

ACER. Australian Council for Educational Research, which conducted the 2013–14 review of Queensland senior assessment and tertiary entrance.

The whole story in two minutes

This guide covers a lot of ground, so here is the full story in brief, for anyone who wants the essentials without the detail.

Every system was a rank. The OP, ENTER, UAI, TER and ATAR all put Year 12 students in order for university selection, after scaling their results so different subjects could be compared. None of them was a percentage mark.

Each state built its own. Because schools are run by the states, each state developed its own certificate, its own admissions centre and its own rank. NSW had the TER and then the UAI; Victoria had the TER and then the ENTER; South Australia, the Northern Territory, Western Australia and Tasmania had TERs; the ACT had a TER and then the UAI; and Queensland had the TE Score and then the OP.

Most of them became equivalent in the late 1990s. Education ministers worked on interstate equivalence in the mid-1990s, and from the late 1990s the ranks outside Queensland were designed to mean the same thing. They were local names for one common index.

The ATAR gave that index one name. After agreement among the admissions centres, the national ATAR was announced in June 2009. NSW and the ACT issued it to the class of 2009, and Victoria, South Australia, the Northern Territory, Western Australia and Tasmania to the class of 2010. In most places, little changed apart from the name; in NSW, the top value became 99.95 and the reference group changed slightly.

Queensland was different. The OP, used from 1992 to 2019, reported 25 bands with OP 1 at the top, compared students only with other OP-eligible students, and relied on the QCS Test for scaling. After the ACER review and years of preparation, Queensland issued its first ATARs, calculated by QTAC, to the class of 2020 on 19 December 2020.

Old scores can be translated, not converted exactly. An OP becomes a range of ATARs, using tables such as QTAC’s 2019 guide. A UAI converts closely using UAC’s table. An ENTER or a TER from the 2000s is roughly equal to an ATAR of the same value. Old results never expire, and the QCAA can still issue an ATAR for an OP from 1997 to 2019.

The lesson for today. Whatever the rank is called, it is a selection tool built on scaled results. Choose subjects you can do well in, understand how your own state calculates the ATAR, and keep the number in perspective. That advice would have served a student with an OP, ENTER, UAI or TER just as well as it serves a student with an ATAR today.

Common questions

What's the difference between OP and ATAR?

The OP was Queensland’s old rank, running 1 to 25 with 1 as the best. The ATAR runs 0.00 to 99.95 with higher as better. Both are ranks for university entry; Queensland switched from the OP to the ATAR in 2020.

When did Queensland switch from OP to ATAR?

Queensland switched from the OP to the ATAR in 2020, making it the last state to adopt the national ATAR. Every Queensland student now receives an ATAR calculated by QTAC.

What was the ENTER score?

The ENTER (Equivalent National Tertiary Entrance Rank) was Victoria’s old rank, running 0 to 99.95 with higher as better. It worked much like today’s ATAR and was calculated from VCE study scores.

What was the UAI?

The UAI (Universities Admission Index) was the old rank used in NSW and the ACT, calculated from scaled HSC marks. It was replaced by the national ATAR around 2009.

How do I convert an old OP to an ATAR?

You can only estimate. Official tables mapped an OP band to an ATAR range, since the OP used broad bands and the ATAR uses a fine rank. For an exact figure, check the relevant admissions centre’s official conversion.

Is the ATAR harder than the OP?

Not harder, just finer. The OP had 25 broad bands, while the ATAR ranks students in steps of 0.05. Both rank you against your age group; the ATAR simply gives a more precise position.

What does OP stand for?

OP stands for Overall Position. It was Queensland’s tertiary entrance rank from 1992 to 2019. Each OP-eligible student was placed in one of 25 bands, from OP 1 (the highest) to OP 25 (the lowest), based on their position in a statewide order of merit compared with other OP-eligible students. The OP was calculated by the Queensland curriculum authority, most recently the QCAA, and was replaced by the ATAR from 2020.

What does ATAR stand for?

ATAR stands for Australian Tertiary Admission Rank. It is the national rank used to select school leavers for university courses. It is a percentile rank from 0.00 to 99.95, in steps of 0.05, showing a student’s position relative to their age group. NSW and the ACT introduced it in 2009, most other states in 2010, and Queensland in 2020.

What does ENTER stand for, and why was it called “equivalent national”?

ENTER stands for Equivalent National Tertiary Entrance Rank. It was Victoria’s rank from 1998 to 2009. The name reflected the fact that, by the late 1990s, ranks in most states were designed to be equivalent to each other, so an ENTER of 90 meant roughly the same as a UAI or TER of 90 in another state. Victoria replaced the ENTER with the ATAR from the class of 2010.

What does UAI stand for?

UAI stands for Universities Admission Index. It was the rank used in NSW for the HSC classes of 1998 to 2008, and it was also used in the ACT. It was calculated from the best ten units of scaled HSC marks, including two units of English, and its top value was 100. NSW and the ACT replaced it with the ATAR in 2009.

What does TER stand for?

TER stands for Tertiary Entrance Rank. It was used at different times in South Australia, the Northern Territory, Western Australia, Tasmania, the ACT, Victoria and NSW. The TERs used in South Australia, the Northern Territory, Western Australia and Tasmania in the 2000s were on the national 0 to 99.95 scale and were replaced by the ATAR from the class of 2010.

When was the OP introduced?

The OP was introduced in Queensland in 1992, replacing the Tertiary Entrance (TE) Score that had been used since 1974. In its first year, 28,202 Queensland students received an OP. The change followed reviews of tertiary entrance in the late 1980s and 1990, including the Viviani Report.

When did the last students receive an OP?

The Year 12 class of 2019 was the last to receive OPs. QCAA data shows 17,638 students received an OP that year. The final Queensland Core Skills (QCS) Test, which supported OP scaling, was held on 3 and 4 September 2019. Students who entered Year 11 in 2019 studied under the new QCE system and received ATARs instead.

When were the first Queensland ATARs released?

QTAC released the first Queensland ATARs online at 9am on Saturday 19 December 2020, at the same time as QCAA subject results. They went to the Year 12 class of 2020, the first cohort to complete the new QCE system. The Queensland Government congratulated more than 49,600 graduates that day.

When did NSW switch from the UAI to the ATAR?

NSW and the ACT switched from the UAI to the ATAR for the Year 12 class of 2009. The federal Education Minister announced the national ATAR in June 2009, and the first NSW and ACT ATARs were issued that December. UAC said the scaling process, the order of students and the applicants selected for each course did not change.

When did Victoria switch from the ENTER to the ATAR?

Victoria replaced the ENTER with the ATAR in 2010, with the Year 12 class of 2010 receiving the first Victorian ATARs. The change was mainly a renaming, because the ENTER already used the same 0.00 to 99.95 scale and the same aggregate formula as today’s Victorian ATAR.

When did South Australia, the NT, WA and Tasmania switch to the ATAR?

South Australia, the Northern Territory, Western Australia and Tasmania all replaced their Tertiary Entrance Ranks with the ATAR for the Year 12 class of 2010. In Western Australia, the change coincided with the move from the Tertiary Entrance Examination to the new WACE examinations.

Why did Queensland keep the OP longer than other states?

The OP was tied to Queensland’s whole senior system, including school-based assessment and the QCS Test, rather than being a simple label on top of a common scale. Replacing it meant redesigning senior assessment, adding external assessment, creating a new scaling method and moving the rank calculation to QTAC. That took an independent review in 2013 and 2014, a government decision in 2015 and several years of preparation.

Was a low OP good or bad?

A low OP was good. OP 1 was the best possible result and OP 25 the lowest, because the OP described which of 25 bands a student landed in, counting down from the top. This is the opposite of the ATAR, ENTER, UAI and TER, where higher numbers are better.

What ATAR is an OP 1 equal to?

An OP 1 does not equal a single ATAR, because it was a band. QTAC’s table for OPs awarded in 2019 shows the lowest ATAR within OP 1 was 98.85, so OP 1 students had ATARs from about 98.85 to 99.95. UAC’s 2018 table shows ATARs of 99.00, 99.50 and 99.95 all matching an OP 1. The exact relationship varied slightly from year to year.

What ATAR is an OP 5 roughly equal to?

As a rough guide, QTAC’s 2019 table shows the lowest ATAR within OP 5 was 91.15, and the lowest within OP 4 was 93.40. So an OP 5 in 2019 corresponded to ATARs from about 91.15 to 93.35. Other years differed slightly, so treat this as a range rather than an exact conversion.

What ATAR is an OP 10 roughly equal to?

Using QTAC’s 2019 table, the lowest ATAR within OP 10 was 77.85 and the lowest within OP 9 was 80.70. That means an OP 10 in 2019 corresponded to ATARs from about 77.85 to 80.65. UAC’s 2018 table also matches an ATAR of 80.00 with an OP 10.

What was the QCS Test?

The Queensland Core Skills (QCS) Test was a statewide test sat by OP-eligible Queensland students in Year 12. It was an achievement test based on common elements of the senior curriculum, not an intelligence test. Students received an individual grade from A to E, but the test’s main purpose was to provide group results used to scale school subject results for the OP. It was last held in September 2019.

What were Field Positions?

Field Positions (FPs) were supplementary ranks given to OP-eligible Queensland students in up to five fields: extended written expression, short written communication, basic numeracy, complex mathematical problem solving, and practical performance. Each FP ran from 1 (highest) to 10 (lowest). Universities mainly used them to separate applicants who had the same OP. They ended with the OP in 2019.

Who could get an OP?

To be OP-eligible, a Queensland student generally needed to complete at least 20 semester units of Authority subjects across Years 11 and 12, study at least three of those subjects for all four semesters, and sit the QCS Test. Students who did not meet these rules could receive a QTAC selection rank instead. By the late 2010s, only about half of Queensland Year 12 students were OP-eligible.

Does an OP expire?

No. The QCAA states that OPs do not expire and can still be used for university entrance. QTAC also confirms that Year 12 results, including OPs, ATARs and selection ranks, do not expire once you leave school. When you apply, your OP is converted to a selection rank for that application.

How can I get an official ATAR for my OP?

If you received an OP between 1997 and 2019, you can apply to the QCAA for an ATAR statement using its application form and a certified copy of identification. The QCAA calculates these ATARs free of charge. If you are simply applying for tertiary study, the admissions centre can usually obtain your ATAR directly, so you may not need the statement.

Can I get an ATAR if I finished Year 12 in Queensland before 1997?

The QCAA can only calculate ATARs for students who received an OP from 1997 to 2019. If you finished earlier, you will not be able to get an official ATAR from the QCAA. Instead, contact QTAC or the university you are applying to, which can assess your results and give you a selection rank. Some universities publish their own methods, such as QUT’s conversion of TE Scores.

How does a UAI convert to an ATAR?

UAC publishes an official UAI to ATAR conversion table. A UAI of 100 equals an ATAR of 99.95, a UAI of 90 equals 90.80, a UAI of 80 equals 81.65 and a UAI of 60 equals 63.40. Apart from the very top, the ATAR is slightly higher than the old UAI, and the gap grows further down the scale. UAC’s complete table gives every value.

Why was the top UAI 100 but the top ATAR 99.95?

A percentile rank of 100 would mean finishing ahead of everyone in the group, which is impossible, so most states already used 99.95 as the top value. The NSW Technical Committee on Scaling recommended in 2006 that the top UAI become 99.95, and the change happened when NSW adopted the ATAR in 2009, bringing NSW into line with the rest of Australia.

Is an ENTER the same as an ATAR?

Almost. The ENTER and the Victorian ATAR use the same certificate, the same study scores, the same VTAC scaling approach, the same aggregate formula and the same 0.00 to 99.95 scale. The main difference is the name. For most purposes, an ENTER can be read as roughly equal to an ATAR of the same value.

Is a TER the same as an ATAR?

A TER from South Australia, the Northern Territory, Western Australia or Tasmania in the 2000s was on the national scale and can generally be read as roughly equal to an ATAR of the same value. Older TERs, such as NSW TERs before 1998, were referenced to different groups, so they need more caution. Ask the relevant admissions centre if precision matters.

What was the TES in Western Australia?

In Western Australia, the Tertiary Entrance Score (TES) was a score calculated from a student’s scaled Tertiary Entrance Examination results. For many years it was out of 510; in 2009 it was reported out of 400. TISC converted the TES into the Tertiary Entrance Rank (TER), which was the actual rank. The TES and TER should not be confused.

What was Queensland’s TE Score?

The Tertiary Entrance (TE) Score was Queensland’s entrance score from 1974 to 1991, before the OP. It was a fine-grained number, often remembered as a three-digit figure. QUT explains that a TE Score can be converted to a selection rank by dividing it by 10, so a TE Score of 885 becomes a selection rank of 88.50.

What was the Interstate Transfer Index?

The Interstate Transfer Index (ITI) was a name for the common national index agreed by education ministers to compare Year 12 students across states. It was used particularly in Queensland, where the OP was not on the common scale, so Queensland students had a separate national-scale rank for interstate applications. The term is still used in describing ATAR-equivalent ranks for New Zealand applicants.

Were the old ranks equivalent between states?

From the late 1990s, yes, outside Queensland. Education ministers worked in the mid-1990s on interstate rank equivalence, and the ENTER, UAI and TERs were then treated as local names for one common index. Queensland’s OP was not on that scale, but conversion tables were published and Queensland students received a national-scale rank for interstate applications.

Is the ATAR calculated the same way in every state?

No. Each state calculates its ATAR from its own senior certificate, using its own scaling method and aggregate rules. For example, NSW uses ten units of HSC courses, Victoria uses a primary four plus increments, and South Australia uses a university aggregate out of 90. The ranks are then aligned so that all ATARs are treated as equivalent between states.

Did the change to the ATAR affect who got into university?

Outside Queensland, no. UAC confirmed that when the UAI became the ATAR, the scaling process, the order of students and the applicants selected for each course stayed the same, because course cut-offs moved by the same amount as ranks. Queensland’s change was more substantial in how students were assessed and ranked, but entry still depended on demand and places available.

Can I still use an old UAI, ENTER or TER to apply to university?

Yes. Year 12 results do not expire, and admissions centres can still use an old UAI, ENTER or TER to calculate a selection rank. However, if you have done any study since school, such as a vocational qualification or university units, that may give you a stronger selection rank. Admissions centres generally consider your best available rank.

How do universities treat old Year 12 results for mature-age applicants?

Universities can still use old Year 12 results, but for applicants who left school some years ago they often consider other evidence as well, such as vocational qualifications, previous tertiary study, admissions tests like the Special Tertiary Admissions Test, and in some cases work experience. Each admissions centre publishes rules for applicants who are not recent school leavers.

Is the ATAR a percentage?

No. The ATAR is a percentile rank, not a percentage mark. An ATAR of 80 means you finished ahead of about 80 per cent of your age group; it does not mean you scored 80 per cent in your subjects. The same was true of the ENTER, UAI and TER. Only the OP worked differently, using bands from 1 to 25.

Why do ATAR values look higher than old UAI values?

When NSW moved from the UAI to the ATAR, the reference group changed from the Year 10 cohort to the age cohort that started high school together, and the top value changed from 100 to 99.95. As a result, the same position usually produced a slightly higher ATAR than UAI, with the gap growing lower down the scale. Course cut-offs rose by the same amount, so no one gained an advantage.

What replaced the QCS Test?

Nothing replaced it directly. Under the new QCE system, every General subject includes an external assessment set and marked by the QCAA, worth 25 per cent of the result in most subjects and 50 per cent in mathematics and science. QTAC then uses inter-subject scaling to make subjects comparable. Together, these do the comparability job that the QCS Test used to support.

What subjects count for a Queensland ATAR?

A Queensland ATAR is based on a student’s best five eligible results: five General subjects, or four General subjects plus one Applied subject, or four General subjects plus a completed VET qualification at Certificate III or above. Students must also achieve a C or better in an English subject, and the results must be accumulated within five years.

How many students received an OP 1 each year?

It varied, because OP bands had no fixed quota. QCAA figures show 2.0 per cent of OP-eligible students received an OP 1 in 1992. In 2018, about 2.83 per cent received an OP 1, and in 2019, the final year, about 2.85 per cent did. The Queensland Government reported 535 OP 1s in its announcement for the class of 2019.

Do Field Positions have an ATAR equivalent?

No. Field Positions ended with the OP and have no equivalent in the ATAR system. The ATAR’s much finer scale reduces the need for tie-breakers. Courses that care about particular skills now usually express that through subject prerequisites or adjustment factors for relevant subjects.

How do I compare my ATAR with my parent’s OP?

Find the year your parent received their OP and use an official OP to ATAR guide, such as QTAC’s 2019 table, to turn the OP into a range of ATARs. For example, an OP 3 in 2019 corresponded to ATARs from about 95.50 to 97.25. Compare your ATAR with that range rather than a single number, and remember the relationship varied slightly each year.

Where can I find official conversion tables for old scores?

For OPs, QTAC published a table of the lowest ATAR in each OP band for 2019, UAC published a 2018 ATAR to OP comparison table, and the QCAA can issue an ATAR statement for OPs from 1997 to 2019. For UAIs, UAC publishes a UAI to ATAR conversion table. For other old ranks, contact the admissions centre in the state where the rank was issued.

Which rank did I get if I finished Year 12 in 2009?

It depends on your state. Students who finished in NSW or the ACT in 2009 received an ATAR, because those jurisdictions adopted it first. Students who finished in Victoria received an ENTER, and students in South Australia, the Northern Territory, Western Australia and Tasmania received a TER, because those states switched with the class of 2010. Queensland students received an OP.

What was the difference between an OP and an OP-ineligible rank?

An OP was calculated by the Queensland curriculum authority for students who met the OP eligibility rules, including sitting the QCS Test. Students who did not meet those rules could still apply for tertiary study, and QTAC calculated a separate rank for them from their Senior Statement results. Both were used for Queensland admissions, but they were different measures. Under the ATAR system, more pathways lead to the same rank.

Did Queensland students sit external exams under the OP system?

Not in individual subjects. Under the OP system, subject assessment was school-based and moderated statewide. The only common statewide assessment for OP-eligible students was the QCS Test, which was not tied to a particular subject. External assessment in every General subject arrived with the new QCE system from 2019, contributing 25 per cent of the result in most subjects and 50 per cent in mathematics and science.

Why did Queensland abolish the TE Score?

By the late 1980s the TE Score, used since 1974, had lost public confidence. Reviews found that universities were using it as the main selection tool although it was intended as one criterion among several, that it was being misused by employers, and that its very fine distinctions did not always reflect meaningful differences between students. The Queensland Government abolished it with effect from 1992, when the OP replaced it.

Is an NT ATAR the same as a South Australian ATAR?

They are calculated the same way. Northern Territory students complete the SACE-based NTCET, and SATAC calculates their university aggregate and ATAR using the same approach as for South Australian students. Before 2010, both jurisdictions used the TER through SATAC. Like all ATARs, the result is treated as equivalent to ATARs from every other state.
