What a QLD ATAR cutoff really means

A Queensland cutoff is the rank of the last student admitted, not a standard fixed in advance. QTAC publishes it after each round.

The range across Queensland courses

Queensland cutoffs range widely by course. The same degree at two Brisbane universities can differ, and neither number measures quality.

The highest cutoffs go to courses like medicine, law, and some commerce and engineering degrees at the University of Queensland, QUT and Griffith. Direct-entry medicine usually needs 95 and above, plus an admissions test.

Adjustment and bonus points

The ATAR you need is not always the raw cutoff. Many Queensland universities add adjustment factors, sometimes called bonus points, to your rank. These reward things like where you live, subjects you took, or difficulty during Year 12.

They lift your selection rank above your ATAR for that course. A student with an ATAR of 86 and five points is considered at 91. See how bonus points work.

Cutoff versus selection rank

Universities publish cutoffs as either an ATAR or a selection rank. A selection rank is your ATAR plus any adjustment points. So a course with a selection-rank cutoff of 90 might be reachable with an ATAR below 90, once your points are added.

Always check whether a cutoff is an ATAR or a selection rank. It changes how far your own ATAR needs to reach. Our selection rank calculator can help.

Adjustment schemes in depth

Adjustment factors, often called bonus points, are one of the most misunderstood parts of the cutoff picture, and understanding them can change which courses are realistic for you. They are points added to your ATAR after it is calculated, to form a selection rank, which is the figure a course actually compares against its cutoff. Because they are applied on top of the ATAR, a student whose ATAR falls a little short of a published cutoff may still reach it once their adjustments are counted.

Educational access and equity

The main equity route is an educational access scheme, which recognises circumstances that may have affected your study, such as financial hardship, illness, disability, or disruption during Year 12. Eligible students receive adjustment factors that raise their selection rank for participating courses. Some adjustments are applied automatically from information the admissions centre holds; others need an application with supporting evidence by a deadline, so it pays to check early whether you qualify.

Subject-based adjustments

Many Queensland universities award adjustments for studying particular subjects relevant to a course, such as a specified mathematics, science or language subject. These subject adjustments are set by each university and vary between them, so the lift available for a given subject at one university may differ at another. They reward students who took the subjects that prepare them for a course, and they can make a target course more reachable if you chose your subjects well.

Regional and other schemes

Some schemes recognise living or attending school in a regional or remote area, and universities run their own additional schemes from time to time. Because adjustments are capped, they improve your selection rank within a limit rather than without bound, so they bridge a modest gap rather than a large one. The practical approach is to find out which schemes you qualify for, add their likely points to your ATAR estimate, and compare that selection rank, not your bare ATAR, with each course’s cutoff.

Count your adjustments into your target. When you compare yourself with a course, use your selection rank, your ATAR plus the adjustments you expect, not your ATAR alone. It can move a borderline course into reach.

Cutoffs by field of study

Although cutoffs are set course by course, it helps to see the broad bands that different fields tend to fall into, because they frame what rank a direction of study usually asks for. Treat these as rough territory, not promises: every course sets its own cutoff, and the figures move each year with demand and places. The point is to know roughly where your target field sits so you can plan a realistic set of preferences.

Health and medical fields

The health fields span the widest range. Direct and provisional-entry medicine, dentistry and some allied-health degrees sit among the highest cutoffs in the state, commonly needing a rank in the high 90s together with an admissions test such as the UCAT and often an interview. Nursing, by contrast, is far more accessible, with many programs sitting in the 60s to 70s, and paramedicine, physiotherapy and pharmacy falling somewhere in between depending on the university and the year.

Law, business and commerce

Law at the leading Queensland universities is one of the higher-cutoff fields, often in the high 90s for single degrees and higher again for some double degrees. Business and commerce cover a very broad band, from competitive commerce degrees at research-intensive universities down to accessible business programs elsewhere, so a student interested in business has realistic options across a wide range of ranks.

Engineering, science and technology

Engineering and the sciences also range widely. Some specialised or highly sought engineering programs carry high cutoffs, while general science and many engineering degrees sit in the middle of the scale. Information technology and computing have grown in demand, which can push some programs up, but accessible options remain. Mathematics and science prerequisites matter as much as the rank in these fields.

Arts, education and the humanities

Arts, humanities, education and creative fields tend to sit in the more accessible bands, commonly in the 60s to 80s, though specific high-demand programs and some double degrees run higher. Education is a field where the state has at times set particular entry expectations, so check the current requirements for teaching degrees, which can include more than the rank alone.

Fields are territory, not a map. These bands tell you roughly where a direction of study sits, but you still plan around each specific course’s current cutoff and prerequisites, not the field average.

Planning your preferences around cutoffs

Cutoffs are only useful if you turn them into a plan. The goal is a preference list that gives you the best shot at your most-wanted course while making sure you are not left without an offer if your rank lands lower than hoped. Because QTAC makes you the best offer it can from your ordered list, the order of your preferences is the single most important decision once you know roughly where your ATAR will fall.

Spreading your preferences

Build your list across your estimate band. Put your genuine first choice at the top, whatever its cutoff, because listing it first costs you nothing. Below it, add courses whose recent cutoffs sit around the middle of your band, and finish with one or two safer options, including a guaranteed-entry course where one exists, below your floor. This spread means a rise in a cutoff or a lower-than-hoped ATAR does not leave you empty-handed, while still keeping your top choice alive.

Aiming above the published figure

Because cutoffs move each year with demand, treat a published figure as a floor to clear with room to spare, not a target to hit exactly. Aiming a few points above the most recent cutoff gives you a buffer against an upward shift. For a course that has trended up in recent years, give yourself more margin; for one that has been stable or easing, a smaller buffer may be enough.

Revising when results arrive

Your pre-results list is built on an estimate; your post-results list should be built on your real ATAR. When results arrive, compare your actual selection rank with each course’s recent cutoff and adjust your preferences before the deadline, swapping an out-of-reach top choice for a realistic one, or adding a higher reach if your rank beat expectations. The preference-change window exists precisely for this, and using it well is one of the highest-value moves in the whole process.

The list is a living document. Lodge early to secure your place in the process, refine as your sense of your results sharpens, and revise decisively once your real ATAR is known, before the deadline.

Reading field bands without over-reading them

Broad field bands, the idea that health spans a wide range or that arts sits lower, are a useful first frame, but they can mislead if taken too literally. Within any field, individual courses scatter widely around the band, and a specific program at a specific university can sit far from the field’s typical figure. The band tells you roughly where to set your expectations before you look up the real numbers; it is never a substitute for the course’s own recent cutoff.

The safest way to use a field band is as a sorting tool. If a field sits well above your estimate band, you know to lean on pathways and safety options from the start. If it sits within reach, you know the field is realistic and can focus on comparing specific courses. Either way, you then move from the band to the actual cutoffs, because that is where the decision is really made.

Reading a course’s trend over recent years

A single year’s cutoff is a snapshot; a few years of ATAR-era figures tell a story. If a course’s cutoff has risen steadily, demand is building and you should give yourself a larger buffer above the latest figure. If it has been stable, a smaller buffer may be enough. If it has eased, the course may be becoming more accessible, though you still aim above the latest figure. Reading the trend, not just the latest number, is how experienced planners judge how much margin to leave.

Because Queensland’s ATAR-era history is still relatively short, trends are read with a little more caution than in longer-established states. Where only a couple of comparable years exist, treat the direction as tentative and give yourself a slightly more generous buffer. As more ATAR-era years accumulate, these trends become easier to read with confidence.

Buffer to the trend. Let the course’s recent direction set your margin: more buffer for a rising cutoff, less for a stable one, and always above the latest figure rather than at it.

Putting the cutoff picture together

A cutoff is one number in a larger picture, and the students who navigate it best are the ones who hold all the pieces at once. The cutoff tells you where demand landed last year. Your selection rank, your ATAR plus adjustments, is what that cutoff is compared against. Prerequisites and any admissions test are separate hurdles the cutoff does not capture. And your preference order decides which qualifying course you actually receive. Keep all four in view and the cutoff stops being intimidating and becomes a planning input.

The method follows naturally. Estimate your ATAR as a band, add the adjustments you expect, and you have a selection-rank band. For each target course, note its recent cutoff and type, its prerequisites and any test, and compare your band against it. Build a preference list that spreads across your band, your genuine first choice at the top, realistic options in the middle, and a safer or guaranteed-entry option below your floor. Then, when your real ATAR arrives, revise the list before the deadline. That sequence turns a wall of numbers into a clear, personal plan.

A final word of perspective

For all the attention cutoffs attract, they are an admissions threshold, not a verdict on your ability or a ceiling on your future. A cutoff reflects how many people wanted a course and how many places it had, nothing more. Clearing it opens one door; missing it points you to another. With pathways, transfer and later study all available, where you end up depends far more on what you do after you enrol than on the single number that let you in. Plan carefully, aim above the figure, know your routes, and keep the cutoff in proportion.

Four pieces, one plan. Cutoff, selection rank, prerequisites and preference order: hold all four together, and the cutoff becomes a tool you use rather than a hurdle you fear.

Common cutoff mistakes to avoid

A handful of cutoff misunderstandings cost students every year, and all are avoidable. The first is treating a cutoff as a fixed pass mark. It is not; it is the rank of the last student admitted last year, so it describes past demand, not a guaranteed threshold for this year. The second is comparing a bare ATAR with a selection-rank cutoff. If a course publishes a selection-rank cutoff, you must compare your ATAR plus adjustments, not your ATAR alone, or you will misjudge how far your rank needs to reach.

The third mistake is ignoring prerequisites. A cutoff tells you about the rank, but a course can require specific subjects regardless of rank, so a high ATAR does not rescue an application that is missing a prerequisite. The fourth is reading an old figure phrased as an OP as though it were an ATAR; the systems are different, so an OP-era number cannot be compared directly with an ATAR cutoff. The fifth is listing too few preferences, which leaves a student with no safety net if a cutoff rises.

The deadline mistake. Missing a preference-change deadline can leave you matched on a list you meant to update. Note the key dates early and revise your preferences in good time.

A cutoff checklist before you apply

A short checklist keeps the cutoff picture under control. For each target course, note its most recent cutoff and whether that figure is an ATAR or a selection rank. Check its prerequisites and any admissions test or interview. Work out your own selection rank, your estimated ATAR plus the adjustments you expect, and compare it with each course’s cutoff. Build a preference list that spreads across your estimate band, first choice first, with a safer option below your floor. Note the key dates, especially the preference-change deadline. With those in place, you are reading cutoffs as the planning tool they are, rather than as a verdict.

Treat an indicative cutoff as a guide, not a guarantee, since the actual cutoff depends on demand each year. Aim above it and plan a spread of preferences.

What should I note from a course page for my plan?

The cutoff and its type and year, the prerequisites, any test or interview, and the pathway options. Record these for each target course so your preference plan is accurate.

How do I compare a course across universities from their pages?

Line up the cutoff type and year, prerequisites, structure and outcomes for the same course at each university. The headline number alone does not tell you which suits you.

Does a course page tell me my chances?

It tells you the cutoff and requirements, which help you judge whether your selection rank is competitive, but not your exact chance, which also depends on this year’s demand.

Should I save course pages for reference?

Yes. Noting each target course’s cutoff, prerequisites and requirements in one place makes your preference planning accurate and results day calmer.

How often are course pages updated?

Universities update course information regularly, especially after offer rounds and before application periods. Check the current page rather than an older version you saved earlier.

Where is the most reliable course information?

On QTAC and each university’s own course pages, updated each year. They are more reliable than third-party lists or older figures for cutoffs, prerequisites and requirements.
